What a daily calorie estimate means

Food supplies energy, and the body uses that energy to maintain essential functions, move, digest food and carry out daily activities. A calorie calculator estimates how much energy a person may use in a day. The result can provide a starting point for understanding maintenance needs, but it is not a measurement of metabolism and it cannot predict an individual's exact response to food.

This calculator asks for sex as used by the equation, age, weight, height, units and an activity level. It estimates resting energy expenditure, also called basal metabolic rate (BMR), then applies an activity factor to estimate total daily energy expenditure (TDEE). It also displays illustrative calorie adjustments for gradual weight loss or gain. These figures are estimates, not personal medical or diet prescriptions.

BMR: energy used at rest

Basal metabolic rate is the energy the body needs to support essential functions while at rest. These include breathing, circulation, maintaining body temperature, supporting organs and keeping cells functioning. BMR does not include the full energy cost of walking, exercising, working or digesting meals.

The calculator uses the Mifflin–St Jeor equation. It estimates BMR from weight in kilograms, height in centimetres and age in years, with a different constant for the male and female versions of the equation.

Men: BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5

Women: BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age − 161

The result is expressed as an approximate number of kilocalories per day. Food labels and everyday conversation often shorten kilocalories to “calories”. The equation is a population-based estimate. It does not directly measure body composition, hormones, health conditions or individual metabolic differences.

From BMR to daily maintenance calories

Most people use more energy in a day than their resting metabolism alone. The calculator estimates total daily energy expenditure by multiplying BMR by an activity factor.

TDEE = BMR × activity factor

The available factors are:

  • 1.2, sedentary: little or no planned exercise and mostly sitting during the day.
  • 1.375, light activity: light exercise or sport around one to three days per week.
  • 1.55, moderate activity: moderate exercise around three to five days per week.
  • 1.725, very active: hard exercise or sport around six to seven days per week.
  • 1.9, extra active: very demanding training or a physically strenuous job.

The activity categories are broad descriptions. Two people who exercise the same number of days may still use different amounts of energy because their sessions, jobs, daily movement and bodies differ. A person with a desk job who attends a few short workouts may not fit the very-active category simply because exercise appears on most days.

Worked examples

Example 1: A moderately active man

Age: 30 years. Weight: 70 kg. Height: 175 cm. Activity factor: 1.55.

BMR = 10(70) + 6.25(175) − 5(30) + 5

= 700 + 1,093.75 − 150 + 5 = 1,648.75

Maintenance estimate: 1,648.75 × 1.55 = 2,555.56, or about 2,560 calories per day when rounded to the nearest ten.

Example 2: A lightly active woman

Age: 28 years. Weight: 60 kg. Height: 165 cm. Activity factor: 1.375.

BMR = 10(60) + 6.25(165) − 5(28) − 161

= 600 + 1,031.25 − 140 − 161 = 1,330.25

Maintenance estimate: 1,330.25 × 1.375 = 1,829.09, or about 1,830 calories per day.

Example 3: How activity changes the estimate

For the 30-year-old man in Example 1, the BMR remains about 1,649 calories. Only the activity factor changes. At 1.2, the estimate is about 1,979. At 1.375, it is about 2,267. At 1.55, it is about 2,556. At 1.725, it is about 2,845. At 1.9, it is about 3,133 calories per day.

This illustrates why choosing an activity level that reflects ordinary life matters. Selecting a higher factor raises the maintenance estimate even though the height, weight and age have not changed.

Example 4: Entering US units

A 40-year-old man weighs 180 lb and is 5 ft 10 in tall. The calculator first converts the weight and height to metric units: 180 lb is about 81.65 kg, and 5 ft 10 in is 177.8 cm. With a sedentary factor of 1.2, the BMR is about 1,729 calories and the maintenance estimate is about 2,075 calories per day, rounded to roughly 2,080.

Understanding the goal estimates

The calculator shows example daily targets around the maintenance estimate. It uses an adjustment of about 275 calories per day for a slower change of approximately 0.25 kg per week, and about 550 calories per day for approximately 0.5 kg per week. The same amounts are added for the corresponding gain estimates.

illustrative weight-loss target = maintenance calories − daily adjustment

illustrative weight-gain target = maintenance calories + daily adjustment

These are simplified estimates based on an approximate energy relationship. The calculator also uses a lower guardrail in its goal table: 1,500 calories for the male formula and 1,200 for the female formula. When an illustrative target falls below that value, the table flags it as too low and displays the guardrail figure. These numbers are part of the calculator's calculation logic; they should not be interpreted as universal safe minimums or a personalised recommendation.

Weight change is not perfectly linear. Water balance, digestion, changes in activity, muscle mass, sleep, medication and metabolic adaptation can affect the scale. A calculation should never be used to justify severe restriction or to replace professional advice.

Calories, kilocalories and food labels

In scientific writing, a calorie with a lowercase c is a small unit of energy. Nutrition labels commonly use a capital-C Calorie, which is equal to one kilocalorie (kcal), or 1,000 small calories. In everyday nutrition conversations, “calories” usually means kilocalories. This calculator follows that common food-energy convention.

Energy values on packaged foods may be shown in both kilocalories and kilojoules. One kilocalorie is approximately 4.184 kilojoules. Do not compare a value in kJ directly with a value in kcal without converting the units.

Why the estimate may differ from real maintenance needs

An equation cannot capture every factor that affects energy use. The result can be higher or lower than an individual's actual needs. The Mifflin–St Jeor equation is a useful general estimate, but it is not a laboratory measurement. Activity multipliers also simplify a wide range of jobs, exercise sessions and daily movement into five categories.

For a practical reality check, some adults compare the estimate with their weight trend over several weeks while keeping food intake and activity reasonably consistent. Day-to-day scale changes are strongly affected by water and food volume, so a single measurement is not enough to judge whether an estimate is accurate. Any tracking should be approached in a way that supports wellbeing rather than creating distress or rigid food rules.

Who should seek individual guidance

This tool is not designed to provide nutrition targets for everyone. People under 18, people who are pregnant or breastfeeding, athletes with demanding training, people with chronic health conditions, and anyone recovering from an eating disorder may need individual assessment. A registered dietitian or qualified healthcare professional can take medical history, growth, medication, activity and other relevant factors into account.

If calorie counting causes anxiety, compulsive checking or restrictive behaviour, stop using the estimate and seek appropriate support. A number on a screen is not a measure of health, worth or personal success. Adequate nutrition, sleep, movement, recovery and overall health matter alongside energy intake.

Common mistakes when using a calorie calculator

  • Confusing BMR with maintenance. BMR estimates resting energy use. The maintenance estimate is higher because it includes an activity factor.
  • Overstating activity. Choose the category that best represents your overall routine, not the most intense day of the week.
  • Mixing metric and US units. If Metric is selected, enter kilograms and centimetres. If US is selected, enter pounds, feet and inches.
  • Entering height incorrectly. In US mode, split height into feet and remaining inches. Five feet ten inches is not entered as 5.10 feet.
  • Treating the output as exact. It is an estimate based on a formula and broad activity categories.
  • Assuming the goal table is a prescription. The displayed adjustments are simplified calculations, not individual medical advice.
  • Reacting to one day's weight. Short-term changes do not necessarily reflect changes in body fat or long-term energy balance.

How to use the calculator

Select the sex option used by the equation, choose Metric or US units, and select the activity level that most closely describes your normal routine. Enter age, weight and height. The calculator accepts ages from 15 to 100 and checks that height and weight fall within broad input ranges. If the values are outside those ranges, review the units and entries.

The main result is the estimated daily maintenance calories. The note gives the approximate BMR, and the workings explain the unit conversion, BMR calculation and activity multiplication. The goal table presents illustrative changes around maintenance and a balanced macronutrient split based on the calculator's displayed proportions. These outputs should be read as educational estimates, not a complete meal plan.

  3. Why should someone avoid selecting “extra active” just because they exercise twice a week?
  4. What must be done before entering 180 lb and 5 ft 10 in into a metric-only equation?

Answers: 1. BMR is estimated energy use at rest; maintenance also includes activity and is estimated by multiplying BMR by an activity factor. 2. 1,500 × 1.2 = 1,800 calories per day. 3. The category describes overall activity and demanding training or physical work, not simply the existence of a few exercise sessions. 4. Convert the weight to kilograms and height to centimetres.

Macronutrients are not the same as calories

Calories describe energy, while macronutrients describe the main nutrients that supply much of the energy in food. Protein and carbohydrate provide about 4 kilocalories per gram, while fat provides about 9 kilocalories per gram. Alcohol provides about 7 kilocalories per gram, although it is not treated as an essential macronutrient. Foods also contain water, fibre, vitamins, minerals and other compounds that affect nutrition beyond the energy number.

The calculator displays an illustrative balanced split at maintenance using 25% of energy from protein, 50% from carbohydrate and 25% from fat. It converts those energy shares into grams by dividing protein and carbohydrate calories by 4 and fat calories by 9. This is a mathematical illustration only. It is not a personalised macro prescription and does not assess individual protein needs, medical conditions, food access or dietary preferences.

If the maintenance estimate is 2,000 calories, the displayed split assigns 500 calories to protein, 1,000 to carbohydrate and 500 to fat. That is approximately 125 g protein, 250 g carbohydrate and 55.6 g fat. The figures are rounded and are simply one way to divide the estimated energy total.

Energy balance is useful, but it is not the whole picture

Over time, body weight is influenced by the relationship between energy intake and energy use. However, a person's health cannot be reduced to a calorie total. Food quality, nutrient adequacy, sleep, stress, medications, physical activity, illness and many other factors influence wellbeing. Two diets with similar calorie totals may differ considerably in fibre, protein, micronutrients and how satisfying they are.

Use an estimate as context rather than as a score. The purpose of the calculation is to explain how an equation arrives at a rough energy figure. It should not encourage skipping meals, ignoring hunger, exercising to compensate for eating or pursuing rapid weight changes. If a change in eating or weight is medically important, discuss it with a qualified professional who can consider the full situation.

Frequently asked questions

What is BMR?

It is the estimated energy your body uses at rest to support essential functions such as breathing, circulation and temperature regulation.

What is TDEE?

Total daily energy expenditure is an estimate of the energy used across a full day. This calculator estimates it by multiplying BMR by an activity factor.

How accurate is the result?

It is an estimate, not a direct measurement. Individual energy needs can differ from the equation because of body composition, health, activity and other factors.

Why does the calculator ask for sex?

The Mifflin–St Jeor equation uses different constants in its male and female versions. The calculator uses the selected option to choose the corresponding equation.

Should I automatically eat the lowest number shown?

No. The goal figures are simplified estimates and are not personal medical advice. A qualified professional can help determine an appropriate approach for individual circumstances.

Does this calculator create a meal plan?

No. It estimates energy needs and displays some illustrative figures. It does not assess food preferences, nutrient requirements, allergies, medical needs or meal quality.

Simple machines: mechanical advantage, velocity ratio and efficiency

Simple machines make tasks easier by changing the size or direction of a force, or by changing how far a force acts. A lever can help lift a heavy load, a pulley can redirect a pull, and an inclined plane can reduce the effort needed to raise an object. A machine does not create energy. In an ideal machine, a smaller effort is exchanged for a longer distance moved. The Phademy Simple Machines Calculator focuses on mechanical advantage, velocity ratio and efficiency, the three quantities used in many school physics questions.

What counts as a simple machine?

A simple machine is a basic mechanical device that helps transmit or modify a force. The traditional categories include the lever, pulley, inclined plane, wheel and axle, wedge and screw. Many complex machines are combinations of these basic mechanisms. A bottle opener, a ramp, a pair of scissors and a screw jack all use simple-machine principles.

The important idea is not that a machine removes the work required. Instead, it changes the relationship between effort force and the distance over which that force acts. A machine may let you use less force, but you generally need to move the effort through a greater distance. This trade-off is an expression of energy conservation.

Some machines mainly change the direction of a force rather than multiply it. A fixed pulley lets you pull down to lift a load upward. Its ideal mechanical advantage is 1, but the change in direction can make the task more convenient. A machine can therefore be useful even when its force advantage is not greater than one.

Mechanical advantage

Mechanical advantage, MA, compares the load force with the effort force: MA = Load/Effort. The load is the force the machine is intended to overcome, such as the weight of an object. The effort is the force applied by the user or an input mechanism. Mechanical advantage has no unit because it is a ratio of two forces measured in the same unit.

If MA is greater than 1, the machine produces a larger output force than the input effort. If MA equals 1, the force magnitudes are equal. If MA is less than 1, the machine provides a gain in speed or distance rather than force. A fishing rod is a familiar example where the effort may be greater than the load force, but the tip moves through a much larger distance.

Rearranging the formula gives Load = MA × Effort and Effort = Load/MA. These forms are useful when a question asks how much force is needed to lift a load or what load can be balanced by a known effort. Always use force values in the same unit, normally newtons.

Velocity ratio

Velocity ratio, VR, compares the distance moved by the effort with the distance moved by the load: VR = effort distance/load distance. It is a ratio and has no unit. In an ideal machine, it describes the distance trade-off required by the machine’s geometry. A large velocity ratio means the effort moves a long way compared with the load.

For a pulley system, the velocity ratio is often equal to the number of rope sections supporting the moving load. If four rope sections support the load, the effort must move about four metres for the load to rise one metre in an ideal arrangement. The exact setup matters, so count the supporting sections rather than every visible pulley wheel.

For a lever, velocity ratio is related to the effort arm and load arm lengths. For an inclined plane, it is the length of the slope divided by its vertical height. These geometric relationships help calculate VR without measuring the movement directly. The calculator itself uses the effort-distance and load-distance values you enter.

Efficiency

Efficiency tells you what percentage of the input work or energy becomes useful output. For the simple machine relationships used here, efficiency η = (MA/VR) × 100%. Mechanical advantage describes force multiplication, while velocity ratio describes the distance trade-off. Their ratio indicates how much of the ideal performance is achieved in practice.

An ideal machine has no energy losses, so MA equals VR and efficiency is 100%. Real machines have friction, deformation, sound and moving parts that absorb energy. Therefore, useful output work is less than input work and efficiency is usually below 100%. A machine with MA = 4 and VR = 5 has efficiency (4/5) × 100 = 80%.

Efficiency is not the same as mechanical advantage. A machine can have a high mechanical advantage but a lower efficiency because it loses energy through friction. Similarly, a machine with MA below 1 can still be useful if it increases speed or movement distance. State clearly which quantity the question asks for.

Work and the force-distance trade-off

Work done by a constant force acting in the direction of movement is W = Fd. Input work is effort multiplied by the distance moved by the effort. Useful output work is load multiplied by the distance moved by the load. Efficiency can therefore also be written as useful output work divided by input work, multiplied by 100%.

For an ideal machine, input work equals output work. If a 600 N load rises 0.5 m, output work is 600 × 0.5 = 300 J. If the effort force is 150 N and moves 2 m, input work is 150 × 2 = 300 J. The machine has traded a fourfold force advantage for a fourfold distance moved.

For a real machine, input work is greater than useful output work. If the same 600 N load rises 0.5 m but the effort is 200 N and moves 2 m, input work is 400 J while useful output work is 300 J. Efficiency is 300/400 × 100 = 75%. The missing 100 J has been transferred to other forms, often thermal energy due to friction.

How the main simple machines work

A lever rotates around a pivot called the fulcrum. The effort and load act at different distances from the pivot. A longer effort arm can reduce the effort force needed, but the effort end moves farther. A seesaw, crowbar and wheelbarrow are familiar examples. The position of the fulcrum determines the relative arm lengths and the mechanical advantage.

A pulley is a grooved wheel with a rope or cable. A fixed pulley mainly changes the direction of the effort. A movable pulley can reduce the effort needed by sharing the load across several supporting rope sections. In an ideal system, the number of supporting sections gives the velocity ratio and often the ideal mechanical advantage.

An inclined plane is a sloping surface used to raise or lower a load over a longer path. A longer ramp for the same vertical height reduces the required force in the ideal case, but the load travels farther. A screw can be understood as an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. A wedge is like a moving inclined plane that concentrates force along an edge.

A practical method for solving machine questions

Identify the load and effort before using the formulas. The load is not automatically the mass of the object. If the question gives mass, calculate weight using W = mg when the load is the object’s weight. Record the force in newtons. Then identify which distance belongs to the effort and which belongs to the load.

If the question gives force values, calculate MA. If it gives movement distances, calculate VR. If it gives MA and VR, calculate efficiency. When one quantity is missing, rearrange the appropriate equation. Do not mix a distance ratio with a force ratio: they answer different questions.

After calculating efficiency, check whether the result makes sense. For an ordinary passive machine, efficiency should not exceed 100%. If it does, recheck whether load and effort have been reversed, whether the distances are assigned to the correct moving parts, and whether all values refer to the same machine and operating condition.

Worked examples

Calculate mechanical advantage

A machine lifts a 600 N load using an effort of 150 N. MA = Load/Effort = 600/150 = 4. The machine provides a fourfold force advantage in this measurement.

Find the effort needed

A machine has mechanical advantage 5 and must lift a 1,000 N load. Effort = Load/MA = 1,000/5 = 200 N. The machine reduces the required effort force, but the effort must move a greater distance.

Calculate velocity ratio

The effort moves 3.6 m while the load rises 0.9 m. VR = 3.6/0.9 = 4. The effort moves four times as far as the load.

Calculate efficiency

A machine has MA = 4 and VR = 5. Efficiency = (4/5) × 100 = 80%. The machine delivers 80% of the ideal force-distance performance.

Find MA from efficiency and VR

A machine has efficiency 75% and VR = 4. Since 75 = (MA/4) × 100, MA = 0.75 × 4 = 3. The actual mechanical advantage is 3.

Work input and output

A 500 N load rises 0.4 m, while an effort of 125 N moves 2 m. Output work = 500 × 0.4 = 200 J. Input work = 125 × 2 = 250 J. Efficiency = 200/250 × 100 = 80%.

A fixed pulley

A fixed pulley changes the direction of the effort but ideally does not multiply force. If a 100 N load is lifted with a 100 N effort, MA = 1. If effort and load move equal distances, VR = 1 and ideal efficiency is 100%. Real friction makes the actual efficiency lower.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Swapping load and effort in the mechanical advantage formula.
  • Using mass in kilograms where the formula requires force in newtons.
  • Reversing effort distance and load distance in the velocity ratio.
  • Assuming every pulley wheel is one supporting rope section.
  • Treating efficiency and mechanical advantage as the same quantity.
  • Reporting efficiency above 100% without checking the measurements or formula.
  • Assuming a machine creates energy or removes the need to do work.

Practice questions

  • A 900 N load is lifted with an effort of 300 N. Find mechanical advantage.
  • The effort moves 5 m while the load moves 1 m. Find velocity ratio.
  • A machine has MA = 3 and VR = 4. Calculate its efficiency.
  • A 40 kg load is lifted near Earth’s surface. Estimate its weight using g = 9.8 m/s².
  • Explain why a longer ramp reduces the effort force but does not create energy.

Calculator scope and limitations

The calculator has separate formula modes for mechanical advantage, velocity ratio and efficiency. It calculates MA from load and effort, VR from the two distances, and efficiency from MA and VR. Enter force values in newtons and distance values in the same unit. The ratios themselves have no units, while efficiency is shown as a percentage.

The calculator does not identify the geometry of a specific lever, pulley or gear arrangement. You must determine the correct load, effort and movement distances from the diagram or question. It also does not calculate friction forces or model a machine dynamically. The ideal relationships are used to interpret the quantities, while real efficiency reflects losses in the actual machine.

Frequently asked questions

What is mechanical advantage?

It is the ratio of load force to effort force. It tells you how much the machine multiplies the applied force.

What is velocity ratio?

It is the distance moved by the effort divided by the distance moved by the load. It describes the movement trade-off in the machine.

Why is efficiency usually below 100%?

Friction, deformation and other losses mean that not all input work becomes useful output work.

Can mechanical advantage be less than one?

Yes. Some machines provide a gain in speed or distance rather than force, so the effort force can be greater than the load force.

Does a fixed pulley multiply force?

An ideal fixed pulley mainly changes the direction of the applied force. Its ideal mechanical advantage and velocity ratio are both 1.

How do I calculate velocity ratio for a pulley?

Count the rope sections supporting the moving load in a simple ideal pulley system. That count is the velocity ratio in the usual school model.

Can efficiency be 100%?

Only in an ideal machine with no energy losses. Real machines have friction and other losses, so their efficiency is below 100%.

Why does a ramp make lifting easier?

It allows the same vertical rise over a longer distance, reducing the force needed in the ideal case while increasing the distance over which the effort acts.

What a linear equation calculator does

A linear equation is an equation in which the unknown has a power of one. The graph of a linear equation in one variable is not usually the main focus; the task is to find the value that makes the left-hand side equal to the right-hand side. Examples include 3x + 5 = 20, 2(x − 4) = x + 7 and x/3 + 2 = 5. A linear equation calculator helps you solve these expressions without skipping the algebraic steps. Enter one equation, and Phademy returns the value of the variable, the working and a check that substitutes the answer into the original equation.

The calculator accepts common algebra notation, including brackets, decimals and fractions such as 1/2. It is designed for equations with one unknown. If the unknown appears squared, use the quadratic equation calculator. If there are two or more unknowns in a system, use the simultaneous equations calculator. These distinctions matter because choosing the right tool is the first step toward getting a meaningful answer.

How to enter an equation correctly

Type the equation as a complete statement with an equals sign. For example, enter 3x + 5 = 20, not just 3x + 5. Use the letter x or another single variable consistently throughout the expression. Multiplication can be written as 3x or 3*x, and brackets can be used to show which terms belong together. Write a fraction with a slash, such as x/3 or (x + 2)/5. Use a decimal point for decimal values.

When an equation contains a minus sign, make sure it is attached to the correct term. The expressions −2x + 7 and −(2x + 7) are not the same. The first means negative two times x, then add seven. The second means take the negative of the entire bracket. Brackets are especially important when a negative number or a fraction applies to more than one term.

Before calculating, check that both sides of the equals sign are present and that the equation contains only one variable. The tool is not a general-purpose expression simplifier in this mode. If you enter an expression without an equals sign, it does not define a value of x to solve for.

The balance principle behind solving equations

An equation states that two expressions have the same value. Think of the equals sign as a balance: if you add, subtract, multiply or divide one side by a valid quantity, you must do the same to the other side to preserve equality. The aim is to isolate the variable, meaning that the variable is left by itself on one side and its numerical value is on the other.

For a simple equation such as x + 7 = 12, subtract 7 from both sides. This gives x = 5. For 4x = 28, divide both sides by 4 to get x = 7. When the variable has both a coefficient and a constant, reverse the operations in the correct order. In 3x + 5 = 20, undo the addition first and the multiplication second.

Doing the same operation to both sides is more reliable than memorising a rule that says a term changes sign when it crosses the equals sign. The sign change is a shortcut for adding or subtracting the term on both sides. Understanding the balance principle helps prevent errors when equations become longer.

Worked example: a two-step equation

Equation: 3x + 5 = 20.

  1. Subtract 5 from both sides: 3x + 5 − 5 = 20 − 5.
  2. Simplify: 3x = 15.
  3. Divide both sides by 3: x = 15 ÷ 3.
  4. Answer: x = 5.

Check: put 5 into the original equation. The left side is 3(5) + 5 = 15 + 5 = 20. The right side is 20. Both sides match, so the answer is correct.

Worked example: brackets and the variable on both sides

Equation: 2(x + 4) = 3x − 1.

  1. Expand the bracket: 2x + 8 = 3x − 1.
  2. Subtract 2x from both sides: 8 = x − 1.
  3. Add 1 to both sides: 9 = x.
  4. Answer: x = 9.

Check: the left side is 2(9 + 4) = 26. The right side is 3(9) − 1 = 26. The two sides are equal.

This example shows why you should expand brackets before collecting terms. It also shows that the unknown does not have to start on the left. You can move terms in a controlled way until the variable is isolated.

Worked example: equations with fractions

Consider x/3 + 2 = 7. Subtract 2 from both sides to obtain x/3 = 5. Multiply both sides by 3, giving x = 15. A second example is x/2 + x/3 = 5. The lowest common denominator of 2 and 3 is 6. Multiply every term on both sides by 6: 3x + 2x = 30. Combine like terms to get 5x = 30, so x = 6.

When clearing fractions, multiply every term in the entire equation by the common denominator. Do not multiply only the fractions and leave a whole number unchanged. In the equation x/2 + 3 = 8, multiplying every term by 2 gives x + 6 = 16, not x + 3 = 16.

Worked example: decimals and negative coefficients

Solve 0.5x − 1.5 = 4. Add 1.5 to both sides to get 0.5x = 5.5. Divide by 0.5, or equivalently multiply by 2, to obtain x = 11. Check: 0.5(11) − 1.5 = 5.5 − 1.5 = 4.

Now solve −3x + 4 = 19. Subtract 4 to get −3x = 15. Divide both sides by −3, so x = −5. A negative coefficient does not change the balance rule. It simply means the final division is by a negative number.

Equations with no solution or infinitely many solutions

Not every equation has one numerical answer. Sometimes the variable disappears and the remaining statement is false. For example, 2x + 1 = 2x + 5 becomes 1 = 5 after subtracting 2x from both sides. Since 1 is not equal to 5, no value of x can make the original equation true. This is called an inconsistent equation, and the calculator reports that there is no solution.

In another situation, the variable disappears and leaves a statement that is always true. For example, 2x + 4 = 2(x + 2) expands to 2x + 4 = 2x + 4. Subtracting 2x gives 4 = 4. Every value of x satisfies the equation, so there are infinitely many solutions. The calculator identifies this special case rather than inventing one answer.

How to check a solution independently

Substitution is the most direct check. Take the value returned for x and replace every x in the original equation with that value. Simplify the left side and right side separately. If they produce the same number, the value is a solution. Always use the original equation for this check, not a rearranged line that may contain an earlier mistake.

For example, if a solution is x = 4 for 5x − 6 = 14, substitute 4: 5(4) − 6 = 20 − 6 = 14. The right side is also 14. If one side gives 13 or 15, revisit the algebra. A check can reveal a sign error, an incorrect expansion or a division mistake even when the final line looks neat.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Changing only one side: subtracting 5 from the left but not the right breaks the equality.
  • Expanding brackets incorrectly: 3(x + 2) is 3x + 6, not 3x + 2.
  • Combining unlike terms: 2x + 3 cannot be simplified to 5x because x and a constant are different kinds of terms.
  • Forgetting a negative sign: −2(x − 3) expands to −2x + 6.
  • Clearing fractions incompletely: multiply every term by the common denominator.
  • Dividing by zero: division by zero is not a valid algebraic operation.
  • Checking a different equation: substitute into the exact original equation that was entered.

When a linear equation is useful

Linear equations appear whenever a quantity changes at a constant rate or a fixed starting amount is combined with a per-unit amount. A taxi fare might include a fixed booking charge plus a cost for every kilometre. If the total fare and the rate are known, an equation can help find the distance. A phone plan may have a monthly fee plus a charge per extra unit. A student can model a target score by combining marks already earned with marks still needed.

For example, suppose a service charges a fixed ₦500 plus ₦200 for each item. If the bill is ₦1,700, write 500 + 200x = 1,700. Subtract 500: 200x = 1,200. Divide by 200: x = 6 items. The equation translates a short real-life description into a calculation. The answer still needs common-sense checking: six items must be possible in the situation being described.

Frequently asked questions

What is a linear equation?

It is an equation where the variable has a highest power of one. Examples include 2x + 3 = 9 and 4y − 7 = 13. An equation containing x² is not linear.

Can I solve an equation with fractions?

Yes. Enter fractions with a slash, such as x/3 + 2 = 7 or 1/2x = 4. Use brackets where needed to make the intended numerator or denominator clear.

Can the calculator solve equations with decimals?

Yes. Enter decimal values with a decimal point, such as 0.5x − 1.5 = 4. Check that you have not confused a decimal point with a thousands separator.

Why does an equation have no solution?

After simplifying, it may leave a false statement such as 1 = 5. That means no value of the variable can make both sides equal.

What does infinitely many solutions mean?

It means the two sides simplify to the same expression, such as 2x + 4 = 2(x + 2). Every value of x makes the statement true.

Why should I substitute the answer back?

Substitution checks that the proposed value satisfies the original equation. It is a simple way to catch arithmetic and sign errors.

Can I use this for two variables?

This calculator is intended for one unknown. Use the simultaneous equations calculator for a system involving two or more variables.

Turning a word problem into a linear equation

Start by deciding what the unknown represents and giving it a clear symbol. Then identify the relationship described in the question. Words such as “per”, “each” and “every” often indicate a rate multiplied by the unknown. Words such as “starting fee”, “initial amount” or “already has” often indicate a fixed value. Finally, connect the expressions with the total or condition stated in the problem.

For example, a learner has already saved ₦2,000 and adds the same amount each week. After six weeks, the total is ₦5,000. Let w be the weekly amount. The equation is 2,000 + 6w = 5,000. Subtract 2,000 to get 6w = 3,000, then divide by 6: w = ₦500. Check the meaning of the answer: six deposits of ₦500 add ₦3,000 to the original ₦2,000.

Another example is a rectangle whose length is 4 cm more than its width and whose perimeter is 28 cm. Let the width be x, so the length is x + 4. The perimeter equation is 2x + 2(x + 4) = 28. Expand to get 4x + 8 = 28, so 4x = 20 and x = 5. The width is 5 cm and the length is 9 cm. Both dimensions are positive, and 2(5) + 2(9) = 28.

Practice questions with answers

  1. Solve 7x − 9 = 26. Add 9 to get 7x = 35, then divide by 7. Answer: x = 5.
  2. Solve 4(x − 2) = 2x + 6. Expand to 4x − 8 = 2x + 6. Subtract 2x and add 8: 2x = 14. Answer: x = 7.
  3. Solve x/4 + 3 = 8. Subtract 3, then multiply by 4. Answer: x = 20.
  4. Solve 5x + 2 = 5x − 1. Subtract 5x from both sides to get 2 = −1. Answer: no solution.

Try each question before reading the explanation. After finding a value, substitute it into the original equation. If the two sides are equal, your result is verified. If the question is a word problem, also check that the answer has a sensible unit and fits the context.

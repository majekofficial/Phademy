Density, pressure, liquid pressure and upthrust explained

Density and pressure describe different physical ideas, even though they often appear together in mechanics questions. Density tells you how much mass is contained in a given volume. Pressure tells you how much force acts over a given area. In a liquid, pressure increases with depth because of the weight of the liquid above. Upthrust is the upward force exerted by a fluid on an immersed object. The Phademy calculator brings these four relationships together so you can select a formula, choose the unknown and work through the calculation with consistent SI units.

Density: mass packed into a volume

Density is mass per unit volume. Its symbol is the Greek letter rho, ρ, and its SI unit is kilogram per cubic metre (kg/m³). The relationship is ρ = m/V, where m is mass in kilograms and V is volume in cubic metres. Rearranging gives m = ρV and V = m/ρ. These three forms answer the common questions: how dense is the material, how much mass occupies this volume, or how much space does this mass occupy?

Density is an intensive property for a uniform material under specified conditions. Cutting a block into smaller pieces reduces each piece’s mass and volume, but the ratio m/V remains the same. This explains why a small piece of iron can be denser than a much larger piece of wood. Size alone does not determine density.

For an object made from more than one material or containing air spaces, average density is found by dividing the total mass by the total external volume. This average value is often the useful one when predicting whether an object floats. Temperature and pressure can change density, especially in gases, so reference values should be treated as approximate unless conditions are stated.

Pressure: force spread over an area

Pressure is the normal force acting per unit area. The formula is P = F/A, where P is pressure in pascals, F is force in newtons and A is area in square metres. One pascal is one newton per square metre. Rearranging gives F = PA and A = F/P. The formula shows that increasing force increases pressure, while increasing the contact area reduces pressure for the same force.

A person standing on soft ground exerts more pressure when balancing on one foot than when standing on two feet, because the supporting area is smaller. A sharp blade concentrates force along a narrow edge, producing high pressure that helps it cut. Snowshoes spread a person’s weight over a larger area, reducing pressure on the snow.

In basic problems, use the component of force perpendicular to the surface. A force acting at an angle can be resolved into perpendicular and parallel components. The parallel component may cause sliding, while the perpendicular component contributes to the contact pressure. Do not automatically use the full angled force if the question gives an angle and asks for normal pressure.

Pressure in a liquid

For a liquid of uniform density at rest, the gauge pressure at depth h is P = ρgh. Here ρ is the liquid density, g is gravitational field strength and h is the vertical depth below the free surface. The equation explains why pressure increases when you go deeper, why a denser liquid produces greater pressure at the same depth, and why stronger gravity increases the pressure gradient.

The pressure at a particular depth does not depend on the shape or width of the container. A narrow vessel and a wide tank containing the same liquid can have the same pressure at the same depth. The amount of liquid and the total force on the bottom may differ, but the pressure at a given depth is determined by density, gravity and depth.

The formula ρgh gives gauge pressure, meaning pressure above the pressure at the liquid surface. Absolute pressure is the surface pressure plus the gauge pressure. In an open tank, atmospheric pressure acts at the surface. Many school questions ask only for the pressure due to the liquid, so read the wording carefully before adding atmospheric pressure.

Upthrust and Archimedes’ principle

An object immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force called upthrust. Archimedes’ principle states that the upthrust equals the weight of the fluid displaced by the object. For a fluid of density ρ and a displaced volume V, U = ρgV. The volume is the volume of fluid displaced, which equals the submerged volume of the object when the fluid is effectively incompressible.

If an object is fully submerged, the displaced volume equals its full external volume. If it floats partly above the surface, only the submerged part displaces fluid. This is why the upthrust on a floating object adjusts to balance its weight. A floating object is in vertical equilibrium when upthrust equals weight, even though it may be only partly immersed.

An object sinks when its weight exceeds the maximum upthrust available before it is fully submerged. It rises when upthrust is greater than its weight. For a freely floating object at rest, the average density of the object is less than the density of the fluid. An object can be made to float by changing its shape, as happens with a steel ship: its hollow hull increases the total displaced volume and lowers its average density.

Units and conversions that prevent errors

The formulas in this calculator use SI units. Convert mass to kilograms, volume to cubic metres, area to square metres, depth to metres and force to newtons before substituting. One litre is 0.001 m³, one cubic centimetre is 0.000001 m³, and one gram per cubic centimetre is equivalent to 1,000 kg/m³. These conversions are common in laboratory and examination questions.

If a value is given in grams, divide by 1,000 to obtain kilograms. If a depth is given in centimetres, divide by 100 to obtain metres. If an area is in cm², divide by 10,000 to obtain m². Convert each quantity independently and write the converted value beside the original so that you do not accidentally mix units within one calculation.

Pressure is sometimes given in kilopascals or atmospheres. One kilopascal is 1,000 pascals. If a question gives pressure in kPa, convert to Pa when combining it with SI force and area values. Always state the unit with the final answer, because a bare number does not communicate what physical quantity has been calculated.

How to choose the right relationship

First identify what the question asks you to find. If it describes a mass and volume, start with density. If it describes a force acting on a surface, use pressure. If it mentions a depth below a liquid surface, consider ρgh. If it asks for the upward force on an immersed object, use Archimedes’ principle. The wording often points directly to the correct formula.

Next list the known values with units. Compare them with the symbols in the chosen equation and identify the missing quantity. Rearrange before substituting if the unknown is not already the subject. This approach reduces the chance of inserting a value into the wrong place and makes your working easier for a teacher or examiner to follow.

Finally, check whether the answer is physically reasonable. A density should be positive for an ordinary material. Pressure should increase when depth increases if the liquid and gravity stay the same. Upthrust should increase with displaced volume and fluid density. A result with an unexpected unit or an implausible size usually points to a conversion or substitution error.

Worked examples

Calculate density

A sample has a mass of 2.4 kg and a volume of 0.003 m³. Density is ρ = 2.4/0.003 = 800 kg/m³. The answer is lower than water’s approximate density of 1,000 kg/m³, so a compact object made uniformly from this material would tend to float in fresh water.

Find mass from density

A metal has density 7,800 kg/m³ and occupies 0.002 m³. Use m = ρV = 7,800 × 0.002 = 15.6 kg. The result is a mass, not a weight. Its weight near Earth would be approximately 15.6 × 9.8 = 152.9 N.

Calculate pressure on a surface

A force of 600 N acts normally over an area of 0.30 m². P = F/A = 600/0.30 = 2,000 Pa. If the same force acts over 0.10 m², pressure becomes 6,000 Pa, three times as large.

Pressure at a depth

Find the gauge pressure 4 m below the surface of water with density 1,000 kg/m³, using g = 9.8 m/s². P = ρgh = 1,000 × 9.8 × 4 = 39,200 Pa, or 39.2 kPa. Atmospheric pressure is not included in this gauge-pressure result.

Calculate upthrust

A submerged object displaces 0.0015 m³ of water. U = ρgV = 1,000 × 9.8 × 0.0015 = 14.7 N. This is the upward buoyant force. Compare it with the object’s weight to determine whether it accelerates upward, downward or is in equilibrium.

Floating object and displaced volume

A floating object weighs 25 N in fresh water. At equilibrium, the upthrust is 25 N. The displaced volume is V = U/(ρg) = 25/(1,000 × 9.8) ≈ 0.00255 m³, or about 2.55 litres.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Entering litres or cubic centimetres as though they were cubic metres.
  • Using grams where the equation expects kilograms.
  • Confusing mass, measured in kilograms, with weight, measured in newtons.
  • Using the total volume instead of the submerged volume when calculating upthrust for a partly immersed object.
  • Adding atmospheric pressure when the question asks only for liquid gauge pressure.
  • Using the surface area in cm² without converting it to m².
  • Assuming liquid pressure depends on container width or shape at a fixed depth.

Practice questions

  • A block has mass 500 g and volume 250 cm³. Convert to SI units and calculate its density.
  • A 120 N force acts on an area of 0.04 m². Calculate the pressure.
  • Calculate the gauge pressure 8 m below fresh water using g = 9.8 m/s².
  • A body displaces 0.003 m³ of a liquid with density 850 kg/m³. Find its upthrust.
  • Explain why a floating object has upthrust equal to its weight.

Calculator scope and limitations

The calculator provides four separate formula choices: density, pressure, pressure in a liquid and upthrust. It solves for one selected unknown using the other values. It does not automatically convert every possible unit, so convert the values to the units shown beside the input fields before calculating.

Liquid pressure in the tool is the gauge-pressure relationship ρgh. The upthrust calculation uses the fluid density and displaced or submerged volume. It does not determine the submerged fraction of an object by itself, nor does it model changing fluid density, acceleration, fluid flow or pressure variation in a compressible fluid.

Frequently asked questions

What is the SI unit of density?

Kilogram per cubic metre (kg/m³). A common alternative is g/cm³, where 1 g/cm³ equals 1,000 kg/m³.

What is the difference between pressure and force?

Force is a push or pull measured in newtons. Pressure is force per unit area and is measured in pascals.

Does liquid pressure depend on the container shape?

At a fixed depth in a uniform liquid at rest, pressure depends on density, gravitational field strength and depth, not the container’s shape.

Is ρgh absolute pressure?

No. It is the gauge pressure due to the liquid column. Add the pressure at the surface to obtain absolute pressure.

Why does a ship made of steel float?

Its hollow shape displaces a large volume of water. The average density of the complete ship, including enclosed air, is low enough for the displaced water’s weight to balance the ship’s weight.

What volume is used in the upthrust equation?

Use the volume of fluid displaced. For an object partly submerged, this is the submerged volume, not the full external volume.

What value of g should I use?

Use the value supplied in the question. If none is given, 9.8 m/s² is a common school-level approximation near Earth’s surface.

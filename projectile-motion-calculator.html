How far, how high and how long: a launched object

A projectile is anything thrown or launched and then moved only by gravity: a football, a javelin, water from a hose. Enter the launch speed, the launch angle and, if relevant, the starting height. Choose the value of g to match your course (9.8, 9.81 or 10 m/s²) and the calculator returns the range, the time of flight and the maximum height. Air resistance is ignored.

The idea: two independent motions

Split the launch velocity into a horizontal part u cos θ, which stays constant, and a vertical part u sin θ, which gravity changes. The horizontal and vertical motions are independent and share only the time. For a launch from ground level:

time of flight T = 2u sin θ ÷ g

maximum height H = u² sin²θ ÷ 2g

range R = u² sin 2θ ÷ g

If the starting height is above zero, the object keeps travelling after it passes the launch height, so the time of flight and range are longer. The calculator solves that case directly.

Worked examples

The classic 45° launch

Launch speed 20 m/s at 45° with g = 9.81. R = 400 × sin 90° ÷ 9.81 = 40.7747 m, T = 2 × 20 × sin 45° ÷ 9.81 = 2.8832 s and H = 400 × 0.5 ÷ 19.62 = 10.1937 m.

The exam value of g

Launch speed 25 m/s at 30° with g = 10. T = 2 × 25 × 0.5 ÷ 10 = 2.5 s, H = 625 × 0.25 ÷ 20 = 7.8125 m and R = 625 × sin 60° ÷ 10 = 54.1266 m.

Launching from a height

15 m/s at 30° from a platform 20 m high, with g = 9.8. The time of flight is 2.9257 s, the range 38.006 m and the maximum height above the ground 22.8699 m.

Straight up

At 90° the horizontal range is 0. With 30 m/s and g = 9.8, the ball reaches 45.9184 m and the flight lasts 6.1224 s.

Angles and range

For a launch from ground level, the maximum range occurs at 45°. Two angles that add up to 90°, such as 30° and 60°, give the same range but different heights and times: the steeper one goes higher and stays up longer. Try 25 m/s at 30° and again at 60° with g = 10 and the range is 54.1266 m both times.

What the model leaves out

  • Air resistance, which shortens the range of light or fast objects.
  • Spin and wind.
  • The curvature and rotation of the Earth, which matter only for very long distances.
  • The height at which the object lands. It is assumed to land at ground level.

Common mistakes

  • Entering the angle in radians. The field expects degrees.
  • Using the maximum-range formula for a launch from a height.
  • Mixing g values from different sources in one problem.
  • Treating time of flight as the time to the highest point. That is half the total time at ground level.

Frequently asked questions

What angle gives the greatest range?

45° when the object is launched and lands at the same height and air resistance is ignored.

Why does a higher launch angle give a longer flight?

A larger share of the speed goes into the vertical direction, so the object takes longer to rise and fall.

Does mass affect a projectile's path?

Not when air resistance is ignored. All masses follow the same path for the same launch speed and angle.

Which value of g should I choose?

Use the one your textbook or exam board specifies. 9.8 and 9.81 are standard, and 10 is a common rounding in exams.

Horizontal and vertical components of launch velocity

A launch speed at an angle is a vector, so it has two perpendicular components. The horizontal component is u cos θ and the vertical component is u sin θ. The angle θ is measured above the horizontal. These components explain the curved path: horizontal velocity remains constant in the ideal model, while vertical velocity changes because gravity acts downward. The calculator uses the launch speed, launch angle, optional initial height and a selectable value of gravitational acceleration to determine the trajectory results.

For example, a launch speed of 20 m/s at 30° has a horizontal component of about 17.32 m/s and a vertical component of 10 m/s. The speed is not 20 m/s in each direction. The components combine to give the original 20 m/s vector. Treating each component as the full launch speed would greatly overestimate the range and height.

Understanding the calculator's results

Time of flight is the total time from launch until the projectile reaches the landing level used by the model. Range is the horizontal distance travelled during that time. Maximum height is the greatest vertical position measured from the same reference level as the starting height. Time to maximum height is the time at which vertical velocity becomes zero. Landing speed is the magnitude of the velocity just before landing, and landing angle describes the direction of that velocity relative to the horizontal.

These quantities answer different questions. A projectile can have a long flight time but a modest horizontal range if its horizontal component is small. It can reach a high maximum height but still land relatively close to the launch point. Do not confuse maximum height above the ground with the vertical rise above the launch point when the projectile starts from an elevated position.

Worked example: a ball launched from a platform

Imagine a ball launched at 15 m/s at 30° above the horizontal from a platform 5 m above the landing ground. Choose g = 9.8 m/s². The components are uₓ = 15 cos 30° ≈ 12.99 m/s and uᵧ = 15 sin 30° = 7.5 m/s. The vertical motion starts with a positive upward velocity, but gravity acts downward. The ball therefore rises briefly before falling to the ground below its launch point.

Its time of flight is found by solving the vertical displacement equation for the moment the height reaches zero. The positive time is approximately 2.05 s. The horizontal range is then about 12.99 × 2.05 = 26.6 m. The rise above the launch point is uᵧ²/(2g), approximately 2.87 m, so the maximum height above the ground is about 7.87 m. This example shows why launch height changes the time and range even when speed and angle stay the same.

Why 45 degrees gives maximum range only in a special case

The familiar 45° result assumes that the projectile launches and lands at the same height, gravity is constant and air resistance is ignored. Under those conditions, complementary angles such as 30° and 60° produce the same ideal range for the same launch speed. A 45° launch gives the greatest range because it balances horizontal speed and time in the air.

If the projectile starts from a raised platform and lands at a lower level, the optimum angle is generally not exactly 45°. The projectile has extra time to travel horizontally, and a lower launch angle may produce a longer range. If it lands above its launch point, the optimum angle can shift in the other direction. Avoid applying the 45° shortcut to every projectile question.

What happens at the highest point?

At the highest point of an ideal projectile's path, the vertical component of velocity is zero. The horizontal component is still present, so the projectile is still moving. Its total speed is therefore not zero unless the horizontal component is also zero, as in a purely vertical launch. Gravity continues to act downward at the top of the path, so the vertical acceleration remains −g when upward is positive.

This is another important distinction between velocity and acceleration. The object does not stop responding to gravity at the top. It changes from moving upward to moving downward because the downward acceleration steadily reduces the upward vertical velocity, brings it to zero, and then makes it negative.

Reading the trajectory as a graph

The path of an ideal projectile is a parabola when plotted as vertical position against horizontal position. The horizontal axis measures distance and the vertical axis measures height. The path is symmetric around its highest point only when launch and landing heights are equal. When the projectile lands at a different height, the two sides of the path have different durations and the visible curve is not symmetric around the landing point.

A graph is useful for seeing the shape and comparing trajectories, but it does not replace checking the numerical outputs and units. A graph may use a display scale that makes a path look steeper or flatter than expected. Read the axis labels, and use the calculated range and height for precise answers.

Air resistance, spin and real-world differences

The calculator uses the ideal projectile model and ignores air resistance. In real life, drag acts opposite the direction of motion and usually reduces horizontal speed. A spinning ball can also experience a force that curves its path, known as the Magnus effect. Wind changes the motion relative to the ground. These effects explain why a real football, tennis ball or javelin may not follow the simple predicted parabola.

Ignoring drag is often suitable for introductory physics problems and for comparing the basic effects of speed, angle and gravity. It is less suitable for detailed sports analysis, long-distance projectiles, very light objects or situations where wind and spin are significant. The model is not a safety tool for predicting the landing point of a real launched object.

Choosing a value for gravitational acceleration

The calculator offers 9.8, 9.81 and 10 m/s² because different textbooks and examinations use different rounding conventions. Use the value stated in the question. If no value is given, use the convention required by your course. The difference between 9.8 and 9.81 is small, but it can affect the final decimal places. Using 10 may create a more noticeable difference in time and range.

Do not mix a value of g from one convention with a worked solution that expects another. When comparing two calculations, make sure speed, angle, starting height and gravity are identical. A small difference in output may simply be due to rounding rather than a mistake in the formula.

Practice questions with answers

  1. A ball is launched horizontally at 10 m/s from a 20 m cliff. If g = 10 m/s², how long does it take to fall? Answer: 20 = ½ × 10 × t², so t = 2 s. The ideal horizontal range is 10 × 2 = 20 m.
  2. A projectile is launched from level ground at 20 m/s and 30°. Find its initial vertical component. Answer: 20 sin 30° = 10 m/s.
  3. At the highest point of an angled launch, what is zero? Answer: The vertical velocity component is zero; horizontal velocity and gravitational acceleration remain.

How launch speed changes the trajectory

Launch speed has a strong effect because it changes both velocity components. If the launch angle and gravity stay the same, doubling the launch speed doubles the horizontal and vertical components. The ideal range and the maximum rise above the launch point increase by a factor of four for a same-level launch, while the time of flight doubles. This follows from the equations: range is proportional to speed squared, and maximum rise contains the square of the vertical component. These comparisons assume the launch and landing heights are equal and air resistance remains ignored.

When comparing two launches, change one variable at a time. If speed and angle both change, it may be difficult to identify which change caused the difference in range. Keep the same value of g and starting height as well. This is a useful way to explore the model and build intuition before attempting a longer calculation.

Why the angle is measured from the horizontal

The calculator's angle is measured above the horizontal line, not from the vertical. This matters because the horizontal component uses cosine and the vertical component uses sine. A launch at 0° is horizontal, while a launch at 90° is straight upward. If a question gives an angle from the vertical, convert it by subtracting that angle from 90° before entering it. For example, 20° from the vertical is 70° above the horizontal.

Always sketch a small right triangle beside the launch direction if you are unsure which component is opposite or adjacent to the angle. The hypotenuse is the launch speed. The horizontal side is u cos θ and the vertical side is u sin θ when θ is measured from the horizontal. This simple diagram prevents swapping sine and cosine.

Four force formulas for school physics

A force is a push or a pull, measured in newtons (N). This calculator covers the four relationships met in most courses: Newton's second law, weight, friction and Hooke's law for springs. Choose a formula, choose the quantity you want and enter the others.

The formulas

  • Newton's second law: F = m × a. A resultant force produces acceleration in proportion to the mass.
  • Weight: W = m × g. Weight is the force of gravity on a mass, and it differs from mass.
  • Friction: F = μ × R, where μ is the coefficient of friction and R is the normal reaction force from the surface.
  • Hooke's law: F = k × e, where k is the spring constant and e the extension, valid below the elastic limit.

Worked examples

Newton's second law

A 5 kg trolley accelerates at 3 m/s²: F = 5 × 3 = 15 N. In reverse, a 20 N force producing 4 m/s² acts on a mass of 20 ÷ 4 = 5 kg, and a 30 N force on a 6 kg mass gives an acceleration of 5 m/s².

Mass versus weight

A person with a mass of 70 kg has a weight of 70 × 9.8 = 686 N on Earth. With g = 10 (as many exams use), the weight is 700 N. The mass is still 70 kg. On the Moon, where g is roughly a sixth of Earth's, the weight is much less but the mass is unchanged. The Gravitation Calculator shows how g depends on a planet's mass and radius.

Friction

A box pressing on the floor with a normal force of 500 N and a coefficient of friction of 0.3 meets a maximum friction force of 0.3 × 500 = 150 N.

A spring

A spring with k = 200 N/m stretched by 0.05 m exerts a force of 200 × 0.05 = 10 N. If a 10 N force stretches a spring by 0.04 m, then k = 10 ÷ 0.04 = 250 N/m.

Resultant forces

Newton's second law applies to the resultant, the net force. A 100 N push on a box with 40 N of friction gives a resultant of 60 N. For a 12 kg box, a = 60 ÷ 12 = 5 m/s². Find the resultant first, then use F = ma.

Common mistakes

  • Using weight where mass is needed, or the other way round.
  • Mixing units, for example grams with newtons. Convert grams to kilograms first.
  • Applying Hooke's law beyond the elastic limit, where the spring is permanently deformed.
  • Using the total force instead of the resultant in F = ma.

Frequently asked questions

What is a newton?

The force needed to give a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s².

Is weight the same as mass?

No. Mass is the amount of matter in kilograms. Weight is a force, in newtons, that depends on the local gravitational field.

What value of g does the calculator use?

You can choose 9.8, 10 or 9.81 m/s² for the weight calculation.

Why does friction depend on the normal reaction?

A harder contact between surfaces gives more resistance to sliding. That is why a heavier box is harder to push.

Force, mass and acceleration are not interchangeable

Mass measures how much matter an object contains and is measured in kilograms. Acceleration describes how quickly velocity changes and is measured in metres per second squared. Force is an interaction that can change an object's motion, and the SI unit is the newton. Newton's second law connects the three through F = ma when F represents the resultant force. A 2 kg object accelerating at 3 m/s² has a resultant force of 6 N.

The word resultant is important. Several forces may act at the same time. A person pushing a box, friction from the floor and the box's weight all contribute to the force situation. Newton's second law uses the vector sum of all forces, not simply one force chosen from the diagram. If forces balance, the resultant force is zero and acceleration is zero, even if the object is already moving at constant velocity.

How to draw and interpret a free-body diagram

A free-body diagram isolates one object and shows the external forces acting on it as arrows. The arrow direction represents the direction of the force, and its length can represent relative magnitude. On a level surface, weight acts downward and the normal reaction usually acts upward. A push or pull may act horizontally, while friction acts along the surface opposite the actual or impending relative motion.

Choose positive directions before substituting numbers. If right is positive, a force acting left is negative. If upward is positive, weight is negative. Resolve angled forces into horizontal and vertical components when necessary. The calculator's individual formula modes are useful for a single relationship, but a complex free-body diagram may require you to calculate the resultant force first.

Weight and mass: why the distinction matters

Weight is the gravitational force acting on a mass, calculated using W = mg. Near Earth's surface, g is approximately 9.8 m/s², although some school questions use 9.81 or 10 m/s². A 5 kg object therefore has a weight of about 49 N when g = 9.8 m/s². The object's mass remains 5 kg if it is taken to the Moon, but its weight changes because the local gravitational field is weaker.

A balance that compares masses and a spring scale that measures force do not measure the same physical quantity. In everyday speech people often say their weight in kilograms, but kilograms are units of mass. In physics calculations, write mass in kg and weight in N. Use the gravity selector that matches the question instead of assuming every problem uses the same rounded value.

Friction and the normal reaction

The calculator uses the relationship F = μR for friction, where μ is the coefficient of friction and R is the normal reaction. The coefficient is dimensionless, while the normal reaction is measured in newtons. If μ = 0.4 and R = 50 N, the model gives a friction force of 20 N. The normal reaction is the contact force perpendicular to the surface; on a simple horizontal surface with no other vertical forces, it may equal the object's weight.

Do not assume that the normal reaction always equals mg. On an inclined plane, it is commonly related to the component of weight perpendicular to the plane. A person pushing down on a box can increase the normal reaction, while lifting slightly can reduce it. Angled pulls and vertical acceleration can also change it. The calculator's F = μR relationship does not itself determine R from a complicated diagram; you must first find the correct normal reaction.

Static and kinetic friction: an important model distinction

Static friction acts when surfaces are not sliding relative to one another. It adjusts up to a maximum value to prevent slipping. Kinetic friction acts when surfaces slide. Introductory problems often use a single coefficient and the simplified relationship F = μR, but the coefficient may differ between static and kinetic cases. Read the wording of the question to identify which coefficient is intended.

Friction is not always equal to μR in every situation. For static friction below its limiting value, the actual friction force is whatever is needed to maintain no slipping. The expression μR gives the maximum static friction in the simple model, or the kinetic friction magnitude when the kinetic coefficient is used. Treat the calculator's result according to the assumptions supplied by your exercise.

Hooke's law and spring force

Hooke's law states that the force exerted by an ideal spring is proportional to its extension or compression: F = ke in magnitude. The spring constant k is measured in newtons per metre, and extension e is measured in metres. A spring with k = 200 N/m stretched by 0.05 m produces a restoring force magnitude of 10 N. The direction of the restoring force is opposite the displacement from the spring's natural length.

Use the extension or compression from the natural length, not the spring's total length. If a spring is 20 cm long at rest and 25 cm long when loaded, its extension is 5 cm or 0.05 m. Convert centimetres to metres before entering the value. Hooke's law is a good approximation only within the spring's elastic limit. Beyond that range, the spring may not return to its original length and the simple proportional relationship can fail.

Balanced forces and Newton's first law

If the resultant force on an object is zero, its acceleration is zero. That does not mean the object must be stationary. It may remain at rest, or continue moving in a straight line at constant velocity. This is Newton's first law. A book resting on a table has downward weight balanced by the upward normal reaction. A car travelling at steady speed on a straight, level road has driving force balanced by resistive forces in the simplified model.

When the forces are unbalanced, the object accelerates in the direction of the resultant force. A larger resultant force produces greater acceleration for the same mass. A larger mass requires more force to produce the same acceleration. These relationships are the central meaning of F = ma and are more useful than memorising the formula without understanding the force diagram.

Worked application: a box pulled across a floor

Suppose a 10 kg box is pulled horizontally with a 40 N force. The friction force is 10 N in the opposite direction. The resultant horizontal force is 40 − 10 = 30 N. Newton's second law gives a = F/m = 30/10 = 3 m/s². Notice that the acceleration is not 4 m/s², because the applied force is not the only horizontal force acting on the box.

If the coefficient of friction is 0.2 and the normal reaction is 98 N, the friction magnitude is 0.2 × 98 = 19.6 N instead. The net force and acceleration would then need to be recalculated. This illustrates why the normal reaction and the friction coefficient must match the physical situation.

Practice questions with answers

  1. A 4 kg trolley has a resultant force of 12 N. Find its acceleration. Answer: a = 12/4 = 3 m/s².
  2. Find the weight of a 6 kg object using g = 10 m/s². Answer: W = 6 × 10 = 60 N.
  3. A spring with k = 150 N/m is extended by 0.02 m. Find the force magnitude. Answer: F = 150 × 0.02 = 3 N.

Why the direction of a force must be stated

Force is a vector, so a complete answer includes both magnitude and direction. Saying that a resultant force is 15 N is incomplete if the question asks which way an object accelerates. In one-dimensional problems, a positive or negative sign can show direction after you define an axis. In two-dimensional problems, horizontal and vertical components may be needed. The calculator's formula modes return a numerical value for a selected relationship; the diagram and sign convention in the question provide the physical direction.

For instance, if a 20 N force acts right and a 7 N force acts left, the resultant is 13 N right. If the directions are reversed, the magnitude is still 13 N but the direction is left. Adding magnitudes without considering direction would give 27 N, which is not the resultant in this example.

Newton's third law and force pairs

Newton's third law states that when object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts an equal-magnitude force in the opposite direction on object A. These two forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel each other on a single free-body diagram. A swimmer pushes water backward and the water pushes the swimmer forward. A person presses on a wall and the wall presses back on the person.

This is different from two balanced forces acting on the same object. Weight and normal reaction can balance on a stationary book because both act on the book. The action-reaction pair for the book's weight involves the book attracting Earth, while the normal reaction pair involves the book pushing down on the table. Keeping track of which object each force acts on is essential in force questions.

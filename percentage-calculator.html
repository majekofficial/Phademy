Finding a percentage of any number

Percentages appear everywhere: sale prices, interest, marks, tax and tips. This calculator answers the most common version of the question, which is what is a given percentage of a number. Type the number, type the percentage, and the result appears along with a reminder of what was calculated, such as 36 (15% of 240).

How it works

answer = number × percentage ÷ 100

A percentage is a fraction out of 100, so 15% means 15 hundredths, or 0.15. Multiplying 240 by 0.15 gives 36. The same method works for decimals and for values below 1%.

Worked examples

A simple percentage

What is 15% of 240?

240 × 15 ÷ 100 = 36

Decimal percentages

12.5% of 80 is 80 × 0.125 = 10. Half of one percent works too: 0.5% of 250 is 1.25.

Adding a charge

A 7.5% fee on 1,500 is 112.5, so the total is 1,500 + 112.5 = 1,612.5. Use the Tax Calculator if you want the total shown for you.

Related percentage questions, worked by hand

This tool finds a percentage of a value. Three other everyday questions use the same ideas:

  • What percentage is A of B? Divide A by B and multiply by 100. For 36 out of 240, (36 ÷ 240) × 100 = 15%. The Grade Calculator does this for marks.
  • Percentage change. Subtract the old value from the new one, divide by the old value and multiply by 100. A rise from 80 to 100 is (20 ÷ 80) × 100 = 25%. Divide by the original, not the new value, otherwise the answer is wrong.
  • Increase or reduce by a percentage. Multiply by 1 plus or minus the rate. Increasing 200 by 10% is 200 × 1.10 = 220; reducing it by 10% is 200 × 0.90 = 180. The Discount Calculator handles the reduction case.

Percentages from the ground up

The word percent comes from the Latin per centum, meaning “by the hundred”. A percentage is simply a way of writing a fraction whose denominator is 100, which makes very different quantities easy to compare. “Three out of four students passed” and “thirty-six out of forty-eight students passed” sound different, but both are 75%. That single idea explains almost everything on this page: converting between fractions, decimals and percentages, finding parts of a whole, and measuring change.

The same number in three costumes

  • 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%
  • 1/4 = 0.25 = 25% and 3/4 = 0.75 = 75%
  • 1/5 = 0.2 = 20%, 1/10 = 0.1 = 10% and 1/20 = 0.05 = 5%
  • 1/3 = 0.3333… ≈ 33.3% and 2/3 ≈ 66.7%
  • 1/8 = 0.125 = 12.5%
  • 1 = 100%, and more than one whole is more than 100%, such as 1.5 = 150%

To change a decimal into a percentage, multiply by 100 (move the decimal point two places to the right). To change a percentage into a decimal, divide by 100. A percentage into a fraction: put it over 100 and simplify, so 35% = 35/100 = 7/20.

Mental shortcuts worth knowing

  • 10%: move the decimal point one place to the left. 10% of 240 is 24.
  • 1%: move it two places. 1% of 240 is 2.4.
  • 5%: half of 10%. 5% of 240 is 12.
  • 15%: 10% plus 5%. 24 + 12 = 36.
  • 25%: a quarter. 50% is half, 75% is three quarters.
  • 20%: double 10%. 20% of 240 is 48.

Build up any percentage from these blocks. For 35% of 2,400: 10% is 240, so 30% is 720; 5% is 120; the total is 840. This is usually faster than typing, and it gives you an answer to check the calculator against.

Three questions hidden behind the word “percent”

Most percentage problems are one of three types, and confusing them is the commonest source of mistakes. The calculator on this page answers the first type. The other two are easy by hand, and you can confirm them with the calculator.

Type 1: what is p% of N?

answer = N × p ÷ 100

This is the calculator's job. 15% of 240 = 240 × 15 ÷ 100 = 36.

Type 2: what percentage is A of B?

percentage = (A ÷ B) × 100

Divide the part by the whole. 36 out of 240 is (36 ÷ 240) × 100 = 15%. Check: typing 240 and 15 into the calculator returns 36, which confirms that 36 is 15% of 240.

Type 3: p% of an unknown whole is A. What is the whole?

whole = A ÷ (p ÷ 100)

If 45% of a number is 90, the number is 90 ÷ 0.45 = 200. Check: 45% of 200 is 90.

Percentage increase, decrease and change

To measure how much something has changed, compare the change with the starting value, not the ending value:

percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100

A positive answer is an increase and a negative answer a decrease. Alternatively, use a multiplier: increasing by p% multiplies by (1 + p ÷ 100), and decreasing by p% multiplies by (1 − p ÷ 100).

The asymmetry of increases and decreases

A price rises from 250 to 300: the change is 50, and 50 ÷ 250 = 20%, an increase of 20%. If it later falls back from 300 to 250, the change is −50, and −50 ÷ 300 = −16.67%. The same absolute change is a different percentage because the starting value is different.

Up 10% then down 10%

Start with 100. Increase by 10% (10% of 100 is 10) to reach 110. Now decrease by 10% (10% of 110 is 11) to reach 99. You are 1% below where you started, not back at 100. Run the two steps through the calculator to see 10 and then 11.

Reverse percentage

A coat costs 40 after a 20% discount. The sale price is 80% of the original, so the original is 40 ÷ 0.8 = 50. Check: 20% of 50 is 10, and 50 − 10 = 40.

Percentage points versus percent

When a rate moves from 10% to 12%, it has risen by 2 percentage points, but by 20% in relative terms (2 ÷ 10). News reports, election polls and interest-free-to-business comparisons often blur the two. Whenever a statistic is quoted as a percentage of a percentage, ask which one is meant. A pass rate that rises from 80% to 84% is up 4 points and up 5% relatively.

More worked examples

Everyday amounts

20% of 150 is 30. 17.5% of 80 is 14. 150% of 40 is 60, because 150% is one and a half times the number. 7.5% of 640 is 48.

Small percentages of large numbers

0.75% of 2,400 is 2,400 × 0.0075 = 18. This pattern appears in small service charges and in tolerances: a part that is allowed to vary by 0.5% of 250 mm may differ by 1.25 mm.

Marks on a test

A test is out of 150 marks. A score of 72% means 0.72 × 150 = 108 marks.

Population growth

A town of 2,500 people grows by 4% in a year: 4% of 2,500 is 100, so the new population is 2,600. Two years of 4% growth is 2,500 × 1.04 × 1.04 = 2,704, which is 204 more than the start, more than twice 100 because the second year's growth is on a bigger base.

Adding a service charge

A bill of 1,500 with a 7.5% charge: 7.5% of 1,500 is 112.5, so the total is 1,612.5. The Tax Calculator and Tip Calculator present results like this with the total shown.

A multi-step case study: buying a jacket

A jacket is listed at 800. It is 25% off, and a 10% sales tax is added to the discounted price. What does it cost?

  1. Discount: 25% of 800 is 200, so the sale price is 800 − 200 = 600.
  2. Tax: 10% of 600 is 60.
  3. Final price: 600 + 60 = 660.

Notice that the order of operations did not matter for the total in this particular case: the same result comes from multiplying 800 × 0.75 × 1.10 = 660. Percentages applied as multipliers commute. What changes the answer is whether the second percentage is applied to the original or to the reduced amount. If tax were added on the original 800 (80) and the 25% discount applied to the total of 880, you would pay 660 again here only because both are multipliers; but if the two percentages were added together (+10% − 25% = −15%) you would pay 680, which is wrong. Always apply successive percentages one after another.

Practice questions with answers

  1. What is 30% of 450? 450 × 0.30 = 135.
  2. 18 is what percentage of 72? 18 ÷ 72 = 0.25, so 25%.
  3. A shirt costs 40 after a 20% discount. What was the original price? 40 ÷ 0.8 = 50.
  4. A price rises from 250 to 300. What is the percentage increase? 50 ÷ 250 = 20%.
  5. It falls back from 300 to 250. What is the percentage decrease? 50 ÷ 300 = 16.67%.
  6. 45% of a number is 90. What is the number? 90 ÷ 0.45 = 200.
  7. A student scores 72% on a 150-mark paper. How many marks is that? 108.
  8. A population of 2,500 grows 4%. How many people are added? 4% of 2,500 = 100.

Percentages in real life

  • Shopping. Sales, vouchers and loyalty offers are all percentages of a price. Comparing “30% off” with “buy two, get one free” means converting both to a common unit, here a saving of 30% versus 33%.
  • Health and nutrition labels. Labels often show the percentage of a reference daily amount supplied by one serving. They are guides, and the reference values are based on a typical adult.
  • Sport. A basketball player making 36 of 48 shots has a shooting percentage of 75%. Percentages let you compare players with different numbers of attempts.
  • Weather forecasts. A 30% chance of rain means that, in similar conditions, rain occurs on roughly three out of ten occasions, not that it rains for 30% of the day.
  • Exams. Weighted marks, pass marks and grade boundaries are all expressed as percentages, so the Grade Calculator and Final Grade Calculator build directly on this page.

Key terms

  • Percent (%): parts per hundred.
  • Percentage point: the arithmetic difference between two percentages.
  • Base (or whole): the amount you take the percentage of.
  • Multiplier: the number you multiply by to apply a percentage change, such as 1.25 for +25%.
  • Percentage change: the difference between new and old values divided by the old value.
  • Reverse percentage: finding the original value from a value after a percentage change.

Limits of this calculator

  • It finds a percentage of a number only. For percentage change and reverse percentages, use the formulas above, and use the calculator to check.
  • It rounds the displayed answer for readability, so very long decimals are shortened.
  • It assumes you enter the percentage as a number: type 15 for 15%, not 0.15.
  • It cannot judge whether a percentage is meaningful. Percentages of tiny samples, such as 50% of 2 people, say little.

A short history of the percent

The idea of reckoning “per hundred” is old. In ancient Rome, taxes and fees were often fixed as fractions such as one hundredth, and the Latin phrase per centum eventually shortened into the symbol we use today. Merchants in medieval Europe used percentages to compare prices, profits and exchange rates between places that used different weights and currencies. By the 1400s and 1500s, printed arithmetic books routinely taught percentage calculations because trade depended on them. The modern symbol % took shape gradually from handwritten abbreviations of “per cento”. The reason percentages lasted is practical: a common denominator of 100 makes comparisons immediate.

Rounding and precision with percentages

  • Round at the end. If you round 16.666…% to 17% and then use that figure in a further calculation, the error compounds. Keep a few extra digits until the final line.
  • Match the precision of the data. If prices are quoted to the nearest cent, answers to the cent are enough. Five decimal places suggest a precision that does not exist.
  • Beware of rounding that changes the total. Percentages in a table rounded to whole numbers can add up to 99% or 101%. This is normal and is usually noted as “figures may not add to 100 due to rounding”.
  • Small numbers. A rise of 1 person in a group of 4 is a 25% increase, which sounds large. Always quote the count with the percentage when the base is small.

A second case study: comparing two offers

A shop offers a 20% discount on a 450 item, plus a loyalty voucher of 15% off the reduced price. A rival shop offers 32% off the same item. Which is cheaper?

  1. First shop, step one. 20% of 450 is 90, so the price becomes 360.
  2. First shop, step two. 15% of 360 is 54, so the price becomes 306.
  3. Second shop. 32% of 450 is 144, so the price is 306.
  4. Comparison. Both end at exactly 306. The combined discount of the first shop is 1 − 0.80 × 0.85 = 0.32, which is 32%, not 35%, even though 20 + 15 = 35.

This is the standard trap with stacked discounts: successive percentages multiply, they do not add. Check each step with the calculator: 20% of 450 gives 90, and 15% of 360 gives 54.

More practice questions

  1. What is 12% of 250? 30.
  2. A salary of 40,000 rises by 5%. What is the new salary? 5% is 2,000, so 42,000.
  3. A bill of 64 has a 12.5% service charge. How much? 8, so the total is 72.
  4. The population falls from 8,000 to 7,200. Percentage decrease? 800 ÷ 8,000 = 10%.
  5. A test has 40 questions and you answered 85% correctly. How many correct? 34.
  6. A recipe uses 250 g of flour. You scale it up by 40%. How much flour? 350 g.

Common mistakes

  • Typing 0.15 instead of 15. The field expects a percentage, so enter 15. Entering 0.15 returns 0.15% of the number.
  • Adding and then subtracting the same percentage. Increasing 100 by 20% gives 120, but reducing 120 by 20% gives 96, not 100, because the second percentage applies to a larger number.
  • Confusing percentage points with percent. Moving from 10% to 12% is a rise of 2 percentage points but a 20% increase.
  • Using the wrong base. A 25% discount applies to the original price, not to a price that already includes tax.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate 10% quickly?

Divide the number by 10. Then 5% is half of that and 1% is one tenth of it, so 15% of 240 is 24 + 12 = 36.

Can a percentage be greater than 100?

Yes. 150% of 40 is 60. It simply means more than the whole.

How do I find the original number before a percentage was added?

Divide the final amount by 1 plus the rate. If 1,612.5 includes 7.5%, the original is 1,612.5 ÷ 1.075 = 1,500.

Is there a limit on decimal places?

You can enter decimals freely. The answer is rounded for display only.

How do I turn a fraction into a percentage?

Divide the numerator by the denominator and multiply by 100. For 3/8, 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375, which is 37.5%.

How do I calculate percentage increase?

Subtract the old value from the new one, divide by the old value and multiply by 100. From 80 to 100 that is 20 ÷ 80 × 100 = 25%.

Why is a 50% increase followed by a 50% decrease not back to the start?

The decrease is taken from the larger amount. Starting at 100, +50% gives 150 and −50% gives 75.

How do I find the original price before a discount?

Divide the sale price by one minus the discount rate as a decimal. For 60 after 25% off, 60 ÷ 0.75 = 80.

What is the difference between 5% and 5 percentage points?

A 5% change is relative to the starting value. A change of 5 percentage points is the arithmetic difference between two percentages, such as 20% to 25%.

Can I calculate a percentage of a percentage?

Yes. Multiply the two as decimals. 50% of 40% is 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2, which is 20%.

How do I work out VAT or sales tax inside a price?

If the price includes tax at rate r, the tax-free amount is price ÷ (1 + r). For a total of 258 including 7.5%, the pre-tax price is 258 ÷ 1.075 = 240.

How do I find 15% quickly?

Take 10% (shift the decimal point), then add half of that. For 240: 24 + 12 = 36.

What does “per cent” mean in a probability?

A probability of 30% means 30 chances in 100. It is a measure of likelihood, not a guarantee.

Why is my answer different from my teacher's?

Check whether the percentage was applied to the original value or to a changed value, and whether rounding was done at the end rather than part-way.

Is percent always out of 100?

Yes, by definition. Percentages above 100 mean more than the whole and percentages below 1 are fractions of a hundredth.

How do I explain a percentage increase of more than 100%?

It means the value more than doubled. A rise from 20 to 50 is an increase of 150%, because the change of 30 is 1.5 times the original 20.

How do I calculate a percentage with a calculator that has no % key?

Divide the percentage by 100 and multiply by the number, or use this tool.

Why do two discounts of 20% and 15% give 32% and not 35%?

The second discount is applied to an already reduced price, so it removes less money than 15% of the original.

How do I find a percentage of a time, such as 25% of an hour?

Convert the time to minutes (60), then take 25%, which is 15 minutes.

Can percentages be negative?

Yes, as a change. A change of −8% means a decrease of 8%. A share of a whole is not negative.

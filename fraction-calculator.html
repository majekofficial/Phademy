Adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing fractions

Enter two fractions in the form a/b, choose an operation and the calculator returns the answer in its simplest form. It is meant for checking your own working and for seeing how a result arises, rather than just handing over a number.

The four methods

  • Add or subtract: rewrite both fractions over a common denominator, then add or subtract the numerators. 1/2 + 1/3 = 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6.
  • Multiply: multiply numerators together and denominators together, then simplify. 4/6 × 1/3 = 4/18 = 2/9.
  • Divide: multiply by the reciprocal of the second fraction. 3/4 ÷ 2/5 = 3/4 × 5/2 = 15/8.

a/b + c/d = (ad + bc) ÷ bd | a/b × c/d = ac ÷ bd | a/b ÷ c/d = ad ÷ bc

Worked examples

Subtracting with a common denominator

5/6 − 1/4. The lowest common denominator is 12, so the sum is 10/12 − 3/12 = 7/12.

A negative fraction

−1/2 − 3/4 = −2/4 − 3/4 = −5/4. Negative fractions are accepted with a minus sign in front of the numerator.

A result that simplifies to a whole number

2/3 ÷ 2/3 = 1. Any non-zero value divided by itself is 1, and the calculator shows it as a whole number.

Mixing whole numbers and fractions

Whole numbers must be written as fractions. Enter 3 as 3/1: 3/1 + 1/4 = 12/4 + 1/4 = 13/4.

Input rules

  • Use the form a/b with whole-number parts. Mixed numbers such as 2 1/2 and decimals such as 0.75 are not accepted, and the result shows a dash.
  • To enter a mixed number, convert it first: 2 1/2 is (2 × 2 + 1)/2 = 5/2.
  • The denominator cannot be zero. A fraction with a zero denominator is undefined.
  • Answers are automatically simplified, so you may see a different fraction from the one you worked out if you did not reduce yours.

Common mistakes

  • Adding numerators and denominators together, as in 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5. Fractions need a common denominator first.
  • Flipping the wrong fraction when dividing. Only the second fraction is inverted.
  • Not simplifying. 4/18 and 2/9 are equal, but exam answers usually expect the simplest form.

What a fraction represents

A fraction describes a number of equal parts of a whole. The numerator is the number of parts being considered, and the denominator tells you how many equal parts make one whole. In 3/4, the denominator 4 divides the whole into four equal parts and the numerator 3 selects three of them. The denominator cannot be zero because division by zero is undefined.

Finding a common denominator

Addition and subtraction require fractions to refer to parts of the same size. The simplest route is often to find the lowest common denominator, which is the least common multiple of the two denominators. For 1/6 and 1/4, the lowest common denominator is 12. Convert 1/6 to 2/12 and 1/4 to 3/12 before adding or subtracting.

Worked addition example

2/3 + 3/5. A common denominator is 15. Convert 2/3 to 10/15 and 3/5 to 9/15. Add the numerators: 10/15 + 9/15 = 19/15. This is an improper fraction because the numerator is greater than the denominator. It is also 1 4/15 as a mixed number, although the calculator displays the simplified improper fraction.

Worked subtraction example

7/8 − 1/6. The lowest common denominator is 24. Convert the fractions to 21/24 and 4/24. Subtract: 21/24 − 4/24 = 17/24. Since 17 and 24 have no common factor greater than 1, the answer is already in its simplest form.

Multiplying and dividing fractions

Multiplication is usually more direct than addition. Multiply the numerators together and multiply the denominators together, then reduce the result. For example, 2/7 × 3/4 = 6/28 = 3/14. You do not need to find a common denominator for multiplication.

Division uses the reciprocal of the second fraction. The reciprocal swaps the numerator and denominator. For example, 5/6 ÷ 2/3 becomes 5/6 × 3/2 = 15/12 = 5/4. The second fraction must not be zero, because dividing by zero is undefined. The calculator reports an error if the divisor is zero.

Simplifying a fraction

A fraction is in simplest form when its numerator and denominator have no common positive factor other than 1. To simplify 18/24, find the greatest common divisor, which is 6. Divide both parts by 6: 18 ÷ 6 = 3 and 24 ÷ 6 = 4. The simplified fraction is 3/4. Simplifying does not change the value; it writes the same number with smaller whole-number parts.

Negative fractions

A negative sign can be written before the fraction or in the numerator. For example, −2/5 and (−2)/5 represent the same value. The calculator accepts a minus sign in front of the numerator. Keep the sign attached to the number when converting or simplifying, and remember that a negative multiplied by a negative gives a positive result.

Input format and supported operations

Enter each value in the form a/b, such as 3/4 or -2/5. The calculator expects a numerator and denominator separated by a slash. It does not accept a decimal such as 0.75 or a mixed number such as 2 1/2 directly. Convert those forms first: 0.75 is 3/4, and 2 1/2 is (2 × 2 + 1)/2 = 5/2. A whole number can be entered as 5/1.

The tool calculates addition, subtraction, multiplication and division for two fractions. It reduces the final answer and displays a whole number when the denominator reduces to 1. It is designed for these two-input operations, not for solving algebraic equations or automatically interpreting long expressions with several operations and brackets.

Case study: scaling a recipe

A recipe uses 3/4 cup of flour, but you want to prepare half the quantity. Multiply 3/4 by 1/2. The calculation is 3/8 cup. If the recipe instead calls for 2/3 cup and you want to triple it, multiply 2/3 by 3/1 to get 6/3, which simplifies to 2 cups. Fraction arithmetic makes scaling quantities precise, although real kitchen measurements may still need practical rounding to the available measuring tools.

Practice questions

  1. Calculate 1/4 + 1/2. Answer: 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4.
  2. Calculate 5/6 − 1/3. Answer: 5/6 − 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2.
  3. Calculate 3/5 × 10/9. Answer: 30/45 = 2/3.
  4. Calculate 4/7 ÷ 2/3. Answer: 4/7 × 3/2 = 12/14 = 6/7.

Common mistakes and limitations

  • Adding denominators: 1/2 + 1/3 is not 2/5. Convert to a common denominator first.
  • Inverting the first fraction during division: keep the first fraction and invert only the second.
  • Forgetting to simplify: 6/8 and 3/4 are equal, but 3/4 is simpler.
  • Entering a mixed number directly: convert it to an improper fraction before entering it.
  • Using a zero denominator: fractions with a zero denominator are undefined.

More fraction questions

Can I enter 0.5 instead of 1/2?

No. Enter fractions using a/b notation. Convert 0.5 to 1/2 first.

Can I enter a whole number?

Write it over 1. For example, enter 4 as 4/1.

Why is my answer an improper fraction?

An improper fraction has a numerator larger than its denominator. It is a valid exact result; for example, 7/4 equals 1 3/4.

Can I divide by a fraction that equals zero?

No. Division by zero is undefined, even if the zero is written as 0/5 or another equivalent fraction.

Equivalent fractions and comparing values

Equivalent fractions have different numerators and denominators but represent the same number. Multiplying or dividing the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number produces an equivalent fraction. For example, 2/3 is equivalent to 4/6 and 6/9. This idea is the foundation of finding common denominators for addition and subtraction.

Comparing two fractions

To compare 3/4 and 5/8, rewrite 3/4 as 6/8. Since 6/8 is greater than 5/8, 3/4 is greater than 5/8. Another method is cross-multiplication when denominators are positive: compare 3 × 8 with 5 × 4. The products are 24 and 20, so 3/4 is larger. This calculator performs arithmetic operations on two fractions; it is not a dedicated comparison tool, but the same reasoning helps you check your work.

Finding a fraction of an amount

To find a fraction of a quantity, multiply the quantity by the fraction. For example, 3/5 of 40 is 3/5 × 40/1 = 120/5 = 24. This is useful for splitting quantities, calculating portions and interpreting ratios. If the quantity is money or a physical measurement, consider whether the final result should be rounded to the smallest practical unit.

Fractions, decimals and percentages

A fraction can be converted to a decimal by dividing its numerator by its denominator. To convert a fraction to a percentage, multiply that decimal by 100. For example, 3/8 = 0.375 = 37.5%. Some fractions produce terminating decimals, while others repeat. The fraction form can be more exact than a rounded decimal, so keep the fraction when exactness matters.

Order of operations still matters

When a calculation contains several operations, follow the conventional order of operations and use brackets to make grouping clear. This two-fraction calculator asks you to choose one operation between two inputs. It does not parse a full expression such as 1/2 + 3/4 × 2/5. Break a longer expression into steps or use an appropriate algebra or scientific tool, checking each intermediate result carefully.

Why fractions are useful beyond school exercises

Fractions appear in proportions, probability, recipes, time, construction and financial splits. One quarter of an hour is 15 minutes; three quarters of a metre is 75 centimetres; and a probability of 1/6 describes one equally likely outcome among six. In each case, the denominator describes the number of equal parts and the numerator tells how many parts are being considered. Keeping the fractional form can make these relationships clearer than converting everything to rounded decimals.

How to check whether a fraction answer is reasonable

Estimate the size of the answer before accepting it. If you add two positive fractions, the result should be larger than either addend. If you multiply two positive proper fractions, the result should be smaller than either factor. If you divide a positive number by a proper fraction, the result should be larger than the starting number. These checks do not replace exact working, but they help reveal a sign error, a reversed reciprocal or a mistaken denominator before you submit an answer.

How the denominator affects the size of a part

When the numerator is fixed and positive, increasing the denominator makes each part smaller. For example, 1/8 is smaller than 1/4 because dividing the same whole into eight equal parts creates smaller pieces than dividing it into four. This can help you estimate whether an answer is reasonable before simplifying it. When both numerator and denominator change, compare equivalent forms or use a common denominator rather than judging by the size of the digits alone.

Keep exact values when possible

When a fraction has a repeating decimal equivalent, the fraction is often the clearest exact form. For example, 1/3 is exactly one third, while 0.333 is only an approximation. If you round too early and then use that rounded decimal in later steps, small errors can accumulate. Keep the simplified fraction through the working and convert to a decimal only when the question or practical situation requires it.

For homework, show the equivalent fractions and the reduction step, not only the final answer. Clear working makes it easier to locate a mistake and earn method marks.

Frequently asked questions

Can I enter a mixed number?

Not directly. Convert it to an improper fraction first, for example 2 1/2 to 5/2.

Does it simplify automatically?

Yes. The result is shown in lowest terms.

How do I divide fractions?

Multiply the first by the reciprocal of the second, which is what the calculator does internally.

Why do I get a dash?

The input is not in the a/b form, or a denominator is zero.

Momentum, impulse and collisions

Momentum describes how hard it is to stop a moving object: it grows with both mass and velocity. This calculator handles three situations: the momentum of a single object, the impulse of a force acting for some time, and a collision in which total momentum is conserved.

The ideas and formulas

momentum p = m × v

impulse = F × t = m(v − u) = change in momentum

conservation: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

Momentum is a vector, so direction matters. Choose one direction as positive, and give velocities in the opposite direction a negative sign. In an isolated collision, the total momentum before equals the total momentum after.

Worked examples

Momentum of one object

A 2 kg trolley moving at 3 m/s has p = 2 × 3 = 6 kg m/s. If you know p = 6 kg m/s and m = 2 kg, the velocity is 6 ÷ 2 = 3 m/s.

A force acting over time

A 0.15 kg ball arrives at 25 m/s and leaves in the opposite direction at 20 m/s, in contact for 0.02 s. The change in momentum is 0.15 × (−20 − 25) = −6.75 kg m/s. The force is F = −6.75 ÷ 0.02 = −337.5 N. The negative sign shows the force opposes the original direction of motion.

A collision

A 2 kg trolley at 3 m/s hits a 1 kg trolley at rest, and the second trolley leaves at 4 m/s. The initial momentum is 2 × 3 + 0 = 6. Afterwards, 2v₁ + 1 × 4 = 6, so v₁ = 1 m/s. Enter the masses, the two initial velocities and the final velocity of the second object.

Using impulse in real life

Impulse explains why airbags, crumple zones and bending your knees when you land all work. The change in momentum is fixed, so spreading it over a longer time reduces the average force: F = Δp ÷ t. Doubling the stopping time halves the force.

What the collision mode assumes

  • It uses conservation of momentum only. It does not decide whether kinetic energy is conserved, so it does not tell you whether the collision is elastic or inelastic.
  • All motion is along one straight line.
  • No external force, such as friction, acts during the collision.

Common mistakes

  • Ignoring direction. A ball bouncing back reverses its velocity, which doubles the change in momentum rather than cancelling it.
  • Using grams instead of kilograms.
  • Forgetting that momentum, unlike kinetic energy, can be negative.
  • Mixing up u (before) and v (after).

Frequently asked questions

What is the unit of momentum?

kilogram metres per second (kg m/s), which is the same as newton seconds (N s).

Is momentum always conserved?

In a closed system with no external forces, yes. In everyday settings, friction and other forces may change it.

What is the difference between impulse and momentum?

Impulse is the change in momentum caused by a force acting for a time.

Why can a force be negative?

The sign shows its direction. A negative force acts opposite to the direction you chose as positive.

Momentum is a vector quantity

Linear momentum is the product of mass and velocity: p = mv. Mass is measured in kilograms and velocity in metres per second, so momentum is measured in kg m/s. Momentum has direction because velocity has direction. A 2 kg trolley moving right at 3 m/s has momentum +6 kg m/s if right is defined as positive. The same trolley moving left at 3 m/s has momentum −6 kg m/s under that convention.

This is why the sign of velocity matters in collision calculations. Do not replace a negative velocity with a positive speed unless the direction is genuinely irrelevant to the question. In a one-dimensional collision, choose one direction as positive and use that convention for every object's initial and final velocity. A consistent sign convention makes conservation of momentum work correctly.

Impulse links force to a change in momentum

Impulse is the product of a force and the time interval over which it acts when the force is constant: J = Ft. It is also equal to the change in momentum, Δp = m(v − u), for an object of constant mass. The unit of impulse is newton-second (N s), which is equivalent to kg m/s. A force acting for a longer time can produce a larger change in momentum if its magnitude remains the same.

For a varying force, impulse is the area under a force-time graph rather than simply one force multiplied by the full time. The calculator's simple formula mode uses the constant-force relationship. If a question supplies a changing force graph, find the signed area under the graph or use the method taught in your course.

Worked example: stopping a moving object

A 1,200 kg car moves at 20 m/s and comes to rest in 5 seconds. Its initial momentum is 1,200 × 20 = 24,000 kg m/s. Its final momentum is zero, so the change in momentum is 0 − 24,000 = −24,000 kg m/s. The average force over the interval is Δp/t = −24,000/5 = −4,800 N. The negative sign means the net force acts opposite the original direction of travel.

The result is an average force for the stated stopping interval. It does not mean the force was exactly 4,800 N at every instant. In a real braking event, force can vary with tyre grip, road surface, brake pressure and vehicle dynamics. The simple impulse calculation gives the overall change in momentum per unit time.

Conservation of momentum in a collision

In an isolated system, total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum after it. For two objects moving along one straight line, m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂. The symbols u represent initial velocities and v represent final velocities. The calculator's collision mode uses this relationship to find one unknown when the other quantities are supplied. It assumes external impulse on the system is negligible during the collision.

Conservation applies to the total system, not necessarily to each object separately. One object may lose momentum while the other gains it. The sum remains constant when external forces have no significant net impulse over the short collision interval. If an object is attached to a wall or receives a substantial external push during the collision, the two-object system may not be isolated.

Elastic and inelastic collisions

Momentum is conserved in both elastic and inelastic collisions when the system is isolated. Kinetic energy behaves differently. In an elastic collision, total kinetic energy is also conserved. In an inelastic collision, some kinetic energy is transformed into sound, heat, deformation or other forms. In a perfectly inelastic collision, the objects stick together and share one final velocity.

The calculator's conservation equation does not by itself classify the collision or guarantee that kinetic energy is conserved. If the question says objects stick together, their final velocities are equal and that extra condition can be used. If the question says the collision is elastic, a kinetic-energy condition may also be needed. Do not assume that every collision preserves kinetic energy simply because momentum is conserved.

Worked example: two objects stick together

A 2 kg trolley moving at 4 m/s collides with a stationary 3 kg trolley. They stick together. Before the collision, total momentum is 2 × 4 + 3 × 0 = 8 kg m/s. Their combined mass after collision is 5 kg. Let their shared final velocity be v. Conservation gives 8 = 5v, so v = 1.6 m/s in the original direction of the moving trolley.

The combined trolley moves more slowly because the same total momentum is now shared by a larger mass. Kinetic energy is not conserved in this perfectly inelastic collision: some is transferred to sound, heat and deformation. Momentum conservation and kinetic-energy conservation are separate ideas.

Why airbags and padded surfaces reduce impact force

For a given change in momentum, increasing the time over which the change occurs reduces the average force. This is the principle behind airbags, crumple zones, helmets and padded landing surfaces. An airbag increases the time over which a passenger is brought to rest and spreads the contact force over a larger area. The change in momentum may be similar, but the average force can be reduced.

This does not mean the passenger experiences no force or that every injury is prevented. It explains one important design principle. Safety systems also manage direction, contact area, restraint and the way energy is absorbed. The impulse relationship is a useful way to understand why extending stopping time can reduce average force.

Momentum compared with kinetic energy

Momentum and kinetic energy both depend on mass and velocity, but they are not interchangeable. Momentum is mv and includes direction. Translational kinetic energy is ½mv² and is a scalar, so it has no positive or negative direction. Doubling velocity doubles momentum but quadruples kinetic energy. Two objects can have equal momentum but different kinetic energies if their masses differ.

For example, a 2 kg object moving at 5 m/s has momentum 10 kg m/s and kinetic energy 25 J. A 5 kg object moving at 2 m/s also has momentum 10 kg m/s, but its kinetic energy is 10 J. Use momentum for collision balance and impulse questions; use kinetic energy for energy-of-motion questions.

Practice questions with answers

  1. A 0.5 kg ball moves at 8 m/s. Find its momentum. Answer: p = 0.5 × 8 = 4 kg m/s.
  2. A 10 N force acts for 0.4 s. Find the impulse. Answer: J = 10 × 0.4 = 4 N s.
  3. A 1 kg object moving at 6 m/s hits a stationary 2 kg object and they stick together. Find their final velocity. Answer: v = (1 × 6 + 2 × 0)/3 = 2 m/s.

Common sign and unit checks

Use kilograms rather than grams, metres per second rather than kilometres per hour, newtons rather than kilonewtons unless converted, and seconds rather than minutes. Convert 72 km/h to 20 m/s by dividing by 3.6. If an object moves in the opposite direction to the selected positive direction, enter a negative velocity. Check that the final momentum has the same total as the initial momentum in a collision calculation.

Choosing the system before applying conservation

Momentum conservation is applied to a defined system of objects. For a collision between two trolleys, the system may include both trolleys. Forces that the trolleys exert on each other are internal to that system and occur in equal and opposite pairs. External forces, such as friction from the track, can change the system's total momentum. The approximation is most useful when the collision happens over a short time and the external impulse is small compared with the collision forces.

This explains why a collision can conserve momentum even though each trolley experiences a large force. The forces between the two objects change their individual momenta, but the changes are opposite and the total remains constant. If the track has significant friction over a long interval, the total momentum of the trolley pair may not remain constant over that longer interval.

Impulse on a force-time graph

If force is constant, impulse is the area of a rectangle on a force-time graph: force multiplied by duration. If force rises and falls, the graph may form a triangle, trapezium or curve. The signed area gives the impulse. Areas above the time axis are positive under the selected sign convention, and areas below are negative. The net impulse is the sum of these signed areas.

For a triangular force pulse with a peak of 100 N lasting 0.2 s, the impulse is one half × 100 × 0.2 = 10 N s. This is the change in momentum. The peak force alone is not enough to determine impulse; the duration and shape of the force-time graph also matter. Use the calculator's constant-force mode only when the problem provides a constant or average force for the interval.

Voltage, current, resistance, power and energy in a circuit

Ohm's law links the three basic quantities of an electric circuit, and a handful of related formulas turn them into power, charge and energy. Choose a formula, choose what to find and enter the other values. The calculator also finds the combined resistance of two resistors in series or in parallel.

The formulas

  • Ohm's law: V = I × R. Voltage in volts, current in amperes, resistance in ohms.
  • Electrical power: P = V × I = I² × R = V² ÷ R, in watts.
  • Charge: Q = I × t, in coulombs.
  • Electrical energy: E = V × I × t, in joules.
  • Resistors in series: R = R₁ + R₂.
  • Resistors in parallel: 1 ÷ R = 1 ÷ R₁ + 1 ÷ R₂.

Worked examples

Ohm's law

A 4 Ω resistor carrying 3 A has a voltage of 3 × 4 = 12 V. With 12 V across it, the current is 12 ÷ 4 = 3 A. A 12 V supply driving 3 A means a resistance of 12 ÷ 3 = 4 Ω.

Three ways to get the same power

For the same circuit, P = VI = 12 × 3 = 36 W. Using the current and resistance, P = I²R = 9 × 4 = 36 W. Using the voltage and resistance, P = V² ÷ R = 144 ÷ 4 = 36 W. Pick the formula that matches the two values you know.

Charge and energy

A 3 A current flowing for 10 s moves 3 × 10 = 30 C of charge. A 12 V supply delivering 3 A for 60 s transfers E = 12 × 3 × 60 = 2,160 J.

Resistors in series and parallel

A 4 Ω and a 6 Ω resistor in series total 10 Ω. In parallel, 1 ÷ R = 1/4 + 1/6 = 5/12, so R = 2.4 Ω. Two equal resistors of 10 Ω in parallel give 5 Ω, half of either one. Parallel resistance is always less than the smallest resistor.

Practical points

  • Household appliances: a 2,000 W heater running for 3 hours uses 2 kW × 3 h = 6 kWh of energy, which is how electricity is billed.
  • Why parallel circuits are used at home: each appliance gets the full supply voltage, and one failing does not stop the others.
  • Safety: a higher current in a wire generates more heat (P = I²R), which is why fuses limit the current.

Common mistakes

  • Using the wrong power formula for the quantities you have.
  • Forgetting to convert milliamps to amps, or kilohms to ohms.
  • Adding resistors in parallel as if they were in series.
  • Treating Ohm's law as true for all components. It holds for ohmic conductors, such as resistors at constant temperature, but not for filament lamps or diodes.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between voltage and current?

Voltage is the energy given to each coulomb of charge, like pressure. Current is the rate at which charge flows.

Why is the parallel resistance smaller than either resistor?

Each extra path gives the current another route, so the overall resistance drops.

How do I find the power of an appliance?

Multiply its voltage by its current, or read it from the label.

How can I choose between the power formulas?

Use the one that contains the two values you already know. They all give the same result.

Voltage, current and resistance in a simple circuit

Voltage is the electrical potential difference between two points. It is measured in volts and describes the energy transferred per unit charge. Current is the rate at which electric charge flows, measured in amperes. Resistance describes how strongly a component opposes current, measured in ohms. Ohm's law connects these quantities for an ohmic conductor under suitable physical conditions: V = IR.

A 12 V supply connected across a 4 Ω resistor produces a current of 3 A in the simple Ohmic model. The relationship can be rearranged to find any one of the three quantities when the other two are known: V = IR, I = V/R and R = V/I. The calculator lets you choose the unknown rather than rearranging the equation by hand.

When Ohm's law applies

Ohm's law is a model for components whose resistance remains approximately constant under the conditions being considered. A fixed resistor at a stable temperature is a common example. Some components are non-ohmic: their current-voltage relationship is not a straight line, or their resistance changes with temperature, light or applied voltage. A filament lamp, diode and thermistor are examples where a single constant resistance may not describe the full behaviour.

For a non-ohmic component, V/I at a particular operating point can still be calculated as a ratio, but that does not mean the component obeys Ohm's law across all operating conditions. If a question specifically concerns a diode characteristic or a temperature-dependent resistor, use the model and graph supplied by the question rather than assuming a fixed resistance.

Electrical power: three equivalent formulas

Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred. The basic relationship is P = VI. Combining it with Ohm's law gives P = I²R and P = V²/R. These expressions are equivalent when the same component follows Ohm's law and the voltage, current and resistance refer to that component. Choose the version that uses the values you know.

For example, a 12 V supply across a 4 Ω resistor gives I = 3 A. Power can be found as VI = 12 × 3 = 36 W, as I²R = 3² × 4 = 36 W, or as V²/R = 12²/4 = 36 W. If your three calculations do not agree, check whether you used the same component values and units in each formula.

Charge and current over time

Current is the rate of flow of charge. For a constant current, Q = It, where Q is charge in coulombs, I is current in amperes and t is time in seconds. A current of 2 A flowing for 5 s transfers 10 C of charge. One ampere is one coulomb per second. This relationship connects circuit current with the amount of charge that passes a point during a time interval.

If current changes with time, total charge is found from the area under a current-time graph rather than one constant current multiplied by the entire interval. The calculator's charge mode uses the constant-current relationship. Convert minutes to seconds before entering time: 3 minutes is 180 seconds, not 3 seconds.

Electrical energy and appliance use

For a constant voltage and current, electrical energy transferred is E = VIt. Since P = VI, the same relationship can be written E = Pt. Energy is measured in joules when power is in watts and time is in seconds. A 100 W device operating for 10 seconds transfers 1,000 J of energy under the stated constant-power assumption.

Household electricity bills commonly use kilowatt-hours rather than joules. One kilowatt-hour is the energy transferred by a 1 kW device operating for one hour, equal to 3.6 million joules. The calculator's energy formula uses SI values and returns joules. To estimate kilowatt-hours from a power rating, convert watts to kilowatts and seconds to hours, then multiply power by time.

Resistors connected in series

In a series circuit, components are connected along one path, so the same current passes through each resistor. For two ideal resistors, total resistance is R = R₁ + R₂. If 4 Ω and 6 Ω resistors are connected in series, the equivalent resistance is 10 Ω. Adding another resistor in series increases the total resistance and reduces the current for the same supply voltage.

The supply voltage is shared between series components according to their resistances. The voltage drop across a resistor is I × R. A larger resistance has a larger voltage drop when the same current flows through all series components. The calculator's series mode finds the combined resistance of two resistors; a longer chain can be handled by adding each resistance.

Resistors connected in parallel

In a parallel circuit, each resistor is connected across the same two nodes, so the voltage across each branch is the same. The total current divides between the branches. For two resistors, 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂. This can be rearranged to R = (R₁R₂)/(R₁ + R₂). For 6 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel, the equivalent resistance is (6 × 3)/(6 + 3) = 2 Ω.

The equivalent resistance of two positive resistors in parallel is smaller than either individual resistance. Adding another parallel path gives current an additional route, so the total resistance falls. This is why the parallel result is not found by simply adding the resistor values. The calculator's parallel mode uses two resistors; for more branches, add their reciprocals before taking the reciprocal of the sum.

Worked example: choosing the right power equation

A resistor has resistance 8 Ω and carries a current of 0.5 A. The power is P = I²R = 0.5² × 8 = 2 W. The voltage across it is V = IR = 0.5 × 8 = 4 V. Using P = VI gives 4 × 0.5 = 2 W. All three values are consistent. If the question instead supplied voltage and resistance, P = V²/R would be the direct choice.

When choosing a formula, list what is known first. If voltage and current are known, use P = VI. If current and resistance are known, use P = I²R. If voltage and resistance are known, use P = V²/R. This avoids unnecessary rearrangement and reduces the chance of substituting the wrong quantity.

Electrical safety and the limits of a calculation

A correct numerical result does not mean a circuit is safe to build. High voltage, high current, overheating, damaged insulation, incorrect fuses and unsuitable components can cause electric shock, burns or fire. Do not experiment with mains electricity. Use properly rated components and supervised low-voltage educational equipment. The calculator is for learning and estimation, not electrical installation design or a substitute for qualified inspection.

Resistor power ratings matter. A resistor may have the correct resistance but still overheat if the power dissipated exceeds its rating. For a resistor, compare the calculated power with the component's rated power and allow an appropriate safety margin. Real components also have tolerances, temperature limits and maximum voltage ratings that a basic Ohm's law calculation does not check.

Practice questions with answers

  1. A 9 V battery is connected across a 3 Ω resistor. Find the current. Answer: I = 9/3 = 3 A.
  2. A 2 A current flows for 30 s. Find the charge transferred. Answer: Q = 2 × 30 = 60 C.
  3. Two resistors of 5 Ω and 10 Ω are connected in series. Find total resistance. Answer: 5 + 10 = 15 Ω.
  4. Two resistors of 4 Ω and 4 Ω are connected in parallel. Find equivalent resistance. Answer: 1/R = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2, so R = 2 Ω.

Common unit conversions

Use volts (V), amperes (A), ohms (Ω), watts (W), seconds (s), coulombs (C) and joules (J). Convert milliamperes to amperes by dividing by 1,000. Convert kilohms to ohms by multiplying by 1,000. Convert minutes to seconds by multiplying by 60. For electricity use in kilowatt-hours, convert power to kilowatts and time to hours before multiplying. Do not enter 500 mA as 500 A; it is 0.5 A.

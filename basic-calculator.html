A calculator that follows the rules of arithmetic

Type a calculation with the on-screen keypad or your keyboard and the answer updates straight away. The calculator follows the standard order of operations, so you can type a whole expression, with brackets, instead of working it out one step at a time.

Order of operations

Brackets come first, then powers and roots, then multiplication and division from left to right, then addition and subtraction from left to right. This is why the same numbers can give different answers depending on how they are grouped:

  • 18 + 6 × 4 = 42. The multiplication happens first (6 × 4 = 24), then 18 is added.
  • (18 + 6) × 4 = 96. The brackets force the addition first, so it is 24 × 4.

Percent, decimals and fractions

The % key behaves like the one on a phone. After an addition or subtraction it means a percentage of the first number, so 200 + 10% is 220, not 200.1. On its own, 50% is 0.5.

When an answer is a repeating decimal, the calculator also shows the fraction. Entering 1 ÷ 3 gives 0.333333333333 and the fraction 1/3. Because it works with exact values where it can, 0.1 + 0.2 is shown as exactly 0.3 rather than the 0.30000000000000004 that many programming languages produce.

More examples

  • Dividing by zero. 5 ÷ 0 shows “Cannot divide by zero” instead of an error or infinity.
  • Shopping check. (3 × 4.50) + (2 × 1.25) gives the total of three items at 4.50 and two at 1.25.
  • Fuel cost per trip. 240 ÷ 12 × 1.35 is the distance divided by miles per gallon, multiplied by the price.

Common mistakes

  • Typing a long expression without brackets and assuming it is evaluated strictly left to right. Multiplication and division take priority.
  • Using % as a button for “divide by 100” inside a long expression. Check the result when it follows a + or − sign.
  • Rounding in your head between steps. Let the calculator keep full precision and round only the final answer.
  • Confusing the minus sign with the negative key. Use ± to change the sign of the number you are typing.

When you need roots, powers, logarithms or trigonometry, switch to the Scientific Calculator. For fractions that you want to keep in exact form, the Fraction Calculator simplifies automatically, and the Percentage Calculator handles “what is X% of Y”.

Frequently asked questions

Can I type with my keyboard?

Yes. Use the number keys and the + − × ÷ keys, plus Enter or = for the answer and Backspace to delete.

Why is 200 + 10% equal to 220?

The percent is taken of the number before it. 10% of 200 is 20, which is then added.

What does AC do?

AC clears the whole calculation. The backspace key removes the last character only.

Does it work with brackets?

Yes. Brackets can be nested, and the calculator evaluates the innermost ones first.

What a basic calculator is designed to do

A basic calculator is for everyday arithmetic: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, percentages and grouped expressions. It is useful for checking totals, comparing prices, sharing costs, estimating quantities and working through school arithmetic. The on-screen keypad is convenient on a phone, while keyboard entry makes longer expressions quicker on a computer. The calculator evaluates the expression as a mathematical expression rather than simply carrying out every operation in the order you typed it.

That distinction matters. In mathematics, multiplication and division have priority over addition and subtraction unless brackets change the grouping. A calculator that follows this convention can solve an expression such as 8 + 3 × 5 correctly as 23. If it instead calculated from left to right without respecting priority, it would produce 55, which is a different expression. Learning to recognise the order of operations helps you understand the result rather than treating the calculator as a mysterious answer machine.

Order of operations, step by step

A common memory aid is BIDMAS or BODMAS: brackets, indices or orders, division and multiplication, addition and subtraction. The exact wording varies, but the important rules are consistent. Evaluate brackets first. Then handle powers and roots. Multiplication and division have equal priority and are evaluated from left to right. Addition and subtraction also have equal priority and are evaluated from left to right. Multiplication is not always done before division, and addition is not always done before subtraction.

Example: multiplication before addition

Consider 18 + 6 × 4. Multiply 6 by 4 first to get 24, then add 18. The answer is 42. If you want the addition to happen first, write (18 + 6) × 4. The bracket gives 24, and 24 × 4 is 96. Brackets are not decoration. They tell the calculator which part of the expression belongs together.

Example: division and multiplication share priority

For 48 ÷ 6 × 2, work from left to right among operations of equal priority. First 48 ÷ 6 = 8, then 8 × 2 = 16. It is incorrect to automatically multiply 6 by 2 first. When an expression is difficult to read, add brackets to show the intended grouping. This is especially useful when checking a formula copied from a textbook or invoice.

Example: nested brackets

In 5 × (12 − (3 + 1)), evaluate the innermost bracket first: 3 + 1 = 4. The outer bracket becomes 12 − 4 = 8. Finally, 5 × 8 = 40. Nested brackets are helpful when an expression contains several stages. If you are entering a long calculation, check that every opening bracket has a matching closing bracket.

Fractions, decimals and percentages

A percentage means a number of parts out of one hundred. Therefore 25% is 25 ÷ 100 = 0.25, and 150% is 1.5. On its own, the percent key converts the displayed number to its percentage value as a decimal. In a percentage addition or subtraction, this calculator follows the familiar pocket-calculator convention of treating the percentage as a portion of the first number. For example, 200 + 10% means 200 plus ten per cent of 200, which is 220. Always inspect the result when using a percent key inside a longer expression because different calculator interfaces can implement percentage entry differently.

Decimals are useful for money and measurements, while fractions can express exact parts. One third is 1/3, which has a repeating decimal expansion of 0.333333…. Multiplying the fraction by 3 gives exactly 1, even though a displayed decimal may have been rounded. If exact fraction arithmetic is important, use the Fraction Calculator, which is designed to keep common fractional results in fractional form.

Finding a percentage mentally

Ten per cent is one tenth of a number, so move the decimal point one place to the left. Five per cent is half of ten per cent. One per cent is one hundredth. To find 15% of 80, calculate 10% of 80 (8) and 5% of 80 (4), then add them to get 12. This is a useful way to estimate a tip or discount before using the calculator to confirm the exact result.

Worked examples from everyday tasks

Shopping total

You buy three notebooks at 4.50 each and two pens at 1.25 each. The expression is (3 × 4.50) + (2 × 1.25). The notebooks cost 13.50 and the pens cost 2.50, giving a total of 16.00. Grouping each quantity and price makes the expression easier to check. If a discount applies to the whole basket, calculate the basket total first and then apply the discount to that total.

Splitting a shared expense

A group pays 72 for transport and 48 for food. The combined expense is 72 + 48 = 120. If four people share it equally, calculate 120 ÷ 4 = 30 per person. If one person paid for the transport and another paid for the food, the calculator can establish the equal share, but the group still needs to settle who owes whom. The calculator does not track payments or decide how an uneven share should be allocated.

Comparing unit prices

A 750-gram pack costs 1,800, while a 1-kilogram pack costs 2,250. To compare them fairly, calculate the cost per kilogram. The smaller pack costs 1,800 ÷ 0.75 = 2,400 per kilogram. The larger pack costs 2,250 per kilogram, so the larger pack is cheaper per kilogram, even though its shelf price is higher. Check that both quantities use the same unit before comparing.

Travel estimate

A journey covers 240 kilometres and the vehicle averages 12 kilometres per litre. The estimated fuel needed is 240 ÷ 12 = 20 litres. If fuel costs 1.35 per litre, the estimated fuel cost is 20 × 1.35 = 27. This is a simple estimate and assumes the stated fuel economy remains constant. Traffic, load, road conditions and driving style can change actual consumption.

Using a formula

The area of a rectangle is length multiplied by width. For a length of 8.2 metres and width of 3.5 metres, enter 8.2 × 3.5 to get 28.7 square metres. The units matter: multiplying metres by metres gives square metres. For more shape options and unit conversions, use the Area Calculator.

Keyboard and keypad use

The on-screen keypad provides digits, decimal point, arithmetic operators, brackets where supported, percent, sign change, clear and backspace controls. You can also use a physical keyboard. Number keys enter digits; the usual arithmetic keys enter operations; Enter or the equals key requests the result; and Backspace removes the last character. AC clears the complete expression, while backspace is useful when you have mistyped only the final digit or operator. On a phone, tap carefully around the decimal point and minus sign, especially when the display contains a long number.

When entering a negative number, distinguish a subtraction operation from a negative sign. In the expression 8 − 3, the minus sign tells the calculator to subtract. In −3 × 8, it is part of the number itself. The sign-change control can change the sign of the number currently being entered. If the expression looks wrong, clear it and enter it again rather than trying to guess how an incomplete expression will be interpreted.

Precision, rounding and errors

Calculators often display a finite number of decimal places even when the exact mathematical result continues indefinitely. For example, 1 ÷ 3 repeats forever in decimal form. A displayed value is an approximation, not a claim that the decimal terminates. Keep as much precision as possible through intermediate steps and round the final answer to the level appropriate for the task. For currency, that is usually two decimal places, although local cash-rounding rules may differ. For scientific work, use significant figures that reflect the precision of the measurements.

Division by zero is undefined. There is no finite number that can be multiplied by zero to recover a non-zero dividend, so the calculator displays an error message instead of an ordinary answer. A very small denominator can also produce a very large result, so check that the units and decimal point are correct. A calculation can be mathematically correct but still be based on an incorrect input.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Ignoring order of operations. Use brackets when you want to force a particular grouping.
  • Assuming multiplication always comes before division. They have equal priority and are evaluated left to right.
  • Entering 15 instead of 0.15 when a formula expects a decimal fraction, or entering 0.15 when the calculator's percentage field expects 15.
  • Rounding every intermediate answer. Keep precision until the final step.
  • Mixing units, such as adding metres to centimetres without converting first.
  • Using the calculator result without checking whether the expression represents the real problem correctly.
  • Confusing AC with backspace. AC clears the expression; backspace removes the most recent character.

When to use a different calculator

A basic calculator is not intended to replace every mathematical tool. For trigonometric functions, logarithms, roots, factorials and other advanced operations, use the Scientific Calculator. For a percentage increase, decrease or comparison, the Percentage Calculator presents the relationship directly. For a long expression involving variables or equations, an algebra solver may be more suitable than a basic arithmetic keypad.

More calculator questions

Why is 18 + 6 × 4 equal to 42?

Multiplication has priority over addition. Calculate 6 × 4 = 24 first, then add 18 to get 42. Use brackets if you intend to add before multiplying.

Does the calculator follow BIDMAS?

Yes. It follows the standard order of operations: brackets, powers where supported, multiplication and division from left to right, then addition and subtraction from left to right.

Why does 200 + 10% give 220?

The percentage is interpreted as 10% of the first number. Ten per cent of 200 is 20, and 200 + 20 = 220.

Can it divide by zero?

No. Division by zero is undefined, so the calculator reports that it cannot perform the operation.

Should I round every answer?

No. Keep the available precision during intermediate calculations and round the final result to a sensible number of decimal places or significant figures.

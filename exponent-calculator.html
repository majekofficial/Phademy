What an exponent means

An exponent tells you how many times a base is used as a factor. In 2⁴, the number 2 is the base and 4 is the exponent. The expression means 2 × 2 × 2 × 2, which equals 16. Exponents are also called powers or indices. They are used throughout arithmetic, algebra, science, finance and computing because they provide a compact way to describe repeated multiplication, very large values and very small values.

Phademy's exponent calculator takes a base and an exponent. It supports whole-number exponents, negative exponents, decimal exponents and fractional exponents. It can return exact integer or fraction results for supported whole-number powers and decimal approximations for other powers. The calculator has important domain limits: for fractional or decimal exponents, the base must be positive; and zero cannot be raised to zero or a negative power.

Base, exponent and the notation

In aⁿ, a is the base and n is the exponent. The base is the number being raised to a power. The exponent tells you which power to use. For example, in 5³, the base is 5 and the exponent is 3. The result is 125 because 5 × 5 × 5 = 125. Do not confuse 5³ with 5 × 3, which is 15. The exponent indicates repeated multiplication, not ordinary multiplication of the two written numbers.

Parentheses can change the meaning of a negative base. The expression (−2)² equals 4 because (−2) × (−2) = 4. By contrast, −2² is normally interpreted as −(2²), which equals −4. When entering a negative base, use parentheses in your written working so the intended operation is clear.

Positive whole-number exponents

A positive whole-number exponent represents repeated multiplication. For example, 3⁴ = 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81. The number of factors is four, not three. A power of one leaves the base unchanged: 7¹ = 7. A power of two is called a square, and a power of three is called a cube.

Example: Evaluate 2⁶. Multiply six factors of 2: 2 × 2 = 4, then × 2 = 8, then × 2 = 16, then × 2 = 32, then × 2 = 64. Therefore 2⁶ = 64.

For larger exponents, repeated multiplication by hand becomes slow and increases the chance of arithmetic errors. The calculator evaluates the power and displays the result. For very large exact integer values, the display may use scientific notation to keep the answer readable.

The zero exponent rule

Any non-zero number raised to the power zero equals one. For example, 8⁰ = 1, (−3)⁰ = 1 and 100⁰ = 1. One way to understand the rule is to look at a pattern: 2³ = 8, 2² = 4, 2¹ = 2. Each time the exponent decreases by one, divide by 2. Continuing gives 2⁰ = 1.

The expression 0⁰ is treated as undefined or indeterminate in many school contexts. This calculator does not return a normal numerical result for zero to the power zero. Do not apply the zero-exponent rule to a zero base without checking the context.

Negative exponents mean reciprocals

A negative exponent does not mean that the final answer must be negative. It means take the reciprocal of the corresponding positive power. The rule is a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ, provided the base is not zero. For example, 2⁻³ = 1/2³ = 1/8 = 0.125. Similarly, 10⁻² = 1/100 = 0.01.

Example: Evaluate 3⁻². First calculate the positive power: 3² = 9. Then take its reciprocal: 3⁻² = 1/9. The answer is positive even though the exponent is negative.

A negative exponent is useful in scientific notation and unit conversions. For example, 0.001 can be written as 1 × 10⁻³. The exponent tells you to move the decimal point three places to the left when starting from one.

Fractional exponents represent roots

A fractional exponent connects powers with roots. The exponent 1/2 means square root, so 9^(1/2) = √9 = 3. The exponent 1/3 means cube root, so 27^(1/3) = 3. More generally, a^(1/q) is the q-th root of a, where the root is defined in the relevant number system.

An exponent such as 2/3 means take the cube root and then square the result, or equivalently square first and take the cube root when the real-valued expression is defined. For example, 27^(2/3) = (cube root of 27)² = 3² = 9. This is why fractional exponents are sometimes called rational indices.

Example: Evaluate 16^(3/4). The denominator 4 indicates a fourth root: the fourth root of 16 is 2. Raise that result to the numerator power 3: 2³ = 8. Therefore 16^(3/4) = 8.

Decimal exponents

A decimal exponent can represent a non-integer power. For example, 2^0.5 is the square root of 2, approximately 1.41421356. The value 2^1.5 can be understood as 2 × √2, approximately 2.82842712. Decimal powers are useful in growth models, scaling relationships and scientific calculations where the exponent is not a whole number.

Unlike a whole-number power, a decimal exponent often produces an irrational result that cannot be written exactly as a terminating decimal or simple fraction. The calculator therefore gives an approximation. The number of displayed decimal places is a presentation choice; it does not mean the underlying value is exact to the last shown digit.

Laws of exponents

The laws of exponents make it possible to simplify expressions without calculating every power separately. When multiplying powers with the same base, add the exponents: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ. When dividing powers with the same non-zero base, subtract the exponents: aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ. When raising a power to another power, multiply the exponents: (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ.

Other useful rules include (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ and (a/b)ⁿ = aⁿ/bⁿ when the denominator is non-zero. These rules have conditions. You cannot add exponents when the bases are different, and you cannot apply a rule that divides by zero.

Same base multiplication: 2³ × 2⁴ = 2⁷ = 128. There are seven factors of 2 in total.

Power of a power: (3²)³ = 3⁶ = 729. The exponents multiply, so 2 × 3 = 6.

Same base division: 5⁴ ÷ 5² = 5² = 25, provided the denominator is not zero.

Worked example: compound growth

Suppose an amount grows by 5% each year and the growth is compounded annually. Each year, the amount is multiplied by 1.05. After ten years, the growth factor is 1.05¹⁰. This is approximately 1.62889463. An initial amount of 1,000 would therefore become about 1,628.89 before considering fees, taxes or other real-world changes.

This example uses an exponent to represent repeated multiplication by the same growth factor. It is not a prediction that every investment will earn a fixed return. The calculator evaluates the power only; it does not assess investment risk or choose an appropriate growth rate.

Negative bases and the calculator's limits

For whole-number exponents, a negative base can produce a real answer. An even exponent gives a positive result, while an odd exponent gives a negative result. For example, (−2)⁴ = 16 and (−2)³ = −8. The exponent calculator supports integer powers, including negative bases, subject to the zero-base restrictions.

For fractional or decimal exponents, this calculator requires a positive base. A negative base with a fractional exponent can be real for some special fractions, such as the cube root of −8, but it is not supported by this tool. If you enter (−8)^(1/3), the calculator will not return −2. Work out that real odd root separately or use a method that explicitly supports real roots of negative numbers.

Zero raised to a positive whole-number exponent is zero. Zero to the power zero and zero to a negative exponent are not accepted because they are undefined in this calculator's rules. A negative exponent on a non-zero base is valid and produces a reciprocal.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Multiplying instead of raising to a power: 4³ is 4×4×4, not 4×3.
  • Ignoring parentheses: −3² and (−3)² have different values.
  • Thinking a negative exponent gives a negative answer: 2⁻² equals 1/4.
  • Adding exponents with different bases: 2² × 3² is not 6⁴.
  • Misreading a fractional exponent: the denominator identifies the root and the numerator identifies the power.
  • Using zero as a base carelessly: 0⁰ and 0 raised to a negative power are not valid here.
  • Rounding too early: keep more precision in intermediate calculations.

Where exponents are used

Exponents appear in area and volume formulas, compound interest, population models, radioactive decay, computer storage and scientific notation. A square-law relationship may involve a quantity proportional to the square of another quantity. A cubic relationship may involve volume or a three-dimensional scale factor. Exponential growth and decay describe processes where the rate of change is proportional to the current amount.

In computing, powers of two are especially common because digital systems use binary values. One byte contains eight bits, and powers of two help describe the number of possible bit patterns. In science, powers of ten help write extremely large and small measurements compactly, such as distances in astronomy or particle-scale measurements.

Frequently asked questions

What is an exponent?

An exponent tells you how many times a base is used as a factor. In 4³, the base is 4 and the exponent is 3.

What is a negative exponent?

It means take the reciprocal of the corresponding positive power. For example, 2⁻³ = 1/8.

What does an exponent of 1/2 mean?

It means the square root of the base. For example, 25^(1/2) = 5.

Why does a fractional power of a negative base show no result?

The calculator only evaluates decimal and fractional exponents for positive bases. Some negative-base roots are real, but they are outside this tool's supported input range.

What is any non-zero number to the power zero?

It equals 1. The calculator does not treat 0⁰ as a normal numerical result.

Can I calculate very large powers?

Whole-number exponents are limited to a range from −400 to 400. Large exact results may be displayed in scientific notation.

How powers connect to roots

Roots and exponents are two ways to describe inverse operations. Squaring a number and taking its square root undo one another for non-negative real values. For example, 7² = 49 and √49 = 7. Writing the square root as an exponent gives 49^(1/2) = 7. Cube roots work similarly: 4³ = 64, so 64^(1/3) = 4. The denominator of a fractional exponent tells you which root is involved.

For an exponent p/q, the denominator q indicates the root and the numerator p indicates the power. For example, 32^(2/5) means take the fifth root of 32, which is 2, then square it to get 4. This is often easier than raising 32 to the second power first. The two approaches are equivalent when the real expression is defined, but choosing the smaller intermediate value can make the arithmetic simpler.

Comparing exponential growth and linear growth

Linear growth adds the same amount each period. Exponential growth multiplies by the same factor each period. If a savings balance increases by ₦500 every month, the pattern is linear. If a population grows by 5% each year, each year's increase depends on the current population, so the pattern is exponential. The exponent records how many repeated periods have passed.

For a starting amount P and a fixed growth factor r per period, the amount after n periods is P × rⁿ. If the growth rate is 5%, the factor is 1.05. If the amount decreases by 5%, the factor is 0.95. This simple model assumes the same rate and compounding interval each time. Real situations may change, so the formula is a model rather than a guarantee.

Scientific notation and powers of ten

Scientific notation writes a number as a value between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of ten. For example, 45,000 can be written as 4.5 × 10⁴, while 0.00072 can be written as 7.2 × 10⁻⁴. Positive powers move the decimal point to create larger values; negative powers describe fractions smaller than one. This notation is useful when measurements have many zeros.

When multiplying numbers in scientific notation, multiply the front values and add the powers of ten. When dividing, divide the front values and subtract the powers. Afterward, adjust the front value if it is not between 1 and 10. The exponent calculator can evaluate a power of ten, while the scientific calculator can help with broader arithmetic.

How to estimate whether a power is reasonable

Estimate before trusting a displayed answer. If the base is greater than 1 and the exponent increases, the result should increase. If the base is between 0 and 1 and the exponent is positive, the result becomes smaller as the exponent increases. A negative exponent on a base greater than 1 produces a positive fraction smaller than one. These patterns help you notice a mistyped exponent or an incorrect sign.

For example, 2⁵ must be larger than 2⁴ because it adds another factor of 2. The result 2⁵ = 32 is therefore plausible, while 2⁵ = 16 is not. For 0.5⁴, the result must be smaller than 0.5³, giving 0.0625 rather than a number greater than one.

Practice questions with worked answers

  1. Evaluate 4³. Multiply 4 × 4 × 4 = 64.
  2. Evaluate 5⁻². Take the reciprocal of 5²: 1/25 = 0.04.
  3. Evaluate 81^(1/2). The square root of 81 is 9.
  4. Evaluate 64^(2/3). The cube root of 64 is 4; square it to get 16.
  5. Simplify 3² × 3⁴. Same base, so add exponents: 3⁶ = 729.

Notice that the questions use different exponent rules. Do not apply the same shortcut to every expression. Identify whether the exponent is positive, negative, zero or fractional, and check whether the bases are the same before simplifying.

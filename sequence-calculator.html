Finding terms and sums of arithmetic and geometric sequences

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers that follows a rule. This calculator handles the two types met in school: arithmetic sequences, where you add the same amount each time, and geometric sequences, where you multiply by the same amount. Enter the first term, the common difference or ratio and the position n, and it returns the nth term, the sum of the first n terms and the first few terms of the list.

Arithmetic sequences

nth term: aₙ = a + (n − 1)d | sum: Sₙ = n ÷ 2 × (2a + (n − 1)d)

  • 3, 7, 11, … has a = 3 and d = 4. The 10th term is 3 + 9 × 4 = 39, and the sum of the first 10 terms is 10 ÷ 2 × (3 + 39) = 210.
  • Decreasing sequences use a negative d. With a = 100 and d = −7, the 12th term is 100 − 77 = 23 and the sum of 12 terms is 6 × (100 + 23) = 738.

Geometric sequences

nth term: aₙ = a × rⁿ⁻¹ | sum: Sₙ = a(rⁿ − 1) ÷ (r − 1), when r ≠ 1

  • 2, 6, 18, … has a = 2 and r = 3. The 5th term is 2 × 3⁴ = 162, and the sum of 5 terms is 2 × (243 − 1) ÷ 2 = 242.
  • A ratio below 1 shrinks the terms. With a = 1000 and r = 0.5, the 6th term is 31.25 and the sum of 6 terms is 1,968.75.
  • A ratio of 1 gives a constant sequence. With a = 5, every term is 5, so the sum of 8 terms is 40.

Where sequences show up

  • Savings that increase by a fixed amount each month are arithmetic.
  • Compound interest and population growth are geometric. A balance growing at 5% a year has r = 1.05.
  • Stacking and seating patterns, such as rows in a theatre that gain two seats each row, are arithmetic.

Common mistakes

  • Using n instead of n − 1 in the formula: the first term is position 1, so there are n − 1 steps to the nth.
  • Entering a ratio as a percentage. A 5% increase is r = 1.05, not 5.
  • Mixing up d and r: difference is added, ratio is multiplied.
  • Using the sum formula for r = 1, which would divide by zero. The calculator handles that case.

Frequently asked questions

How do I tell whether a sequence is arithmetic or geometric?

Subtract consecutive terms. If the difference is constant, it is arithmetic. Divide consecutive terms. If the ratio is constant, it is geometric.

What is the nth term?

A formula that gives the term at any position n without listing all the earlier ones.

Can the common difference be negative?

Yes. The terms then decrease. Negative and fractional ratios are also allowed.

Why do some results show fractions?

Exact fractions are shown with their decimal equivalent. A ratio of 0.5, for example, produces 125/4 ≈ 31.25.

What makes a number pattern a sequence?

A sequence is a list in which each term has a position. The first term is usually written as a, the second as the second term, and the term at position n as aₙ. The position matters: the same value can appear more than once, and a sequence is not simply a set of different numbers. A rule may describe how to move from one term to the next or give a direct formula for any position. This calculator focuses on arithmetic and geometric sequences, two important patterns in algebra.

To identify a pattern, write down several consecutive terms and compare them. For an arithmetic sequence, subtract each term from the next. If the difference remains constant, the sequence is arithmetic. For a geometric sequence, divide each term by the previous non-zero term. If the ratio remains constant, the sequence is geometric. Checking more than one pair is important: two terms alone can fit many possible rules.

Arithmetic sequences in detail

An arithmetic sequence changes by adding the same common difference d at every step. The rule for the nth term is aₙ = a + (n−1)d, where a is the first term. The expression n−1 appears because the first term is already at position one. To reach the second term, only one difference is added; to reach the tenth term, nine differences are added.

For example, consider 8, 13, 18, 23, ... The first term is 8 and the common difference is 5. The 20th term is 8 + 19×5 = 103. A negative common difference is valid too. In 50, 44, 38, 32, ... the common difference is −6, so the 10th term is 50 + 9×(−6) = −4. A zero difference produces a constant sequence, such as 7, 7, 7, 7.

The sum of the first n arithmetic terms is Sₙ = n/2 × [2a + (n−1)d]. It can also be written as n/2 × (first term + nth term). This second form explains the formula: pair the first term with the last, the second with the second-last, and so on. Each pair has the same total. If there is an odd number of terms, the middle term is counted as half of a pair.

Geometric sequences in detail

A geometric sequence is formed by multiplying each term by a fixed common ratio r. Its nth term is aₙ = a×rⁿ⁻¹. If the first term is 3 and the ratio is 2, the sequence is 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, ... The fifth term is 3×2⁴ = 48. A ratio can be negative or fractional. With a = 16 and r = −1/2, the terms are 16, −8, 4, −2, 1, ... The sign alternates and the magnitude gets smaller.

The sum of the first n geometric terms is Sₙ = a(rⁿ−1)/(r−1), when r is not 1. An equivalent form is a(1−rⁿ)/(1−r). Both produce the same result. If r = 1, every term is a, so the sum is simply n×a. This special case must be handled separately because the general expression would divide by zero.

Do not confuse a finite geometric sum with an infinite geometric series. The calculator adds the first n terms that you specify. An infinite series has a separate condition: it converges only when the absolute value of r is less than one. When |r| is below one, the terms approach zero and the infinite sum is a/(1−r). That is a mathematical limit, not the same as the finite sum displayed by this tool.

Worked examples and interpretation

Finding a distant arithmetic term

A theatre has 18 seats in its first row and adds four seats in every new row. The number of seats per row forms 18, 22, 26, 30, ... The 15th row has 18 + 14×4 = 74 seats. The total number of seats in the first 15 rows is 15/2×(18+74) = 690. The first result describes one row; the sum describes all rows from the first through the fifteenth.

Compound growth as a geometric sequence

An amount of 2,000 grows by 3% each year. The yearly multiplier is 1.03, not 3, because the original amount remains and three percent is added to it. After five yearly growth steps, the amount is 2,000×1.03⁵, approximately 2,318.55. In a sequence, the first term is the starting amount and each following term applies the same multiplier once. Actual financial products may compound at different intervals or include fees and deposits, so this simplified example is a mathematical model.

Alternating terms

For 64, −32, 16, −8, ... the first term is 64 and the ratio is −1/2. The sixth term is 64×(−1/2)⁵ = −2. The negative sign is important: an even power of a negative ratio is positive, while an odd power is negative. The calculator can work with negative and fractional ratios, so keep the sign when entering the value.

How to find the rule from a list

If a question gives a list but not the formula, calculate consecutive differences first. For 4, 9, 14, 19, the differences are 5, 5, 5, so it is arithmetic with a = 4 and d = 5. If the differences are not constant, test ratios. For 3, 6, 12, 24, each term is twice the previous one, so r = 2. If neither the differences nor ratios are constant, the pattern may be another type of sequence, such as a quadratic sequence, Fibonacci-type recurrence or a sequence defined by alternating rules. This calculator is not a general pattern-discovery engine.

When a question gives the first few terms, avoid assuming that the pattern is arithmetic just because the numbers increase by a visible amount once. A sequence such as 1, 4, 9, 16 has differences 3, 5, 7, which are not constant. It is the sequence of square numbers, not an arithmetic sequence. Similarly, 1, 2, 4, 7 has differences 1, 2, 3, so it does not fit either constant-difference or constant-ratio rules.

Common mistakes in sequence questions

  • Using n instead of n−1: the first term is already at position one, so only n−1 jumps occur before reaching term n.
  • Entering a percentage as the ratio: a 6% increase uses 1.06, and a 6% decrease uses 0.94.
  • Mixing addition and multiplication: an arithmetic sequence uses a common difference; a geometric sequence uses a common ratio.
  • Forgetting negative signs: a negative difference decreases the terms, while a negative ratio alternates signs.
  • Confusing a term with a sum: aₙ is one term at position n; Sₙ is the total of the first n terms.
  • Using the geometric sum formula when r = 1: use n×a for a constant geometric sequence.
  • Assuming every pattern is arithmetic or geometric: check the differences and ratios rather than forcing a rule.

Using the calculator and checking your answer

Select arithmetic or geometric, then enter the first term, the common difference or common ratio, and the position n. The calculator returns the nth term, the sum of the first n terms and an initial list of terms. Make sure n is a positive whole-number position. If the question asks for the 12th term, enter 12, not 11. The formula itself accounts for the number of jumps.

Check the result by generating a few terms manually. The first term should equal a. In an arithmetic sequence, subtracting adjacent terms should always give d. In a geometric sequence, dividing adjacent non-zero terms should give r. For a sum, add the first few terms directly when n is small and compare that total with Sₙ. For large n, the formula is much faster and less prone to skipped terms.

Practice questions

  1. Find the 12th term of 5, 9, 13, 17, ... Answer: a = 5, d = 4, so a₁₂ = 5 + 11×4 = 49.
  2. Find the sum of the first 20 positive odd numbers. They form 1, 3, 5, ... with a = 1 and d = 2. The 20th term is 39 and the sum is 20/2×(1+39) = 400.
  3. Find the sixth term of 2, 6, 18, ... Answer: a = 2, r = 3, so a₆ = 2×3⁵ = 486.
  4. Find the sum of the first four terms of 5, 10, 20, 40. Answer: 5+10+20+40 = 75.
  5. Is 2, 4, 8, 16 arithmetic? No. The differences are 2, 4 and 8, while the ratio is consistently 2, so it is geometric.

Reading sequence notation in textbooks

Textbooks may use several symbols for the same idea. The first term may be written as a₁, while a general term is written as aₙ. The sum of the first n terms is often written Sₙ. Read the subscript as a position label, not as multiplication: a₅ means the fifth term, not a times five. If a question asks for the sum up to the 10th term, include terms 1 through 10. Do not calculate only the 10th term and mistake it for the total.

Some questions give a₃ or a₇ instead of the first term. You can use the same formulas after expressing the known term in terms of a and d or a and r. For an arithmetic sequence, a₇ = a+6d. For a geometric sequence, a₇ = ar⁶. These equations can help you find a missing first term or common pattern when another piece of information is supplied.

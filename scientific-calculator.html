What a scientific calculator can help you calculate

A scientific calculator is designed for more than everyday addition, subtraction, multiplication and division. It includes mathematical functions used in algebra, trigonometry, geometry, physics, chemistry and higher-level study. This calculator supports sine, cosine and tangent, inverse trigonometric functions, logarithms, powers, square roots, factorials, π and e. It also provides degree and radian modes, memory controls and a short calculation history.

You can enter an expression with the keypad or type it into the calculation field. The result updates while you work, and pressing the equals key commits the calculation. The tool is useful for checking arithmetic and exploring how mathematical functions behave, but it does not replace understanding the method required by a question.

Degrees and radians: check this before using trigonometry

Angles can be measured in degrees or radians. A full turn is 360 degrees or 2π radians. The scientific calculator starts in degree mode, and the Deg/Rad key switches between the two. The selected mode changes the interpretation of the angle inside sine, cosine and tangent, so always check the displayed mode before calculating.

For example, sin(30) in degree mode is 0.5 because the angle is 30 degrees. In radian mode, sin(30) is approximately −0.9880 because 30 radians is a much larger angle. To represent 30 degrees while in radian mode, convert it to π/6 radians. Then sin(π/6) is 0.5.

Inverse trigonometric functions work in the opposite direction. If sin(θ) = 0.5, then asin(0.5) returns an angle of 30 in degree mode or π/6, approximately 0.5236, in radian mode. The answer is an angle, not the reciprocal of the sine value.

Sine, cosine and tangent

For a right-angled triangle and an acute angle θ, sine is opposite divided by hypotenuse, cosine is adjacent divided by hypotenuse, and tangent is opposite divided by adjacent. These ratios connect an angle to the side lengths of a right triangle. They are also used to describe repeating waves and circular motion.

For a triangle with an angle of 30 degrees, sin(30) = 0.5. If the hypotenuse is 10 cm, the opposite side is 10 × 0.5 = 5 cm. If the adjacent side is needed and the hypotenuse is 10 cm, use cos(30) × 10, which is approximately 8.6603 cm.

Tangent is undefined at angles where cosine is zero, such as 90 degrees, because the ratio would require division by zero. A calculator error at such an angle reflects a mathematical limitation, not a missing setting.

Inverse trigonometric functions

The inverse keys asin, acos and atan find an angle from a known ratio. Use them when a question gives two sides of a right triangle and asks for an angle. If the opposite side is 5 and the hypotenuse is 10, then sin(θ) = 5/10 = 0.5, so θ = asin(0.5) = 30 degrees in degree mode.

Inverse sine and inverse cosine return principal angles in their standard ranges. In a geometry problem, the diagram and angle conditions may tell you whether another angle is relevant. Do not assume that one inverse-function result is the only possible angle in every trigonometric equation.

Powers, roots and order of operations

The x² key squares a value, while xʸ raises a value to a chosen power. The square-root key finds the principal square root. For example, √144 = 12, 2⁵ = 32, and 3² = 9. A negative exponent means a reciprocal: 2⁻³ = 1/2³ = 1/8.

Use brackets to make the intended order clear. The expression 2 × (3 + 4)² ÷ 7 is calculated by adding inside the brackets first, squaring the result, multiplying by 2 and dividing by 7. It gives 14. Without brackets, 2 × 3 + 4² ÷ 7 represents a different calculation.

In general, evaluate brackets first, then powers and roots, then multiplication and division, and finally addition and subtraction. Operations at the same priority are handled from left to right. When an expression is complex, add brackets even if you believe the intended order is obvious.

Logarithms: log and ln

A logarithm answers the question: to what power must a base be raised to produce a given number? The calculator uses log for base 10 and ln for the natural logarithm, whose base is the mathematical constant e. Since 10³ = 1,000, log(1,000) = 3. Since e¹ = e, ln(e) = 1.

Logarithms are useful for solving exponential equations, describing quantities that span a wide range, and working with scales in science. For example, if 2ˣ = 32, then x = 5 because 2⁵ = 32. If the base is not immediately recognisable, logarithms can be used to solve for the exponent.

For real-number calculations, the argument of a logarithm must be greater than zero. log(0) and ln(−4) are not real-number results. If you need a logarithm with a base other than 10 or e, use the change-of-base relationship log base b of x = log(x) ÷ log(b), provided b is positive and not 1 and x is positive.

Factorials and counting

The factorial symbol ! means multiply a positive whole number by every smaller positive whole number down to 1. For example, 5! = 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120. By definition, 0! = 1. Factorials appear in permutations, combinations, probability and algebra.

A simple example is arranging five different books in a row. There are 5! = 120 possible orders. If you are choosing a group where order does not matter, a combination formula is more appropriate. The Permutation and Combination Calculator is designed for those counting questions.

Using π and e

π is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, approximately 3.14159. It appears in circle measurements, trigonometry and periodic functions. For example, the circumference of a circle with radius 4 is 2πr = 8π, approximately 25.1327.

The constant e is approximately 2.71828. It is the base of natural logarithms and appears in continuous growth and decay, calculus and many scientific models. The calculator lets you insert π and e directly into expressions such as sin(π/6) or e².

Worked examples to practise

Example 1: square root and power. Evaluate √144 + 2⁵. The square root is 12 and the power is 32, so the total is 44. Enter sqrt(144)+2^5 or use the matching keys.

Example 2: logarithm. Find log(1,000). Since 10 raised to the power 3 is 1,000, the answer is 3. For ln(e), the answer is 1.

Example 3: trigonometric identity. Evaluate sin²(45°) + cos²(45°). The identity sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 means the result should be 1 for any angle. In degree mode, enter sin(45)^2+cos(45)^2. This is also a useful check that the mode is correct.

Example 4: factorial. Calculate 6!. Multiply 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 to obtain 720.

Memory, answer and history controls

The memory keys allow you to store a value, recall it, add the current result to it, subtract the current result from it, or clear it. This is useful when a multi-step problem repeatedly uses the same constant. The Ans key inserts the previous committed answer into a new expression. The history panel keeps the most recent calculations so that you can revisit an earlier expression without typing it again.

Memory and history are conveniences, not a substitute for recording important working in your notebook. If you clear the browser's stored site data or use another browser, saved calculator state may not be available. Copy any result you need to keep for an assignment or report.

Common errors and how to fix them

  • Wrong angle mode: Check Deg or Rad before using trigonometric functions. Convert the angle if the question uses a different unit.
  • Confusing inverse sine with reciprocal: asin(0.5) finds an angle. It does not mean 1 ÷ sin(0.5).
  • Missing brackets: Close every function bracket and use parentheses around grouped expressions.
  • Mixing log and ln: log means base 10 here; ln means base e.
  • Using an invalid logarithm input: The argument must be positive for a real result.
  • Dividing by zero: Expressions with a zero denominator are undefined.
  • Rounding too early: Keep extra digits through intermediate steps and round the final answer to the precision requested.

What the calculator does not replace

A calculator evaluates the expression you enter. It does not automatically know which formula applies to a word problem, which angle in a diagram is required, or how many significant figures your teacher expects. It also cannot determine whether a model's assumptions are appropriate. Write down the formula, substitute values with units, and interpret the result in the context of the question.

For plain arithmetic, the Basic Calculator offers a simpler keypad. For logarithms to arbitrary bases and additional explanations, visit the Logarithm Calculator. For powers and fractional exponents, see the Exponent Calculator.

Percentages, negative values and previous answers

The scientific keypad includes a percentage key and a sign-change control. A percentage is a fraction out of one hundred, so 15% is 0.15. The calculator follows a phone-calculator style for percentage expressions: for example, 200 + 10% gives 220. When a question uses a percentage in a different context, write the intended relationship explicitly, such as 200 × 0.10 for ten per cent of 200.

Negative values are important in algebra and science. The sign-change control can make the current number negative or positive. Be careful to distinguish a negative number from subtraction. For example, (−3)² = 9, while −3² is interpreted as the negative of 3 squared, which is −9 under standard order of operations. Brackets remove the ambiguity.

Use the calculator to check identities, not replace reasoning

Mathematical identities are relationships that hold for every value in their valid domain. For example, sin²θ + cos²θ = 1. Testing a few angles on the calculator can help you notice the pattern and check that you have selected the right angle mode. A numerical check is not a proof, however. When a question asks you to prove an identity, use algebraic or trigonometric reasoning and present the steps.

Similarly, a calculator can verify that a numerical answer is plausible, but it cannot decide whether the original model is appropriate. In a physics question, check the formula, units, sign convention and assumptions before accepting the displayed number. A precise answer to the wrong expression is still the wrong answer.

Use units and significant figures with care

The calculator works with numerical expressions, but it does not attach physical units to each number. If you calculate a force, length, energy or angle in a science problem, keep track of the units separately and check that the formula is dimensionally consistent. A numerical result without its unit may be incomplete, even when the arithmetic is correct. Round only after the calculation is complete and use the precision requested by the question.

Choosing an appropriate level of precision

A calculator can display many digits, but a long decimal does not automatically mean a measurement is exact. The precision of a scientific result is limited by the values supplied. If a question gives a mass to two significant figures and a time to two significant figures, reporting an answer to ten decimal places suggests a level of certainty that the data do not support. Keep extra digits during intermediate steps and round the final result to match the question or the least precise measured value.

Exact values such as defined conversion factors and counted quantities are treated differently from measured values. In a school exercise, follow the significant-figure instructions given by your teacher or examination board. When no rule is stated, use a sensible number of significant figures and include the unit. This makes the result easier to interpret and compare with other calculations.

Frequently asked questions

Does the calculator start in degrees or radians?

It starts in degree mode. Use the Deg/Rad key to switch modes and check the label before entering a trigonometric expression.

How do I calculate sin of 30 degrees?

Make sure degree mode is selected, then enter sin(30). The result is 0.5.

How do I calculate sin of π/6?

In radian mode, enter sin(π/6). The result is 0.5. In degree mode, π/6 is interpreted as an angle of about 0.524 degrees, so the result is different.

What is the difference between log and ln?

In this calculator, log is base 10 and ln is base e. Both are logarithms, but they use different bases.

Why is tan(90°) undefined?

Tangent is sine divided by cosine. Since cos(90°) is zero, the expression would require division by zero.

Why does a long decimal appear?

Many mathematical results are irrational or cannot be represented exactly as finite decimals. The displayed value is rounded, so follow the precision instructions in your question.

Can I use the keyboard instead of the keypad?

Yes. Type a supported expression into the calculation field, or use the on-screen keys. Press Enter or the equals key to commit the result.

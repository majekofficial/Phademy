Solve a quadratic equation and understand its graph

A quadratic equation contains a squared variable and can be written in the standard form ax² + bx + c = 0, where a, b and c are numbers and a is not zero. Quadratics appear in projectile motion, area problems, optimisation, engineering and many other subjects. This calculator finds the roots and also reports features of the related parabola, including its discriminant, vertex, axis of symmetry, y-intercept, vertex form and factorised form.

Enter the coefficients rather than typing the full equation. For example, in 2x² + 3x − 5 = 0, enter a = 2, b = 3 and c = −5. You can enter integers, decimals or fractions such as 1/2. The b and c fields may be left empty to represent zero, but a cannot be zero because the equation would no longer be quadratic.

Identify a, b and c correctly

The coefficient a multiplies x², b multiplies x, and c is the constant term. The sign belongs to the coefficient. In x² − 5x + 6 = 0, the values are a = 1, b = −5 and c = 6. In −3x² + 2x − 7 = 0, they are a = −3, b = 2 and c = −7.

If a term is missing, its coefficient is zero. The equation 4x² − 9 = 0 has a = 4, b = 0 and c = −9. The equation 2x² + 7x = 0 has a = 2, b = 7 and c = 0. Do not shift a coefficient into the wrong field simply because a term is absent.

The quadratic formula

x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) ÷ 2a

The formula gives the solutions, also called roots or zeros, of any quadratic equation in standard form. The plus-minus sign means calculate two values: one using addition before the square root term and one using subtraction. Sometimes both values are equal, and sometimes the answers are complex rather than real.

The expression under the square root is called the discriminant, usually written as D = b² − 4ac. It determines the type of roots before the full calculation is completed. A positive discriminant gives two different real roots. A zero discriminant gives one repeated real root. A negative discriminant gives a pair of complex conjugate roots and no real x-intercepts.

Understand the discriminant

Consider x² − 5x + 6 = 0. Here D = (−5)² − 4(1)(6) = 25 − 24 = 1. Since the discriminant is positive, the graph crosses the x-axis at two distinct points and the equation has two different real roots.

For x² + 2x + 1 = 0, D = 2² − 4(1)(1) = 0. There is one repeated root at x = −1. The parabola touches the x-axis at its vertex and turns around without crossing it.

For x² + 1 = 0, D = 0² − 4(1)(1) = −4. There are no real roots. The parabola y = x² + 1 remains above the x-axis, although the equation has complex solutions x = i and x = −i, where i is the square root of −1.

Worked example: two integer roots

Solve x² − 5x + 6 = 0. Identify a = 1, b = −5 and c = 6. Substitute into the discriminant: D = 25 − 24 = 1. Then x = (5 ± √1) ÷ 2. The two calculations are (5 + 1) ÷ 2 = 3 and (5 − 1) ÷ 2 = 2. Therefore the roots are x = 2 and x = 3.

You can check the result by substituting each root into the original equation. For x = 2, 2² − 5(2) + 6 = 4 − 10 + 6 = 0. For x = 3, 3² − 5(3) + 6 = 9 − 15 + 6 = 0. Both values satisfy the equation.

Worked example: a repeated root

Solve x² + 2x + 1 = 0. The coefficients are 1, 2 and 1. The discriminant is 4 − 4 = 0, so the two values from the quadratic formula are identical: x = (−2 ± 0) ÷ 2 = −1. The equation can also be written as (x + 1)² = 0. This is why the root is called repeated or double.

Worked example: fractional roots

Solve 2x² + 3x − 5 = 0. Here a = 2, b = 3 and c = −5. The discriminant is 3² − 4(2)(−5) = 9 + 40 = 49. Therefore x = (−3 ± 7) ÷ 4. The positive branch gives 4 ÷ 4 = 1, while the negative branch gives −10 ÷ 4 = −5/2. The roots are 1 and −2.5.

Worked example: exact surd roots

For x² − 2x − 1 = 0, the discriminant is (−2)² − 4(1)(−1) = 8. The roots are (2 ± √8) ÷ 2. Since √8 = 2√2, the exact roots simplify to 1 ± √2. Their decimal approximations are approximately 2.4142 and −0.4142. Keeping the surd form is often preferable when a question asks for an exact answer.

The vertex and axis of symmetry

The graph of y = ax² + bx + c is a parabola. Its vertex is the turning point, either the lowest point when a is positive or the highest point when a is negative. The x-coordinate of the vertex is −b ÷ 2a. Substitute that x-value into the quadratic expression to obtain the y-coordinate.

For y = x² − 5x + 6, the vertex x-coordinate is 5/2 = 2.5. Substituting gives y = 2.5² − 5(2.5) + 6 = 6.25 − 12.5 + 6 = −0.25. The vertex is (2.5, −0.25). The vertical line x = 2.5 is the axis of symmetry, because the left and right sides of the parabola mirror each other around it.

Vertex form and the y-intercept

Vertex form is y = a(x − h)² + k, where (h, k) is the vertex. It makes the turning point and direction of opening easy to see. For x² − 5x + 6, the vertex is (2.5, −0.25), so the vertex form is y = (x − 2.5)² − 0.25.

The y-intercept is where the graph crosses the vertical axis, so x = 0. Substituting zero leaves y = c. The y-intercept is therefore (0, c). For x² − 5x + 6, it is (0, 6). The calculator reports this point along with the vertex and axis of symmetry.

Factorised form and the relationship to roots

If a quadratic has roots r₁ and r₂, it can be written as a(x − r₁)(x − r₂) = 0. For x² − 5x + 6, the roots 2 and 3 give (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0. Expanding the brackets returns x² − 5x + 6.

Factorising can be quicker than the quadratic formula when the numbers are simple. However, not every quadratic factorises neatly using integers. The formula works more generally and provides exact surd or complex results when required. The calculator displays the factorised form when it can represent it in its supported form.

How to interpret the parabola

If a is positive, the parabola opens upward and the vertex is a minimum. If a is negative, it opens downward and the vertex is a maximum. The discriminant indicates whether the curve crosses, touches or does not meet the x-axis. The vertex tells you where the turning point occurs, while the y-intercept shows the value of the expression when x is zero.

These features are useful in applications. In a simplified projectile model, a quadratic can describe height over time, and the roots may represent when an object is at ground level. In an area problem, the roots may identify dimensions that make an equation equal zero. Always check whether negative roots or values outside the problem's physical domain should be rejected.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Dropping a negative sign: In x² − 5x + 6, b is −5, not 5.
  • Entering the equation's terms in the wrong boxes: a belongs to x², b belongs to x, and c is the constant.
  • Using a = 0: That removes the squared term and creates a linear equation.
  • Forgetting the ± sign: When the discriminant is positive, calculate both branches of the formula.
  • Calling a negative discriminant “no solution” without qualification: There are no real roots, but there are complex roots.
  • Rounding an exact root too early: Keep fractions and surds in exact form when possible.
  • Confusing the vertex with an x-intercept: The vertex is the turning point; an x-intercept is a point where y equals zero.

What the calculator shows and what it does not

The calculator uses the coefficients to calculate roots, the discriminant, vertex, axis of symmetry, y-intercept, vertex form and factorised form, and it draws a graph of the parabola. It accepts integers, decimals and fractions such as 1/2. It does not accept a = 0 because that would not be a quadratic equation.

The graph is a visual aid, not a substitute for exact algebra. A plotted point may look close to an axis even when it is not exactly on it. Use the numerical roots and the discriminant to determine the exact number and type of solutions. How the sign of a changes the graph

The coefficient a controls whether the parabola opens upward or downward and how stretched or narrow it appears. If a is positive, the squared term becomes large and positive as x moves far from zero, so the curve opens upward. If a is negative, the squared term becomes large and negative, so the curve opens downward. A larger absolute value of a makes the parabola narrower compared with one whose a value is closer to zero.

The coefficient c gives the y-intercept because setting x to zero removes the first two terms. The coefficient b influences the horizontal position of the vertex and the symmetry line. These observations help you make sense of the graph rather than treating it as a picture unrelated to the algebra.

Why a quadratic can have two, one or no x-intercepts

The x-intercepts occur where y equals zero, which is exactly the original quadratic equation. If the vertex lies below the x-axis and the parabola opens upward, the curve crosses the axis twice. If the vertex lies on the x-axis, it touches once. If the vertex lies above the x-axis and the parabola opens upward, it does not cross at all. The discriminant gives the algebraic test for these cases.

For a downward-opening parabola, the corresponding picture is reversed: a vertex above the axis generally produces two crossings, a vertex on the axis produces one repeated crossing, and a vertex below the axis produces none. The discriminant remains the reliable test regardless of whether a is positive or negative.

Use exact answers when the question requires them

When the square root of the discriminant is not a perfect square, the exact roots may contain a surd. For example, a root such as 1 + √2 is exact, while 2.4142 is only an approximation. If you round an intermediate value and then use it again, small errors can accumulate. Keep the exact expression until the final line whenever possible.

In applied questions, a decimal may be more useful because it can be compared with a measured time, length or quantity. Follow the instruction in the question: use exact form when requested, and give a suitable number of decimal places or significant figures when an approximate result is expected.

Connect the algebra to an application

When a quadratic comes from a word problem, first define what the variable represents and write the equation from the conditions given. Solve the equation, then interpret each root in context. A negative time, a negative physical length or a value outside a stated interval may be mathematically valid for the equation but not meaningful for the original situation. State why a root is accepted or rejected instead of selecting an answer based only on the calculator output.

Frequently asked questions

What makes an equation quadratic?

It is a polynomial equation whose highest power of the variable is 2, with a non-zero coefficient for the squared term.

What happens if the discriminant is zero?

The equation has one repeated real root. The parabola touches the x-axis at its vertex.

Can a quadratic have no real roots?

Yes. A negative discriminant means there are no real x-intercepts, although two complex roots exist.

Why is a not allowed to be zero?

If a is zero, the x² term disappears and the equation becomes linear or a constant statement rather than a quadratic.

Can I enter fractions?

Yes. Coefficients can be entered as fractions such as 1/2 as well as integers and decimals.

What is the difference between roots and the vertex?

Roots are x-values where the quadratic equals zero. The vertex is the turning point of the graph and is given by an x-coordinate and a y-coordinate.

How can I check the roots?

Substitute each root into the original equation. A correct root makes the left-hand side equal zero.

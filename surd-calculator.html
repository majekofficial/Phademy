Working with square roots in exact form

A surd is a root that cannot be written as a whole number or a terminating decimal, such as √2 or √13. Keeping an answer as a surd preserves its exact value, which is why exam questions often ask for “exact form”. This calculator has three modes: simplify a square root, rationalise a denominator, and add or subtract surds. Each one can show its workings.

Simplifying a square root

Find the largest square number that divides the number under the root, take its root out of the surd, and leave the remainder inside.

√(a² × b) = a√b

  • √72 = √(36 × 2) = 6√2, which is about 8.485281.
  • √50 = √(25 × 2) = 5√2, about 7.071068.
  • √49 = 7. A perfect square simplifies completely.
  • √13 has no square factor other than 1, so it stays as √13 (about 3.605551).

Rationalising a single-root denominator

Leaving a surd in the denominator is considered unfinished. Multiply the top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator, which is the same expression with the sign of the surd part reversed. The product of conjugates removes the root: (b + c√d)(b − c√d) = b² − c²d.

6 ÷ (2 + √3)

Multiply top and bottom by (2 − √3): the denominator becomes 4 − 3 = 1 and the numerator is 12 − 6√3. The calculator returns 12 − 6√3, about 1.607695. Enter a = 6, b = 2, c = 1, d = 3.

1 ÷ (1 + √2)

Multiplying by (1 − √2) gives (1 − √2) ÷ (1 − 2) = −1 + √2, about 0.414214. This is the same as √2 − 1.

Adding like surds after simplification

Only surds with the same number under the root can be combined, in the same way that 3x and 5x can be added but 3x and 5y cannot. Simplify first, then collect like terms.

  • 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2 (about 11.313708).
  • 2√12 − √3: √12 simplifies to 2√3, so 2√12 = 4√3 and 4√3 − √3 = 3√3 (about 5.196152). Enter c as −1 to subtract.
  • √2 + √3 cannot be simplified further, because the numbers under the roots differ. The calculator leaves it as √2 + √3, about 3.146264.

Common mistakes

  • Stopping part-way through simplifying. √72 = 2√18 is true but not simplest; the largest square factor, 36, gives 6√2.
  • Adding the numbers under the roots: √2 + √3 is not √5.
  • Forgetting to change the sign when forming the conjugate.
  • Entering decimals. The tool accepts whole numbers only.

Frequently asked questions

What is a surd?

An irrational root, such as √2, that cannot be written exactly as a fraction or terminating decimal.

Why do we rationalise denominators?

It gives a standard form that is easier to compare, add and simplify, and it is expected in many exams.

Can I simplify cube roots?

No. This calculator works with square roots only.

Is √2 + √3 equal to √5?

No. √2 + √3 is about 3.146, while √5 is about 2.236.

What is a surd and why keep it exact?

A surd is an irrational root written in exact radical form, such as √2 or 3√5. It cannot be expressed exactly as a ratio of two integers, even though a decimal approximation can be calculated. For example, √2 is approximately 1.41421356, but the decimal continues without repeating. Keeping √2 in an answer is often more accurate and more useful than writing a rounded decimal.

Surds appear naturally in geometry, algebra and trigonometry. The diagonal of a square with side length one is √2 by Pythagoras' theorem. The diagonal of a rectangle with sides 3 and 4 is √25 = 5, which is not an irrational surd because the root simplifies to a whole number. In an exact mathematical answer, simplify the radical as far as possible before converting to a decimal.

Simplifying square roots

To simplify √n, look for the largest perfect-square factor of n. A perfect square is a number such as 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 or 36, formed by multiplying a whole number by itself. Split the root using √(ab) = √a×√b, then take the square root of the perfect-square factor outside the radical. For example, √72 = √(36×2) = 6√2. The number under the root should have no remaining square factor greater than one.

Another example is √180. The largest square factor is 36, so √180 = √(36×5) = 6√5. If you use a smaller factor such as 4, you get 2√45, which is correct but not fully simplified because 45 still contains the square factor 9. Continue until the radicand is square-free. The calculator performs this simplification for positive whole-number inputs.

If the number is already a perfect square, the root is an integer: √81 = 9. If the radicand has no square factor other than one, it remains as it is: √7 cannot be simplified further. The decimal approximation is useful for checking magnitude, but the exact radical is the preferred form when the question asks for simplification.

Combining like surds in practice

Only like surds can be combined. Like surds have the same simplified number under the root. This is similar to collecting like algebraic terms: 3x + 5x = 8x, but 3x + 5y cannot be combined into one term. Therefore 2√3 + 7√3 = 9√3. However, √2 + √3 must remain as two terms because the radicands are different.

Simplify each radical before deciding whether terms are alike. Consider 2√12 − √27. Since √12 = 2√3 and √27 = 3√3, the expression becomes 4√3 − 3√3 = √3. If you try to combine the original radicands directly, you may miss the fact that the terms are like after simplification. Coefficients outside the root are multiplied by the factor taken out during simplification.

Negative coefficients are allowed in addition and subtraction. For example, 5√2 − 8√2 = −3√2. The result is negative because the second term is larger. Do not subtract the numbers inside the radicals. In general, √a + √b is not equal to √(a+b), and √a − √b is not equal to √(a−b).

Rationalising a denominator step by step

Rationalising means rewriting a fraction so that its denominator contains no surd. For a denominator with one square root, multiply the numerator and denominator by that same root. For example, 3/√5 becomes 3√5/5. The value has not changed because the fraction was multiplied by √5/√5, which equals one.

When the denominator contains a sum or difference such as b+c√d, use its conjugate b−c√d. The product of conjugates follows the difference-of-two-squares identity: (b+c√d)(b−c√d) = b²−c²d. The radical terms cancel. For example, 1/(2+√3) is multiplied by (2−√3)/(2−√3). The denominator becomes 4−3 = 1, so the expression is 2−√3.

Before rationalising, check whether the denominator is zero. If b²−c²d equals zero, the original denominator may be zero and the fraction is undefined. The calculator checks this condition. It also simplifies the square root in the denominator first, which can make the final expression shorter and easier to read.

Worked examples

Simplifying a larger root

Simplify √200. Since 200 = 100×2, √200 = 10√2. The decimal value is approximately 14.1421. Check that 10√2 squared is 100×2 = 200, so the exact expression is correct.

Combining unlike-looking terms

Simplify 3√8 + 2√18. First, √8 = 2√2, so 3√8 = 6√2. Next, √18 = 3√2, so 2√18 = 6√2. The sum is 12√2, approximately 16.9706. The key is to simplify both roots before collecting the coefficients.

Rationalising a binomial denominator

Simplify 5/(3+√2). Multiply by the conjugate: 5(3−√2)/[(3+√2)(3−√2)]. The denominator is 9−2 = 7. The exact result is (15−5√2)/7. A decimal can be obtained afterwards, but the rationalised exact form is clearer for algebraic work.

Square roots, negative numbers and the calculator's scope

This tool is designed for square-root surds with whole-number inputs. It does not simplify cube roots or arbitrary algebraic expressions. In the real number system, the square root of a negative number is not real. If a problem involves √−1 or complex numbers, it belongs to a complex-number calculation rather than the ordinary real surd operations on this page.

For simplification, the radicand must be a positive whole number. For rationalising, the calculator uses the form a/(b+c√d), with whole-number parameters and a positive d. For addition or subtraction, it uses a√b ± c√d. These input forms are intentionally specific: they let the calculator show exact working rather than trying to interpret every possible expression typed in free-form algebra notation.

Common mistakes and how to check your work

  • Adding inside the root: √2 + √3 is not √5. Only like simplified surds can be combined.
  • Stopping too early: 2√18 is not fully simplified because √18 = 3√2.
  • Forgetting the coefficient: 4√12 becomes 8√3, not 4√3, because √12 contributes a factor of 2.
  • Using the wrong conjugate: the conjugate changes the sign between the two terms but keeps their magnitudes.
  • Changing only the denominator: multiply the numerator and denominator by the same expression so the fraction's value remains unchanged.
  • Rounding before combining: exact radicals avoid small errors that can accumulate when decimals are rounded.
  • Ignoring a zero denominator: a fraction with denominator zero is undefined, even if a symbolic manipulation seems to cancel terms.

A useful check for a simplified root is to square the result. If √200 becomes 10√2, squaring 10√2 gives 200. For a rationalised expression, multiply the final numerator and denominator back conceptually or compare decimal values before and after rationalisation. For sums, evaluate both forms to a few decimal places as a check, while keeping the exact form as the final answer.

Why exact surds appear in geometry

Surds are common when a right triangle has side lengths that do not form a Pythagorean triple. If the perpendicular sides are 2 and 3, the hypotenuse is √(2²+3²) = √13. The exact length is √13 units, while a calculator may show approximately 3.6056 units. Writing 3.61 is useful for measurement, but it loses exactness. In algebra and geometry proofs, the radical form is often the expected answer.

Another example is the diagonal of a rectangle measuring 5 by 7. Pythagoras gives √(25+49) = √74. Since 74 has no square factor greater than one, this is already simplest form. The Surd Calculator can simplify square roots and provide a decimal check, while the Area Calculator and Slope Calculator support related geometry calculations.

Choosing between an exact and decimal answer

Use the exact surd when the question asks for simplest radical form, when you need to continue algebra, or when an exact comparison matters. Use a decimal when the problem asks for a measured value, an approximate length or a numerical estimate. If you convert too early, a rounded value can create a small difference in later steps. A good habit is to keep the radical through the working and round only the final result to the number of decimal places or significant figures requested.

For example, the exact diagonal of a unit square is √2. If you use 1.414 as an approximation in a later calculation, the result may differ slightly from the exact value. That difference is not a mistake in the formula; it comes from rounding. Exact notation makes the source of the value clear and preserves all the mathematical information.

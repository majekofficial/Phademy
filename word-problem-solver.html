From a sentence to an equation

Word problems are hard because the maths is hidden inside a story. The Word Problem Solver covers seven common types. Choose the type that matches your problem, fill in the numbers and it shows the answer and the working that turns the story into an equation.

The seven problem types

Ages

The older person is a times the younger now, and m times the younger after n years. If the younger is y years old, then ay + n = m(y + n), so y = n(m − 1) ÷ (a − m).

  • Example: Ada is 3 times as old as Bola. In 4 years she will be 2 times as old. Then y = 4 × 1 ÷ 1 = 4, so Bola is 4 and Ada is 12. Check: in 4 years they are 16 and 8.
  • Another: a father is 4 times his son's age now and 3 times in 5 years: the son is 10 and the father 40.

Consecutive numbers

For n consecutive numbers with sum S, the first is (S − n(n − 1) ÷ 2) ÷ n. Three consecutive numbers that add to 63 are 20, 21 and 22; four that add to 10 are 1, 2, 3 and 4.

Sharing in a ratio

Add the parts, divide the total by that sum to get one part, and multiply. Sharing 240 in the ratio 3:2:1 gives 120, 80 and 40. Sharing 900 as 2:3 gives 360 and 540.

Two travellers

Choose whether they move towards each other or in the same direction. Towards each other, the gap closes at the sum of the speeds: 300 km apart at 60 km/h and 40 km/h meet after 300 ÷ 100 = 3 hours. In the same direction, the faster one gains at the difference of the speeds.

Working together

Add the rates. If one person takes 6 days and another 12, together they do 1/6 + 1/12 = 1/4 of the job a day, so they finish in 4 days.

Rectangle with a perimeter

Choose whether the length is a certain amount more than the width or a multiple of it. With a perimeter of 40 and the length 4 more than the width, 2(w + w + 4) = 40 gives width 8, length 12 and area 96. If the length is 3 times the width, then 8w = 40, so the width is 5 and the length 15.

Two items with a total count and total cost

Pens cost 50 and books cost 200, and 30 items cost 3,600 in all. Let p be pens and b books: p + b = 30 and 50p + 200b = 3,600, so b = 14 and p = 16.

A method for any word problem

  1. Read twice and underline the quantities you know and the one you need.
  2. Name the unknown with a letter and write what it stands for.
  3. Translate each sentence into an equation.
  4. Solve, then put the answer back into the story to check it.
  5. Write the answer with its unit.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the wrong type, for example using a ratio when the problem is about rates.
  • Mixing units, such as minutes and hours.
  • Forgetting that “times as old” and “more than” are different relationships.
  • Giving a half-person or a negative age. A sensible check catches errors.

Why translating words into mathematics matters

A word problem describes a situation in ordinary language and asks you to find a quantity that is not directly stated. The challenge is not always the arithmetic. It is deciding what the unknown represents, identifying the relationship between the quantities and writing an equation that matches the story. A reliable method is to read the question carefully, list the known values, define the unknown, translate the relationships, solve, and then check whether the answer makes sense in context.

This solver is organised around seven common school problem types: ages, consecutive numbers, sharing in a ratio, two travellers, working together, rectangles with a perimeter, and two items with a total count and total cost. Selecting the correct type is essential because each type uses a different mathematical model. The tool supplies the equation and working for the selected pattern; it is not a general natural-language system that can solve every possible paragraph automatically.

Age problems: use a timeline

Age questions often compare two people now and at a future or past time. The important fact is that both people's ages change by the same number of years. If a person is x years old now, in five years the person will be x+5, and five years ago the person was x−5. A common mistake is to multiply the age by the number of years rather than add or subtract the elapsed time.

Suppose an older person is three times the younger person's age now, and in four years the older person will be twice the younger person's age. Let the younger person's present age be x. The older person's present age is 3x. In four years, the ages are x+4 and 3x+4. The condition becomes 3x+4 = 2(x+4), so 3x+4 = 2x+8 and x = 4. The younger person is four and the older person is twelve. Check the future ages: eight and sixteen, and sixteen is twice eight.

Age answers should normally be non-negative and realistic. If an equation produces a negative age, revisit the translation and the stated time relationship. A phrase such as “three times as old now” is not the same as “three years older”. One is multiplication; the other is addition.

Consecutive-number problems

Consecutive integers follow one another without gaps. If the first integer is x, the next two are x+1 and x+2. For four consecutive integers, write x, x+1, x+2 and x+3. If the problem gives their sum, add the expressions and set the total equal to the stated value. For three consecutive integers with a sum of 72, the equation is x+(x+1)+(x+2)=72. This simplifies to 3x+3=72, so x=23 and the numbers are 23, 24 and 25.

Consecutive even integers differ by two, not one. If the first is x, the next are x+2 and x+4. Consecutive odd integers can be represented in the same way. Read the word “integer” carefully: an integer is a whole number that may be negative, zero or positive. If the context is counting people or objects, the values must be non-negative whole numbers.

Ratio sharing: find the value of one part

A ratio compares quantities in parts. If an amount is shared in the ratio 2:3, the total number of parts is 2+3 = 5. Divide the total amount by five to find the value of one part, then multiply by two and three. For a total of 500, one part is 100, so the shares are 200 and 300. Always add the ratio terms before dividing the total.

Ratios do not necessarily describe the actual quantities directly. A ratio of 2:3 means the first quantity is two equal parts and the second is three equal parts. It does not mean the quantities are simply 2 and 3 unless the one-part value is one. If the total is money, keep the currency unit in the final shares. If the quantities represent people or objects, check that the results are whole numbers when the context requires whole items.

Two-traveller problems: direction changes the equation

For two travellers moving towards each other, their separation closes at the sum of their speeds. If they begin 240 km apart and travel towards each other at 50 km/h and 30 km/h, the closing speed is 80 km/h. Time to meet is distance divided by closing speed: 240/80 = 3 hours. The first traveller covers 150 km and the second covers 90 km, which add to the original 240 km.

If two travellers move in the same direction, the gap closes at the difference between their speeds, provided the faster traveller is behind and catching up. If a car travelling at 90 km/h follows a vehicle travelling at 70 km/h with a 40 km gap, the relative speed is 20 km/h and the catch-up time is 40/20 = 2 hours. Before using the difference, confirm which traveller is faster and whether the faster one is actually closing the gap.

Units must match. If one speed is in metres per second and another in kilometres per hour, convert them before adding or subtracting. The answer in hours is only appropriate when distance is in kilometres and speed in kilometres per hour.

Work-together problems: add rates, not times

When two workers or machines complete the same job, add the fractions of the job each completes per unit time. If one person finishes a task in six days, the person's rate is 1/6 of the job per day. If another finishes in twelve days, the rate is 1/12 per day. Together their rate is 1/6+1/12 = 1/4 of the job per day, so the job takes four days.

A frequent error is to add the completion times and say that the task takes eighteen days, or to average six and twelve and say nine days. Neither approach describes simultaneous work. The combined rate is faster than either individual rate, so the combined time should be less than the fastest individual time. This is a useful reasonableness check.

Rectangle perimeter problems

The perimeter of a rectangle is P = 2(l+w), where l is length and w is width. If the length is four units more than the width, write l = w+4. Substitute into the perimeter equation: P = 2[w+(w+4)]. If the length is three times the width, write l = 3w. The phrase “more than” usually indicates addition, while “times” indicates multiplication.

For a perimeter of 40 units with length four more than width, 40 = 2(w+w+4), so 20 = 2w+4, 16 = 2w and w = 8. The length is 12. The area is 8×12 = 96 square units. Check the perimeter: 2(8+12)=40. Do not confuse perimeter, which is a length around the boundary, with area, which measures the surface inside.

Two-item count and cost problems

These questions give a total number of items and a total cost, with two different item prices. Let x be the number of the first item and y the number of the second. The total-count equation is x+y=N. The total-cost equation is p₁x+p₂y=M, where p₁ and p₂ are the unit prices and M is the total money. Solve the two equations together to find both counts.

For example, a customer buys 20 tickets. Adult tickets cost 500 each and child tickets cost 300 each. The total is 8,400. The equations are x+y=20 and 500x+300y=8,400. Subtract 300 times the first equation from the second: 200x=2,400, so x=12 and y=8. Check the total count, 12+8=20, and the cost, 12×500+8×300=8,400. If the calculated counts are negative or fractional but the story concerns whole tickets, the supplied totals may be inconsistent.

A general five-step method for unfamiliar problems

  1. Read for the question: identify exactly what the problem asks you to calculate. Do not begin solving before you know the target.
  2. List known information: write down numbers, units, relationships and any timing or direction conditions.
  3. Define the unknown: use a letter and state what it represents. If there are two unknowns, define both clearly.
  4. Build the equation: translate one relationship at a time. Use a quick sketch or table when the story involves ages, journeys or ratios.
  5. Solve and verify: substitute the answer back into the original statements and check units and context.

It is useful to write the equation before inserting all the numbers. For example, “distance = speed × time” is the general relationship. Once the variables are clear, substitute the known values. This reduces the chance of copying a number into the wrong place and makes the method easier to explain in an exam.

How to recognise misleading wording

Words such as “more than”, “less than”, “twice”, “three times”, “per”, “each”, “together” and “remaining” signal different operations. “Five more than x” is x+5, while “five less than x” is x−5. “x less than five” is 5−x, which reverses the order. “Twice x” is 2x, while “x squared” is x². Translate the full phrase rather than relying on a single keyword.

In rate problems, “per hour” means a quantity divided by time. In ratio problems, the parts describe a comparison. In cost problems, price multiplied by quantity gives total cost. These relationships may all involve multiplication or division, but they model different situations. Drawing a small table with quantity, rate and total can make the structure clearer.

Common mistakes and answer checks

  • Using the wrong model: decide whether the story is about ages, consecutive values, ratio parts, travel rates, work rates, rectangle dimensions or item counts and prices.
  • Mixing up “more than” and “times”: addition and multiplication describe different relationships.
  • Adding times instead of rates: simultaneous workers contribute fractions of a job per unit time.
  • Ignoring direction: travellers moving towards each other use a sum of speeds; catch-up in the same direction uses a difference.
  • Forgetting units: hours, minutes, kilometres, metres and currency should remain consistent.
  • Accepting impossible values: negative people, fractional tickets or a negative age usually indicate inconsistent data or an incorrect equation.
  • Skipping the check: place the result back into the original conditions and verify every total.

Practice questions

  1. A mother is four times her child's age. In six years she will be twice the child's age. Answer: child is 6 and mother is 24 now; in six years they are 12 and 30, so this example actually does not satisfy twice. Recheck the equation: 4x+6=2(x+6), giving x=3. Child is 3 and mother is 12.
  2. Three consecutive integers add to 51. Answer: 16, 17 and 18.
  3. Share 720 in the ratio 5:3. Answer: eight parts, 90 per part, so 450 and 270.
  4. Two cyclists are 90 km apart and ride towards each other at 20 km/h and 25 km/h. Answer: 90/45 = 2 hours.
  5. One worker takes 8 days and another takes 8 days. Answer: their combined rate is 1/8+1/8=1/4 job per day, so 4 days.
  6. A rectangle has perimeter 54 cm and length is 3 cm more than width. Answer: width 12 cm and length 15 cm.

Simplify expressions, expand brackets, factorise and solve equations

Algebra uses letters to represent numbers and relationships. An algebra solver can help you simplify an expression, expand brackets, factorise a polynomial or solve an equation for a chosen variable. This tool accepts expressions and equations, shows working, and supports common linear, quadratic and cubic problems in one variable as well as rearranging supported equations with several variables.

Use the calculator as a way to check your reasoning and study the steps. It is more useful to compare the working with your own attempt than to copy a final answer without understanding why it is correct. Algebra is built on operations that preserve equality, so every step should be mathematically justified.

How to enter algebra correctly

Type an expression into the input field. Use ^ for powers, such as x^2, or the superscript form x². Use brackets to group terms, and enter fractions with a slash, such as 1/2. A number beside a letter means multiplication, so 5x means 5 × x and 5xy means 5 × x × y.

Use an equals sign when you want to solve an equation. Without an equals sign, the solver treats the input as an expression to expand, simplify and factorise. If the equation contains more than one letter, choose the variable you want to solve for. Enter one equation at a time; systems of simultaneous equations belong in the Simultaneous Equations Calculator.

Expressions and equations are different

An expression is a mathematical phrase such as 3x + 5 or (x + 2)(x − 1). It does not state that one side equals another. An equation includes an equals sign, such as 3x + 5 = 20, and asks for values that make both sides equal.

You can simplify an expression without solving for a value of x. For example, 2x + 3x − 4 + 9 combines like terms to give 5x + 5. To solve an equation, you need a condition such as 5x + 5 = 20. Subtract 5 from both sides and divide by 5 to obtain x = 3.

Collecting like terms

Like terms have the same variable part raised to the same powers. You can combine 2x and 3x to make 5x, but you cannot combine 2x and 3x² into one term because their powers differ. Constants can be combined with other constants. For example, 4a + 7 − 2a + 3 becomes 2a + 10.

Think of the coefficient as the number of copies of a term. Two apples plus three apples make five apples, just as 2x + 3x = 5x. But two apples plus three oranges cannot be combined into five apples. This simple idea helps explain why only matching variable terms can be collected.

Expanding brackets using the distributive law

To expand a bracket, multiply the term outside by every term inside. The distributive law states a(b + c) = ab + ac. For example, 3(x + 4) = 3x + 12. For two brackets, multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second.

Expand (x + 2)(x + 3) by multiplying x by x and 3, then 2 by x and 3. This gives x² + 3x + 2x + 6. Collect the like terms to obtain x² + 5x + 6. A frequent error is multiplying the first term in a bracket but forgetting to multiply the remaining terms.

Factorising: reverse the expansion

Factorising writes an expression as a product of simpler factors. It reverses expansion. For example, x² + 5x + 6 becomes (x + 2)(x + 3). To find the pair for a monic quadratic, look for two numbers whose product is 6 and whose sum is 5. The numbers are 2 and 3.

A common factor can be taken outside brackets. In 6x + 9, both terms are divisible by 3, so the expression becomes 3(2x + 3). Factorising is useful for solving equations, simplifying algebraic fractions and identifying roots. Always expand your factorised answer to check that it returns the original expression.

Special factorisation patterns

The difference of two squares follows a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b). For example, x² − 9 = (x − 3)(x + 3). The sum and difference of cubes also have standard patterns. The difference of two cubes is a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²). Thus x³ − 8 = (x − 2)(x² + 2x + 4).

Recognising these patterns can save time, but the signs must be handled carefully. The expression x² + 9 is not a difference of squares over the real numbers. Do not change a plus sign into a minus sign simply to force a familiar pattern.

Solving a linear equation

A linear equation has a variable whose highest power is one. The goal is to isolate the variable while doing the same operation to both sides. For 3x + 5 = 20, subtract 5 from both sides to get 3x = 15. Divide both sides by 3 to obtain x = 5.

Check by substitution: 3(5) + 5 = 15 + 5 = 20. The original left and right sides match, so the solution is correct. If you multiply or divide one side, you must do the same to the other side. Changing only one side changes the equation.

Solving a quadratic equation

A quadratic equation has a squared variable. For x² − 9 = 0, factorise to (x − 3)(x + 3) = 0. The zero-product property says that if a product equals zero, at least one factor must equal zero. Therefore x − 3 = 0 or x + 3 = 0, giving x = 3 or x = −3.

Not every quadratic factorises easily. The quadratic formula can solve the general form ax² + bx + c = 0. Use the dedicated Quadratic Equation Calculator when you also want the discriminant, vertex, factorised form and a graph of the parabola.

Equations with more than one letter

An equation such as 5x + 3y − 6 = 0 contains two variables. If you are asked to make x the subject, treat y as a quantity that remains in the expression. Add 6 to both sides to get 5x + 3y = 6, subtract 3y to get 5x = 6 − 3y, and divide by 5. The result is x = (6 − 3y) ÷ 5.

This is a rearrangement, not a single numerical solution, because y has not been assigned a value. If you are given enough independent equations to determine both x and y, use a simultaneous-equation method instead. If the task is to rearrange a physical or mathematical formula, the Change of Subject Calculator is also relevant.

What does a complex solution mean?

Some equations have no solution among real numbers but do have solutions in the complex number system. For example, x² + 1 = 0 implies x² = −1. No real number squared gives −1, but the imaginary unit i is defined by i² = −1. The complex roots are x = i and x = −i.

A message saying there are no real solutions does not mean that mathematics has failed. It identifies the number system in which the solution is being considered. Pay attention to whether a question asks for real roots or allows complex numbers.

Checking your working

For an equation with a numerical answer, substitute the answer into the original equation and compare both sides. For an expression simplification, expand or reverse the operations to see whether the original form is recovered. If you factorise a polynomial, multiply the factors back together. These checks catch sign errors, missing terms and incorrect distribution.

Read each displayed step rather than relying only on the final result. If the solver gives a result you did not expect, compare the exact expression you entered with the question. A missing bracket, a typed x2 instead of x^2, or a negative sign in the wrong place can change the entire problem.

Common algebra mistakes

  • Writing x2 instead of x^2: x2 is interpreted as x multiplied by 2, not x squared.
  • Incorrect distribution: 2(x + 4) is 2x + 8, not 2x + 4.
  • Combining unlike terms: 3x + 2x² cannot be simplified to 5x³ or 5x².
  • Changing a sign incorrectly: moving a term across the equals sign is shorthand for performing the same operation on both sides.
  • Dividing by a variable without checking zero: dividing by x can discard a possible solution where x = 0.
  • Entering multiple equations as one: use a system solver for simultaneous equations.
  • Skipping the check: substitution is a quick way to verify a proposed solution.

Equivalence and the rules of algebra

Two expressions are equivalent if they have the same value for every allowed value of their variables. For example, 2(x + 3) and 2x + 6 are equivalent because the distributive law makes them equal for every x. Two equations are equivalent when they have the same solution set. This idea explains why the same valid operation must be applied to both sides of an equation.

Adding the same number to both sides, subtracting the same number, multiplying both sides by the same non-zero number, or dividing both sides by the same non-zero number preserves equality. Squaring both sides or multiplying by an expression containing a variable can introduce extra solutions or remove possible ones, so those steps require a check in the original equation.

Why dividing by a variable can lose a solution

Consider x² = 3x. Moving everything to one side gives x² − 3x = 0, which factorises to x(x − 3) = 0. The solutions are x = 0 and x = 3. If you divide both sides of the original equation by x, you obtain x = 3, but that step assumes x is not zero and loses the valid solution x = 0.

This is why it is important to factorise and use the zero-product property rather than divide by a variable without checking whether it could equal zero. After solving, substitute every candidate into the original equation to make sure it is valid.

Use brackets to show the intended meaning

Brackets are especially important when a fraction or a negative value is involved. The expression (x + 2)/3 means the entire sum is divided by three, while x + 2/3 means only the 2 is divided by three. Similarly, (−2)² is 4, but −2² is −4 under standard order of operations. Clear brackets make the expression easier to read and reduce input errors.

If a typed expression gives an unexpected result, rewrite it with explicit multiplication signs and brackets. For example, enter 2*(x+4) rather than relying on a complicated chain of implied multiplication. The calculator accepts common shorthand, but clear notation is a good habit for exams and written solutions.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between simplifying and solving?

Simplifying rewrites an expression in a cleaner equivalent form. Solving finds the value or values of a variable that make an equation true.

What is the difference between expanding and factorising?

Expanding multiplies brackets into a sum of terms. Factorising rewrites a sum as a product of factors.

Can I use fractions and decimals?

Yes. Enter fractions with a slash, such as 1/2, and use ordinary decimal notation where needed.

Can it solve equations with x and y?

It can rearrange a supported equation for a selected variable, leaving the other variables in the expression. To find numerical values for several unknowns, use simultaneous equations.

Why does the solver show complex roots?

Some polynomial equations have no real-number solutions. Complex roots use the imaginary unit i, where i² = −1.

How do I know whether the answer is correct?

Substitute a numerical solution into the original equation. For a factorisation, expand the factors and compare with the starting expression.

What a discount actually changes

A discount reduces a listed price by a stated amount or percentage. A shop may advertise 15% off, a supplier may offer a bulk discount, or an online store may show a limited-time promotion. The percentage is easy to notice, but the useful questions are more practical: how much money will you save, what will you pay, and is the final price genuinely good value?

This calculator takes an original price and a discount percentage. It reports two figures: the amount removed from the original price and the price left to pay. It is a simple percentage calculation, not a shopping comparison tool. It does not check whether the advertised original price is fair, add delivery charges, or apply tax.

The discount formulas

discount amount = original price × discount rate ÷ 100

sale price = original price − discount amount

The original price is the base. To turn a percentage into a decimal multiplier, divide it by 100. For example, 15% becomes 0.15. If an item costs ₦24,000, a 15% discount is ₦24,000 × 0.15 = ₦3,600. Subtract the saving from the original price: ₦24,000 − ₦3,600 = ₦20,400.

There is also a one-step way to calculate the amount payable:

sale price = original price × (1 − discount rate ÷ 100)

For 15% off, the part you still pay is 85%, or 0.85. Therefore ₦24,000 × 0.85 = ₦20,400. Both methods produce the same result. The first method is useful when you want to see the saving separately; the second is convenient when you only need the sale price.

Worked examples

Example 1: A straightforward shop discount

A pair of shoes is marked ₦18,000, with 20% off.

₦18,000 × 20 ÷ 100 = ₦3,600 saved

₦18,000 − ₦3,600 = ₦14,400 to pay

The discount is ₦3,600, not ₦20, and the final price is ₦14,400.

Example 2: A small percentage on an expensive item

A laptop costs ₦850,000 and has a 7.5% promotional reduction.

₦850,000 × 7.5 ÷ 100 = ₦63,750

₦850,000 − ₦63,750 = ₦786,250

A percentage that looks small can still represent a large amount when the starting price is high.

Example 3: A price with decimal places

An item costs 49.99 and is reduced by 15%.

49.99 × 0.15 = 7.4985 saved

49.99 − 7.4985 = 42.4915

Displayed to two decimal places, the saving is about 7.50 and the sale price is about 42.49. A shop may use a different rounding rule at the till, so a one-cent difference can occur.

Example 4: Half price

A ₦12,500 item has a 50% discount. Half of ₦12,500 is ₦6,250, so the buyer pays ₦6,250. A 50% discount always leaves half of the original price.

Example 5: No discount and a full discount

At 0% off, the saving is zero and the original price remains payable. At 100% off, the saving equals the original price and the sale price becomes zero. These are useful boundary checks when learning the formula.

How to calculate a discount mentally

Some common percentages are easy to work out without a calculator. Ten per cent is one tenth of the price, so move the decimal point one place to the left. Five per cent is half of 10%. Twenty per cent is twice 10%. Twenty-five per cent is one quarter, and 50% is one half.

For a ₦40,000 item, 10% is ₦4,000, 5% is ₦2,000, 20% is ₦8,000 and 25% is ₦10,000. A 15% discount can be found by adding 10% and 5%: ₦4,000 + ₦2,000 = ₦6,000. The final price is ₦34,000.

For less familiar percentages, calculate 1% first by dividing the price by 100, then multiply that result by the percentage. For example, 3% of ₦70,000 is ₦700 × 3 = ₦2,100.

Finding the original price from a sale price

Sometimes the label shows only the reduced price and the discount rate. You can work backwards if you know both. First find the percentage of the original price that remains. With 25% off, 75% remains. Convert 75% to 0.75 and divide the sale price by it.

original price = sale price ÷ (1 − discount rate ÷ 100)

A jacket costs ₦30,000 after a 25% discount. The buyer paid 75% of the original price.

₦30,000 ÷ 0.75 = ₦40,000 original price

This reverse calculation is not possible when the discount is 100%, because a zero sale price does not reveal the original amount. It is also important to know whether the displayed sale price includes other charges.

Understanding successive discounts

Two discounts applied one after another are not normally added together. The second discount is calculated on the already reduced price. Suppose a ₦200,000 appliance receives 20% off, followed by another 10% off the new price. The first reduction is ₦40,000, leaving ₦160,000. Ten per cent of ₦160,000 is ₦16,000, leaving ₦144,000.

The total saving is ₦56,000. Compared with the original ₦200,000, that is 28%, not 30%. The second reduction is smaller because it is taken from a smaller base. In general, a reduction of a% followed by b% leaves this fraction of the original price:

remaining fraction = (1 − a ÷ 100) × (1 − b ÷ 100)

For 20% and then 10%, this is 0.80 × 0.90 = 0.72. The customer pays 72% of the starting price and therefore saves 28%.

Discounts, tax, delivery and the real checkout total

The calculator isolates the discount. A real purchase may also include delivery, service charges or tax. Check the order of operations shown by the seller. If tax is added after the discount, calculate the sale price first and then apply the tax rate to that amount. If a fee is fixed, add it after calculating the discounted price.

An item costs ₦50,000, has 10% off, and delivery costs ₦2,000. The discount is ₦5,000, so the item costs ₦45,000 before delivery. The amount before any applicable tax is ₦45,000 + ₦2,000 = ₦47,000.

Do not assume every seller calculates tax in the same way. Tax rules depend on the product, location and seller. This tool does not determine tax liability. For a separate percentage-based tax estimate, use the Tax Calculator. If you need to compare a percentage with another amount, the Percentage Calculator can help.

How to decide whether the offer is worthwhile

  • Check the normal market price. Compare the item with other sellers. A large percentage off an inflated starting price may still be a poor deal.
  • Compare the same specification. Check model, size, quantity, warranty, condition and included accessories. A cheaper version may not be equivalent.
  • Look at unit price. Divide the total cost by the number of items, grams, litres or other units. A discounted large pack is not automatically cheaper per unit.
  • Include every unavoidable fee. Delivery, handling and payment charges can reduce the value of the promotion.
  • Check the terms. Some discounts exclude selected products, require a minimum spend or cannot be combined with vouchers.
  • Separate need from urgency. A countdown banner does not make an unnecessary purchase valuable. A saving is useful only if the purchase fits your needs and budget.

Common discount-calculation mistakes

  • Subtracting the percentage as a cash amount. A 20% discount means 20 out of every 100 of the price, not simply subtracting 20 currency units.
  • Using the sale price as the base. The advertised discount is usually calculated from the original price, unless the offer states otherwise.
  • Entering 0.2 for 20%. This calculator expects the percentage number, so enter 20 for 20% and 0.2 for 0.2%.
  • Adding successive percentages. Apply each reduction to the new price rather than adding the rates.
  • Ignoring rounding. Keep a few decimal places during working and round the final displayed amount according to the currency's normal smallest unit.
  • Assuming the result is the checkout total. The tool does not add tax, delivery or other charges.

What this calculator does and does not do

Enter the original price in the first field and the discount percentage in the second. The result reports the saving and the amount remaining. The calculation is based on the two numbers you provide. It does not verify the advertised price, decide whether a promotion is genuine, compare shops, convert currencies or calculate tax.

For an ordinary discount, use a rate from 0% to 100%. A percentage outside that range may produce a mathematical result, but it is not a typical retail discount and should be checked before relying on it. The calculator also does not know whether the seller applies the discount before or after other charges.

Practice questions

  1. A bag costs ₦16,000 and is discounted by 15%. What is the saving and sale price?
  2. A phone accessory costs ₦7,500 with 8% off. How much does the buyer pay before delivery?
  3. A product sells for ₦54,000 after a 10% discount. What was its original price?
  4. A ₦100,000 item receives 20% off and then 5% off the reduced price. What is the final price and total percentage saved?

Answers: 1. ₦2,400 saved; ₦13,600 to pay. 2. ₦600 saved; ₦6,900 to pay. 3. ₦60,000, because ₦54,000 ÷ 0.90 = ₦60,000. 4. First reduction leaves ₦80,000; the next saves ₦4,000, leaving ₦76,000. Total saving is ₦24,000, or 24%.

Discounts in business and bulk purchases

Discounts are not limited to retail sale signs. A supplier may reduce the unit price when a customer orders a larger quantity, while a service provider may offer a reduced rate for an annual commitment. The arithmetic is the same when one percentage applies to the full eligible amount. First identify the amount that qualifies for the discount, then apply the rate to that amount. Do not assume that delivery, installation, optional extras or excluded products receive the same reduction.

A small business orders 20 identical items at 5,000 each. The goods total 20 × 5,000 = 100,000. If the supplier offers 8% off the goods only, the reduction is 8,000 and the goods subtotal becomes 92,000. If delivery is 3,500 and is not discounted, the pre-tax total is 95,500.

When different quantities have different price bands, calculate each band according to the supplier's terms. A bulk discount may apply to every unit once a threshold is reached, or only to the units above that threshold. Those arrangements are not equivalent. The calculator handles one original amount and one percentage; it cannot interpret a tiered pricing contract.

Discount amount versus percentage points

A discount percentage describes a fraction of a price. A percentage-point change describes the difference between two percentages. For example, a promotion increasing from 10% off to 15% off is an increase of 5 percentage points in the discount rate. It is not automatically a 5% reduction in the amount the customer pays. On a 100,000 item, 10% off leaves 90,000, while 15% off leaves 85,000. The second offer reduces the payable amount by a further 5,000 compared with the original price.

This distinction is useful when comparing promotions over time. Always translate the advertised rate into the actual amount payable using the same starting price, rather than relying on the wording of the promotion alone.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate 20% off a price?

Multiply the original price by 0.20 to find the saving, then subtract it. Or multiply the original price by 0.80 to get the amount payable directly.

Is 50% off the same as half price?

Yes. A 50% reduction removes half of the original price, leaving the other half to pay.

Can I use this for a discount on several items?

You can use it for a combined original price if the same percentage applies to every item. If different products have different discount rates, calculate each line separately and add the results.

Does the calculator include VAT or delivery?

No. It calculates only the discount amount and reduced price. Add other charges separately according to the seller's checkout rules.

Why can my receipt differ by a small amount?

Businesses may round each line, round the total, or apply tax at a different stage. Small differences can result from those methods.

Is a bigger discount always better?

No. Compare the final price, product quality, quantity and fees with alternatives. The percentage alone does not show whether the offer is good value.

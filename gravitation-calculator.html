Newton’s law of universal gravitation and gravitational field strength

Gravity is the attractive force between objects that have mass. It keeps the Moon in orbit around Earth, holds planets in their paths around the Sun and gives objects weight near a planet’s surface. The Phademy Gravitation Calculator covers two connected relationships: the gravitational force between two masses and the gravitational field strength produced by a large mass at a given distance. Both depend on the universal gravitational constant and an inverse-square relationship, so distance must be measured from the centres of the masses.

Newton’s law of universal gravitation

Newton’s law states that every pair of masses attracts one another. The magnitude of the force is F = Gm₁m₂/r². The symbols m₁ and m₂ represent the two masses in kilograms, r is the separation between their centres in metres, and G is the universal gravitational constant. Its commonly used value is approximately 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg².

The equation shows that gravitational force is directly proportional to each mass. If one mass doubles while the other mass and separation remain unchanged, the force doubles. If both masses double, the force becomes four times as large. This proportional reasoning is often quicker than recalculating from the beginning and is useful for checking answers.

The distance appears squared in the denominator. This means gravity follows an inverse-square law: the force decreases rapidly as separation increases. The relevant distance is not the gap between the surfaces. For spherical bodies, use the distance between their centres. If an object is at height h above a planet’s surface, the centre-to-centre distance is the planet’s radius plus h.

The inverse-square law

If the distance between two objects changes from r to 2r, the new force is one quarter of the original force because the denominator becomes (2r)² = 4r². If distance becomes three times as large, force becomes one ninth. If distance is halved, the force becomes four times as large. The masses and G have not changed in these comparisons.

This relationship explains why a satellite farther from Earth experiences a weaker gravitational attraction than an identical satellite closer to Earth. It also explains why the gravitational field strength decreases with altitude. The force does not suddenly stop at the edge of the atmosphere; gravity acts over very large distances, although its strength becomes weaker.

When comparing two situations, write a ratio rather than inserting large numbers. For example, F₂/F₁ = (r₁/r₂)² if the masses are unchanged. Ratios help you avoid calculator rounding and make the physical relationship clear in an exam explanation.

Gravitational field strength

Gravitational field strength describes the gravitational force experienced per unit mass at a point. For a spherical planet or star, g = GM/r², where M is the mass of the attracting body and r is the distance from its centre. Its SI unit is N/kg. This is numerically equivalent to m/s², the acceleration of a freely falling object when gravity is the only significant force.

Field strength is a property of the gravitational field at a location. It does not depend on the mass of a small test object. A 1 kg object and a 10 kg object at the same location experience the same field strength, although the larger mass experiences ten times the gravitational force. This follows from F = mg.

Near Earth’s surface, g is commonly approximated as 9.8 N/kg or 9.8 m/s². The value changes slightly with altitude, latitude and local geological conditions. The calculator’s spherical-mass model gives an idealised value based on the supplied mass and centre distance. It is suitable for many school physics calculations.

Distinguishing G from g

The symbols G and g are easy to confuse, but they describe different things. Capital G is the universal gravitational constant. It has the same value throughout the universe in the standard Newtonian model and appears in the gravitational force and field equations. Lowercase g is the gravitational field strength at a particular location and depends on the mass distribution and distance.

For Earth, g near the surface is about 9.8 N/kg, while G is about 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg². Their numerical values and units are very different. Do not substitute 9.8 for G or 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ for g. A useful memory aid is that G is a constant in the law, while g is the local field value you calculate from the law.

Weight is calculated using W = mg. If a 60 kg person is in a region where g = 9.8 N/kg, their weight is approximately 588 N. Their mass remains 60 kg if they travel to the Moon, but their weight changes because the Moon’s gravitational field is weaker.

Scientific notation and units

Planetary masses and astronomical distances are very large, while G is extremely small. Scientific notation keeps these values readable. For example, 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg can be entered as 5.97e24, and 6.37 × 10⁶ m as 6.37e6. The letter e means “times ten to the power of”. Thus 3.2e-4 represents 3.2 × 10⁻⁴.

Use kilograms for mass and metres for distance. If a distance is given in kilometres, multiply by 1,000 before using the equation. If the problem gives a height above a planet’s surface, add the planet’s radius to obtain the distance from its centre. Squaring a kilometre value without converting it to metres produces a result with the wrong scale.

Gravitational force is measured in newtons, while field strength is measured in N/kg. Check that your final unit matches the quantity requested. A force answer cannot be reported as N/kg, and a field strength answer should not be labelled simply N.

Assumptions behind the school-level model

Newton’s law is highly successful for many everyday and astronomical calculations, but the simple calculator assumes the masses can be treated as point masses or spherically symmetric bodies. For a spherical body outside it, its gravitational effect is equivalent to having its mass concentrated at the centre. This makes centre-to-centre distance the correct input.

For irregular bodies or locations close to a complicated mass distribution, the field may not be represented accurately by one centre distance and one total mass. General relativity is also needed for very strong gravitational fields or extremely precise descriptions of some astronomical systems. Those cases are beyond the scope of this school-level calculator.

The formula gives the magnitude of the attractive force. Direction is along the line joining the centres, with each object pulling the other towards itself. In a multi-body situation, gravitational forces from different objects must be combined as vectors. The two-mass calculator does not perform that full vector sum.

Why the centre distance and mass distribution matter

The centre-distance rule is especially important when the object is above a planet. Imagine a planet with radius R and a spacecraft at altitude h. The spacecraft is separated from the planet’s centre by R + h, not by h alone. Since distance is squared, using altitude alone can produce a very large error, particularly when the altitude is small compared with the planet’s radius.

The spherical approximation works because a spherically symmetric body attracts an external object as though all of its mass were concentrated at its centre. This is a powerful result for planets and many stars. For a non-spherical asteroid, a nearby mountain or a body with an uneven mass distribution, the field can depart from this simple model. The closer the test object is to irregular mass, the more important the detailed distribution can become.

For two separated spherical bodies that do not overlap, the centre-to-centre formula gives the force magnitude. The forces on the two bodies have equal magnitude and opposite directions, in accordance with Newton’s third law. The force acts along the line joining their centres. This action-reaction pair does not mean the bodies have equal accelerations: acceleration also depends on each body’s mass.

Worked examples

Force between two small masses

Two masses of 10 kg and 20 kg have centres 2 m apart. F = G × 10 × 20 / 2² = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ × 200 / 4 = 3.337 × 10⁻⁹ N. The force is extremely small, which is why attraction between ordinary objects is not noticeable in daily life.

Effect of doubling distance

Suppose the two masses remain unchanged but their separation increases from 2 m to 4 m. The distance has doubled, so force becomes 1/2² = 1/4 of its earlier value. The new force is 8.3425 × 10⁻¹⁰ N.

Field strength near Earth

Use Earth’s mass 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg and radius 6.37 × 10⁶ m. g = GM/r² ≈ (6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴)/(6.37 × 10⁶)² ≈ 9.82 N/kg. This is close to the familiar 9.8 N/kg approximation.

Field strength at altitude

A satellite is 400 km above Earth’s surface. Add 400,000 m to Earth’s approximate radius of 6.37 × 10⁶ m, giving r = 6.77 × 10⁶ m. Use this centre distance in g = GM/r². Do not enter 400,000 m alone, because that is altitude above the surface, not distance from Earth’s centre.

Force between Earth and a person

For a 70 kg person at Earth’s surface, using g ≈ 9.8 N/kg gives weight W = mg = 70 × 9.8 = 686 N. The same result is consistent with Newton’s gravitational law when Earth’s mass and radius are used.

Comparing two planets

If two planets have the same radius but one has twice the mass, the surface field strength of the more massive planet is twice as large because g = GM/r². If both mass and radius change, compare the ratio M/r² rather than mass alone.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Using the surface-to-surface gap instead of centre-to-centre separation.
  • Forgetting to square the distance in the denominator.
  • Entering kilometres when the formula expects metres.
  • Confusing the universal constant G with local field strength g.
  • Using altitude above the surface as r without adding the planet’s radius.
  • Reporting a force in N/kg or a field strength in newtons.
  • Rounding scientific-notation values too early in a multi-step calculation.

Practice questions

  • Calculate the force between 5 kg and 8 kg masses whose centres are 0.5 m apart.
  • If the distance between two masses triples, what fraction of the original force remains?
  • Calculate g at the surface of a spherical planet with mass 4.8 × 10²⁴ kg and radius 6.0 × 10⁶ m.
  • A probe is 300 km above a planet with radius 6,000 km. What centre distance should be used?
  • Explain why a person’s mass stays the same on the Moon while their weight changes.

Calculator scope and limitations

The calculator has two modes: Newton’s law of gravitation, which finds the force between two masses, and gravitational field strength, which finds g from the mass of a planet or other attracting body. It can solve for the selected unknown in the displayed relationship, including mass, distance or the gravitational constant where the equation permits it.

The model assumes a point-mass or spherical-mass approximation and uses centre-to-centre distance. It does not calculate orbital paths, escape velocity, gravitational potential energy, vector fields from many bodies, air resistance or relativistic effects. Use SI units and scientific notation for very large values.

Frequently asked questions

What is Newton’s law of universal gravitation?

It states that every pair of masses attracts with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres.

What is the value of G?

A commonly used value is 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg². Use a more precise value if your question or course specifies one.

Why does gravity become weaker with distance?

The force follows an inverse-square relationship. Doubling the centre-to-centre distance reduces the force to one quarter.

What is the difference between mass and weight?

Mass measures the amount of matter and is measured in kilograms. Weight is gravitational force and is measured in newtons; it changes when local gravitational field strength changes.

Is g the same everywhere on Earth?

It is close to 9.8 N/kg near the surface, but varies slightly with location and altitude. Many school calculations use 9.8 or 10 N/kg as an approximation.

Can the formula calculate gravity between two people?

Yes, in principle. The force is extremely small for everyday masses, so it is not normally noticeable without sensitive equipment.

Does the calculator include air resistance?

No. Newton’s gravitational force is calculated without drag or other forces. Air resistance must be considered separately when modelling motion through an atmosphere.

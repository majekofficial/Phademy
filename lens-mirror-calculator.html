Thin lens and curved mirror formula, image distance and magnification

Lenses and curved mirrors form images by refracting or reflecting light. The position and size of an image depend on the focal length and the object and image distances. In many school physics problems, the thin-lens or spherical-mirror relationship is written as 1/f = 1/u + 1/v. Magnification compares image size with object size and, in magnitude, can be found from v/u under the convention used by this calculator. The Phademy Lens and Mirror Calculator helps you find focal length, object distance, image distance or magnification while keeping the distances in one consistent unit.

What focal length means

The focal length is the distance from the optical centre of a thin lens, or the relevant principal reference point of a mirror, to its principal focus. Parallel rays close to the principal axis converge at the focus for a converging lens or concave mirror. A diverging lens or convex mirror causes parallel rays to spread out as if they came from a virtual focus.

Focal length is a property of the optical system and the surrounding medium. A short focal length means stronger optical power, while a longer focal length means weaker power. In the thin-lens approximation, the same focal length is used throughout the calculation. Real thick lenses and complex optical systems may need more detailed treatment.

The focal point is not always a physical place where light gathers. For a diverging lens or convex mirror, the focus is virtual: the outgoing rays spread apart, but their backward extensions meet at the focal point. This is why sign conventions are important when describing virtual images and focal lengths.

The lens and mirror equation

The relationship 1/f = 1/u + 1/v links focal length f, object distance u and image distance v. All three distances must use the same unit. You can use centimetres throughout or metres throughout, but do not mix centimetres and metres in one calculation. The equation is reciprocal, so each distance appears as one divided by that distance.

To find focal length, add the reciprocals of object and image distances, then take the reciprocal of the result. To find image distance, rearrange to 1/v = 1/f − 1/u. To find object distance, rearrange to 1/u = 1/f − 1/v. The Change of Subject calculator can help you practise these algebraic rearrangements before entering values.

The equation is commonly applied to thin lenses and spherical mirrors under the paraxial approximation, where rays are close to the principal axis and make small angles with it. Strongly curved systems, thick lenses and rays far from the axis can show optical effects that the simple equation does not capture.

Real and virtual images

A real image forms where light rays actually meet after passing through a lens or reflecting from a mirror. A real image can usually be projected onto a screen. A virtual image forms where rays only appear to come from when traced backwards. A virtual image cannot be projected onto a screen in the same way, although an observer can see it by looking into the optical system.

For a converging lens, an object outside the focal length can form a real image on the opposite side. If the object is inside the focal length, the emerging rays diverge and the image is virtual, upright and magnified. A magnifying glass uses this arrangement. A concave mirror has a similar pattern for objects at different positions relative to its focal point.

Diverging lenses and convex mirrors generally form virtual, upright and diminished images for real objects. The exact sign of the distances depends on the convention being used. The calculator’s stated convention treats real object and real image distances as positive, while a negative result signals a virtual object or image in the corresponding rearrangement. Always compare this with the convention taught in your course.

Magnification and image size

Magnification tells you how large the image is compared with the object. Its magnitude is image height divided by object height. Under the distance convention used here, the magnitude can also be found from m = v/u. If m = 2, the image is twice the object’s size. If m = 0.5, the image is half as large.

Magnification is a ratio, so it has no unit. An image 4 cm tall formed from an object 2 cm tall has magnification 4/2 = 2. The same ratio can be found from image distance divided by object distance when the thin-lens or mirror geometry and sign convention are applied consistently.

Some textbooks include a negative sign in the magnification equation to indicate image orientation. A negative magnification often means the image is inverted, while a positive value means it is upright. The Phademy tool displays the distance ratio described in its formula, so use your class convention when interpreting orientation. Do not infer upright or inverted from the magnitude alone.

Image position for a converging lens

When an object is beyond twice the focal length, the image forms between f and 2f. It is real, inverted and smaller than the object. This arrangement is useful for a camera, where a relatively small real image is formed on the sensor. Moving the object farther away makes the image approach the focal plane.

When an object is at 2f, the image forms at 2f and is the same size as the object. When the object is between f and 2f, the image forms beyond 2f and is enlarged. A projector uses a real enlarged image on a screen. These standard cases are useful for drawing ray diagrams and checking the numerical result.

When the object is at the focal point, the refracted rays are parallel and the image is effectively at infinity. The reciprocal equation leads to a zero reciprocal for image distance. When the object is inside f, the image becomes virtual and magnified. This is the operating principle of a simple magnifying glass.

How to solve an optics problem

Draw a simple labelled diagram if the question describes the optical arrangement. Mark the lens or mirror, principal axis, focal points and object position. Identify whether the question asks for focal length, object distance, image distance or magnification. Then list the known values using a consistent unit.

Substitute into the correct equation and keep the reciprocal terms clear. For example, write 1/v = 1/f − 1/u before evaluating the numbers. Avoid rounding each reciprocal too early, because small rounding differences can affect the final distance. Keep a few extra digits during working and round the final answer to a sensible number of significant figures.

After calculating, interpret the sign and magnitude. A positive or negative result is not automatically a mistake; it can indicate the side on which a virtual object or image lies under the selected convention. Compare the result with the physical arrangement. If a real object is placed outside the focal length of a converging lens, a real image should form on the other side and the image distance should be positive under this tool’s convention.

Optical power, focal length and practical interpretation

For a thin lens, optical power is the reciprocal of focal length when focal length is measured in metres. Its unit is the dioptre (D). A lens with focal length 0.5 m has power 1/0.5 = 2 D. A shorter focal length corresponds to a greater magnitude of optical power. Converging and diverging lenses are often assigned opposite signs for power under a signed convention, so follow the sign rule in your course.

The reciprocal relationship also helps explain why a small change in object position can cause a large change in image distance when the object is close to the focal point. As u approaches f for a converging lens, the quantity 1/f − 1/u approaches zero, so 1/v becomes very small and v becomes very large. This is why a lens near the focal-point condition can project an image a long distance away.

In practical optical instruments, image quality depends on more than the position predicted by the thin-lens formula. Lens curvature, material, wavelength, aperture and alignment can affect sharpness and distortion. The school equation predicts ideal image location for paraxial rays; it is not a complete camera or telescope design tool.

Worked examples

Find focal length

An object is 30 cm from a lens and its real image forms 15 cm from the lens. 1/f = 1/30 + 1/15 = 1/30 + 2/30 = 3/30 = 1/10. Therefore f = 10 cm.

Find image distance

A converging lens has f = 10 cm and an object distance u = 30 cm. 1/v = 1/10 − 1/30 = 2/30 = 1/15, so v = 15 cm. The image is real and smaller than the object.

Find object distance

A lens has f = 12 cm and forms a real image at v = 24 cm. 1/u = 1/12 − 1/24 = 1/24, so u = 24 cm. The object and image are both at twice the focal length, so the image has the same size as the object.

Calculate magnification

If u = 30 cm and v = 15 cm, the distance ratio is m = v/u = 15/30 = 0.5. The image is half the object’s size in magnitude.

Object inside the focal length

For f = 10 cm and u = 6 cm, 1/v = 1/10 − 1/6 = (3 − 5)/30 = −2/30 = −1/15. Thus v = −15 cm. The negative image distance indicates a virtual image under the calculator’s convention.

Check units before calculating

Suppose u = 0.30 m and v = 15 cm. Convert one value so both use the same unit: 0.30 m = 30 cm. Then use f = 10 cm from the first worked example. Mixing 0.30 and 15 directly would give a meaningless result.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Mixing metres and centimetres in the same equation.
  • Confusing focal length with object distance or image distance.
  • Using the wrong sign convention for virtual images.
  • Treating magnification as a length instead of a ratio.
  • Assuming every image can be projected onto a screen.
  • Rounding reciprocal values too early.
  • Using the thin-lens equation for a complex thick optical system without noting the approximation.

Calculator scope and limitations

The calculator provides the reciprocal lens and mirror formula and a separate magnification relationship. It solves for the selected variable in the chosen formula. Keep object distance, image distance and focal length in the same unit, such as centimetres or metres. The tool’s hint states the real-positive convention used for interpreting its distance results.

The calculator does not draw ray diagrams, calculate image orientation from a signed magnification convention, model lens thickness, aberrations, refraction through several surfaces or complex optical systems. The thin-lens and curved-mirror equations are idealised school models. Use the sign convention specified by your teacher or examination board when explaining a negative result.

Frequently asked questions

What is focal length?

It is the distance from the optical reference point of a lens or mirror to its principal focus.

What is the difference between a real and virtual image?

A real image forms where light rays actually meet and can be projected onto a screen. A virtual image only appears to come from a point when rays are traced backwards.

Why must all distances use the same unit?

The formula combines reciprocals of distances. Mixing centimetres and metres makes the terms inconsistent and produces an incorrect result.

What does magnification of 0.5 mean?

The image is half the object’s size in magnitude. Magnification is a ratio and has no unit.

Can a concave lens form a real image of a real object?

A simple diverging concave lens normally forms a virtual, upright and diminished image for a real object. More complex optical arrangements can behave differently.

Why is my negative image distance not an error?

Under the convention used by this calculator, a negative result indicates a virtual image or object. Check the physical arrangement and the sign convention in your course.

Does the equation work for mirrors and lenses?

The same reciprocal relationship is used in many school-level thin-lens and spherical-mirror problems, provided the sign convention and approximations are applied consistently.

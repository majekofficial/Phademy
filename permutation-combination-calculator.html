Counting arrangements and selections

Many probability and counting problems come down to one question: in how many ways can I choose r items from n? The answer depends on whether the order of the chosen items matters. If it does, you count permutations. If it does not, you count combinations. Enter n and r and the calculator returns both, plus a few related counts.

The formulas

P(n, r) = n! ÷ (n − r)!

C(n, r) = n! ÷ (r! × (n − r)!)

Every combination of r items can be arranged in r! different orders, which is why P(n, r) = C(n, r) × r!. Choosing a team of 3 from 10 people is a combination; choosing a president, secretary and treasurer from the same 10 is a permutation.

Worked examples

Small numbers you can check by hand

n = 5, r = 2. P(5, 2) = 5 × 4 = 20 ordered pairs, and C(5, 2) = 20 ÷ 2 = 10 unordered pairs.

A committee versus officers

From 10 people, C(10, 3) = 120 ways to pick a 3-person committee, but P(10, 3) = 10 × 9 × 8 = 720 ways to assign three distinct roles.

Poker hands

The number of 5-card hands from a 52-card deck is C(52, 5) = 2,598,960. If the order in which cards are dealt mattered, the count would be P(52, 5) = 311,875,200.

Edge cases

C(6, 6) = 1: there is one way to choose everything. C(5, 0) = 1: one way to choose nothing. P(6, 6) = 720, which equals 6! because all six items are arranged.

Related counts shown by the calculator

  • n! and r! factorials used in the formulas. For n = 5 and r = 2 these are 120 and 2.
  • Arrangements with repetition (nʳ): 5² = 25 ways to fill 2 positions from 5 choices when items can be reused, as in PIN codes.
  • Combinations with repetition: C(n + r − 1, r) = C(6, 2) = 15, used when choosing r items from n types and repeats are allowed.

Limits

  • n can be up to 1000 and both values must be whole numbers. If r is larger than n the result shows a dash, because you cannot choose more items than exist.
  • Very large results switch to scientific notation. P(60, 30), for example, is shown as 3.13700 × 10⁴⁹, a number with 50 digits, while C(60, 30) = 118,264,581,564,861,424 is shown in full.

Common mistakes

  • Using a permutation when order does not matter, which overcounts by a factor of r!.
  • Forgetting that “choose with replacement” needs a different formula.
  • Confusing r and n. n is the total available and r is how many you pick.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a permutation and a combination?

Permutations count ordered selections and combinations count unordered ones. Choosing AB and BA is two permutations but one combination.

Why is C(n, 0) equal to 1?

There is exactly one way to choose nothing: choose no items.

What does n choose r mean?

It is another name for the combination C(n, r), the number of ways to pick r items from n without regard to order.

Why does the calculator show a dash?

The numbers are not whole, are negative or r is greater than n.

How to decide whether order matters

The most reliable way to choose between a permutation and a combination is to describe what makes two outcomes different. Imagine selecting three students from a class. If the only question is which students attend a meeting, the group {A, B, C} is the same group as {C, B, A}. Order has not created a new outcome, so combinations are appropriate. Now imagine assigning the positions of chairperson, secretary and treasurer. A, B and C in those roles is not the same arrangement as C, B and A, because each person has a different responsibility. The roles make the positions distinct, so permutations are appropriate.

Ask yourself: if I swap two selected items, would the result count as a different answer in the real situation? If the answer is yes, use a permutation. If the answer is no, use a combination. This simple question is more useful than trying to memorise a long list of keywords. Words such as arrange, rank, order, seat and assign often suggest permutations. Words such as select, choose, committee, team and sample often suggest combinations. However, always read the context: a team may have a captain, which makes one role special.

Understanding factorials

A factorial is the product of all positive whole numbers from the chosen number down to one. It is written with an exclamation mark. For example, 5! = 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120. Factorials count the number of ways to arrange distinct objects. Five different books can be placed on a shelf in 5! = 120 orders. For six books, there are 6! = 720 orders. The count grows quickly because every new object can be inserted into each existing arrangement in several positions.

The special rule 0! = 1 is important in counting. It does not mean that zero objects are multiplied together in an ordinary list. It is the value that makes the counting formulas work consistently, including the fact that there is exactly one way to select nothing. The calculator displays factorial values as part of its related results, helping you see the pieces used in nPr and nCr.

Why the formulas work

For a permutation of r objects selected from n distinct objects, there are n choices for the first position, n − 1 choices for the second, and so on. The multiplication principle gives n × (n − 1) × ... until r positions have been filled. This product is n! divided by (n − r)!, because the unused tail of the factorial is cancelled. That gives P(n,r) = n!/(n−r)!.

A combination counts each selected group only once. But a group of r distinct objects can be rearranged in r! orders. Therefore, the permutation count contains r! copies of every group. Dividing nPr by r! gives nCr = n!/[r!(n−r)!]. This relationship, nPr = nCr × r!, is a useful way to check an answer and understand why permutations are never smaller than combinations for the same valid n and r.

Worked examples from everyday situations

Choosing a project team

A lecturer wants four students from a class of twelve to form a project team. The roles are not assigned yet, so the order does not matter. The number of possible teams is C(12,4) = 12!/[4!8!] = 495. This is a count of different groups, not a count of ways to assign duties inside each group. If the lecturer later appoints a leader, that is a separate decision.

Creating a three-digit code

Suppose a code uses three different digits chosen from ten digits, and a digit cannot be repeated. The first position has ten choices, the second has nine and the third has eight. There are 10 × 9 × 8 = 720 codes. Here 012 and 210 are different codes because the position of each digit changes the code. This is a permutation. If repetition were allowed, each of the three positions would have ten choices, giving 10³ = 1,000 codes. The calculator shows nʳ as a related count for arrangements where repetition is allowed.

Choosing scoops by flavour

A shop offers five flavours and a customer chooses two different flavours for a tasting. If the order in which the flavours are listed does not matter, there are C(5,2) = 10 pairs. If the customer may choose the same flavour twice, the ordinary combination formula no longer applies. The combinations-with-repetition formula is C(n+r−1,r). For five flavours and two scoops, that gives C(6,2) = 15 possible flavour selections, including two scoops of the same flavour.

Combinations with repetition and arrangements with repetition

Repetition changes the counting problem. For ordered positions where every position can reuse any of n choices, multiply n by itself r times: nʳ. A four-digit PIN made from ten digits has 10⁴ possible strings if leading zeroes and repeated digits are allowed. It is important to distinguish this from a four-digit number, because a number is not normally allowed to begin with zero. The calculator's nʳ result is a general count of position-filling strings, not a special PIN validator.

For an unordered selection with repetition, imagine placing r identical counters into n labelled boxes. The number of possible selections is C(n+r−1,r). For example, choosing three doughnuts from four flavours when several doughnuts may have the same flavour gives C(6,3) = 20 selections. This model is useful for choosing items from categories when only the quantity of each category matters.

Probability and counting

Permutations and combinations often provide the total number of possible outcomes in a probability question. When every outcome is equally likely, probability is favourable outcomes divided by total outcomes. A five-card hand from a standard deck is one combination from C(52,5) possible hands. To calculate the probability of a particular hand type, count how many hands satisfy the condition and divide by 2,598,960. The counting calculator supplies the total or favourable counts; it does not decide which outcomes meet a separate probability condition.

Be careful about whether outcomes are equally likely. A count alone is not automatically a probability. For a fair random draw, each equally sized selection may have the same chance. In a real process with biased selection or unequal weights, the simple favourable-count ratio may not apply. State the assumptions before using a counting result in a probability calculation.

Common exam traps and how to avoid them

  • Ignoring special roles: selecting a committee is a combination, but choosing a president and vice-president is a permutation because the roles differ.
  • Using nPr when repetition is allowed: nPr assumes that an item cannot be selected again. A code with repeated digits needs nʳ instead.
  • Forgetting the leading-zero rule: a four-character code can begin with zero, while a four-digit whole number cannot. Adjust the first position separately when the question is about numbers.
  • Confusing n and r: n is the total number of available distinct items; r is the number selected or positions filled. The selection cannot exceed the available total in the ordinary no-repetition formulas.
  • Rounding too early: keep exact integer counts throughout. Counting outcomes should normally produce whole numbers.
  • Assuming the largest count is always correct: first decide what makes an outcome unique. A large answer can still describe the wrong experiment.

How to use the calculator effectively

Enter the total number of available items as n and the number selected as r. Both must be non-negative whole numbers, and the calculator accepts n up to 1,000. If r is greater than n, there are not enough distinct items for an ordinary selection without repetition, so the relevant result is unavailable. Check the result labels carefully: nPr is the ordered count, nCr is the unordered count, and nʳ is the ordered count when repetition is permitted. The combinations-with-repetition result answers a different, unordered question.

Large factorials become enormous very quickly. The calculator may display very large permutation results in scientific notation to keep them readable. Scientific notation does not mean the result is approximate in the sense of a measured quantity; it is a compact display of a large number. When checking a hand calculation, use a scientific calculator for factorials or compare the ratio nPr/nCr with r!.

Practice questions

  1. How many ways can five books be arranged on a shelf? Answer: 5! = 120.
  2. How many two-person teams can be chosen from eight people? Answer: C(8,2) = 28.
  3. How many ways can gold, silver and bronze be awarded among ten runners? Answer: P(10,3) = 720, because the medal positions are different.
  4. How many three-character strings can be made from four letters if repetition is allowed? Answer: 4³ = 64.
  5. How many ways can two identical items be selected from six types when repetition is allowed? Answer: C(7,2) = 21.

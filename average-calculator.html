Finding the mean of a list of numbers

The average most people mean in daily life is the arithmetic mean: add the numbers up and divide by how many there are. It is used for test scores, monthly spending, temperatures, delivery times and countless other lists. This calculator takes values separated by commas and returns the mean, rounded to two decimal places where needed.

The formula

mean = sum of all values ÷ number of values

Every value contributes equally, so one unusually large or small number pulls the mean towards it. That is useful when you want every observation to count, and misleading when one value is an outlier.

Worked examples

Four values

Enter 6, 8, 10, 12. The sum is 36 and there are 4 values.

36 ÷ 4 = 9

Test scores

Scores of 72, 85, 90, 64 and 79 add up to 390.

390 ÷ 5 = 78

The calculator shows 78.

When one value distorts the mean

A shop records daily sales of 20, 22, 23, 24 and 91. The mean is:

(20 + 22 + 23 + 24 + 91) ÷ 5 = 180 ÷ 5 = 36

Four of the five days were between 20 and 24, so 36 describes none of them. The middle value, 23, is a better picture of a normal day. The Statistics Calculator shows the median and mode alongside the mean.

Decimals and negative numbers

Entering -4, 10, 6.5 gives a sum of 12.5 over 3 values, which is 4.17 to two decimal places. Negative values are accepted.

Entering your numbers

  • Separate values with commas. Spaces on their own are not treated as separators here, so 72 85 90 will not work; write 72, 85, 90.
  • For long lists copied from a spreadsheet column, the Statistics Calculator accepts spaces and new lines as well.
  • Use a full stop for decimals, as in 6.5, and do not include units or symbols inside the list.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving out a zero. A missed assignment scored 0 still counts in the average. Leaving it out raises the result.
  • Averaging averages. The mean of two class averages is only correct if both classes have the same number of students. Otherwise weight by class size.
  • Using the mean for skewed data. Incomes, house prices and waiting times often have a few very large values. The median is usually more representative.

What an average tells you

The arithmetic mean, often called the average, is a single value that represents the centre of a group of numbers in a particular way. Add all the values and divide by how many values there are. The result is useful for summarising test scores, measurements, prices and other numerical data. It does not describe every value in the group, and it can be affected by unusually high or low observations.

The mean formula, step by step

Mean = sum of all values ÷ number of values

For the values 4, 6, 8 and 10, add them to get 28. There are four values, so divide 28 by 4. The mean is 7. The number of values is the count of observations, not the number of commas or the number of different values. Repeated values each count separately because each one is an observation.

Example with decimal values

A set of measurements is 2.5, 3.0, 3.5 and 5.0. Their sum is 14.0. Divide by four to get a mean of 3.5. The calculator can process decimal inputs, so retain the decimal point when entering each value.

Example with a negative value

Consider -2, 1, 4 and 5. The sum is 8. There are four observations, so the mean is 8 ÷ 4 = 2. Negative values are included in the total; they are not automatically discarded.

How an unusual value changes the mean

The arithmetic mean uses every number, so an extreme value can pull it toward itself. Consider monthly spending of 40, 45, 50, 55 and 300 units. The total is 490, and the mean is 98. The mean is much higher than four of the five observations because the value 300 has a strong influence. In this situation, the median, which is the middle value after sorting, is 50 and may better describe a typical month.

This does not make the mean wrong. It answers a specific question: what is the total divided equally across all observations? The median answers a different question: what value sits in the middle of the ordered data? Choose the measure that fits the purpose of your analysis.

Mean, median and mode are not the same

  • Mean: add all values and divide by the count.
  • Median: arrange values from smallest to largest and find the middle. With an even number of values, average the two middle values.
  • Mode: the value that occurs most often. A data set can have one mode, more than one mode or no repeated value.

For 2, 3, 3, 4 and 18, the mean is 6, the median is 3 and the mode is 3. These measures differ because they summarise the data in different ways. This Phademy tool calculates the arithmetic mean; use the Statistics Calculator for additional descriptive measures where available.

Case study: comparing two class averages

Class A has five scores: 60, 65, 70, 75 and 80. The total is 350, so its mean is 70. Class B has scores of 40, 70, 70, 80 and 90. Its total is also 350, so its mean is also 70. The equal means do not mean the classes have identical results: Class A's scores are more tightly grouped, while Class B has a wider spread. An average alone cannot show variation. To understand the classes fully, examine the individual scores and a measure of spread such as range or standard deviation.

Input format: separate values with commas

Enter the numbers in one field and separate them with commas, for example 12, 15, 18, 20. The calculator splits the field at commas, converts each trimmed item to a number and averages the finite values. Use a decimal point for decimal numbers. The input format is intentionally simple: commas are the separators. Do not rely on spaces, semicolons or line breaks as separators.

Practice questions

  1. Find the mean of 5, 7 and 9. Answer: (5 + 7 + 9) ÷ 3 = 7.
  2. Find the mean of 10, 12, 14 and 20. Answer: 56 ÷ 4 = 14.
  3. Find the mean of -3, 0 and 6. Answer: 3 ÷ 3 = 1.

Common mistakes and limitations

  • Using the wrong separator: separate values with commas, such as 4, 8, 12.
  • Leaving out a repeated observation: if a score occurs three times, enter it three times. Frequency matters.
  • Dividing by the wrong count: divide by the number of observations, not the number of distinct values.
  • Confusing mean with median: the average here is the arithmetic mean only.
  • Ignoring outliers: a very large or small value can substantially change the mean.
  • Mixing incompatible quantities: do not average values with different units or meanings unless they have first been made comparable.

More questions about averages

Can I enter decimal numbers?

Yes. Use a decimal point and separate each value with a comma.

Can I enter negative numbers?

Yes. Negative values contribute to the total just like positive values.

Does order matter?

No. The arithmetic mean is the same whichever order you enter the values.

Why is the mean not one of my original numbers?

The mean is calculated from the total divided by the count. It can fall between the observations and does not have to be one of the values entered.

Does this calculator find a weighted average?

No. It treats every entered number as one observation with equal weight. A weighted mean requires a separate weight for each value.

When a simple mean is appropriate

The arithmetic mean is useful when the observations are comparable and each observation should contribute equally. Examples include the average of a student's equally weighted quiz scores, the mean of repeated measurements taken under the same conditions, or the average number of items sold per day across a set of days. Before calculating, check that every number represents the same kind of quantity and uses the same unit.

Weighted averages are different

A weighted average gives some observations more influence than others. For example, a course grade may combine assignments worth 20%, a midterm worth 30% and a final examination worth 50%. Simply averaging the three scores would be incorrect if the weights differ. A weighted mean is calculated by multiplying each value by its weight, adding those products and dividing by the total weight. This calculator treats every entered number equally and does not apply weights.

Illustration of equal and unequal weights

Suppose a learner scores 80 on coursework worth 25% and 60 on an examination worth 75%. The unweighted mean is (80 + 60) ÷ 2 = 70. The weighted mean is (80 × 0.25) + (60 × 0.75) = 65. The two answers differ because the examination contributes more to the final result. Use the Grade Calculator or the relevant course rules when calculating an assessed grade rather than treating all components as equal.

Check the data before calculating

A correct formula applied to incorrect data still produces a misleading result. Check for duplicated entries, missing observations, accidental extra zeros and inconsistent units. If a list represents daily temperatures, for example, make sure all values use Celsius or all use Fahrenheit before averaging. Do not silently remove an unusual observation simply because it changes the answer; investigate whether it is a data-entry error or a genuine result.

Mean of a sample versus a whole population

A mean can describe all observations in a group or a sample selected from a larger group. A sample mean is often used to estimate a population mean, but the estimate depends on how the sample was selected and how large and representative it is. A calculator can find the arithmetic mean of the numbers you enter; it cannot tell whether those numbers represent the wider population or whether the sampling method was fair.

Communicating an average clearly

When reporting a mean, state what was averaged, how many observations were included and the unit. “The average was 12” is less informative than “The mean waiting time across 18 visits was 12 minutes.” If the data contain large outliers or a wide spread, report a median or range as well where appropriate. A single mean is a summary, not a full description of a data set.

Explain the average with the original data

When sharing an average with someone else, include the list or a short description of the observations whenever possible. A mean of 15 could represent five test scores, fifteen daily sales figures or measurements in centimetres; without context, the number has little meaning. If the data set is small, showing the values makes it easier to spot errors. If it is large, report the number of observations and explain how missing or invalid entries were handled before the mean was calculated.

Frequently asked questions

Is average the same as mean?

In everyday use, yes. Statisticians distinguish the mean from the median and the mode, which are other ways of describing a typical value.

Can the average be a decimal even if every value is a whole number?

Yes. The mean of 1, 2 and 4 is 2.33. It does not have to be one of the numbers in the list.

How do I find a weighted average?

Multiply each value by its weight, add the products and divide by the total of the weights. For example, 0.4 × 68 + 0.6 × 75 = 72.2.

What if I paste a long list?

Separate every number with a comma. If your data is in a spreadsheet column, paste it into the Statistics Calculator instead, which also accepts new lines.

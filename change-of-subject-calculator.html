Rearranging equations and making a chosen variable the subject

The subject of a formula is the variable isolated on one side of an equation. In v = u + at, velocity v is the subject because it already stands alone. If a question asks you to find time, acceleration or initial velocity instead, you must rearrange the same relationship before substituting numbers. This process is called changing the subject of a formula. It is a core algebra skill in physics, chemistry, geometry and many technical subjects. The Phademy Change the Subject calculator reads an equation, lets you select one of its letters and displays a rearranged result with working. It is useful for checking your algebra, but understanding the operations is what helps you solve unfamiliar exam questions.

What “subject” means and why it matters

The subject is the quantity that a formula is currently written to calculate. In A = lw, area A is the subject. The formula can also be written as l = A/w or w = A/l. These are not three different physical laws. They are three algebraic forms of the same relationship, each arranged to make a different quantity the answer. A question may give you area and width but ask for length, so using the original form without rearranging would make the calculation less direct.

Changing the subject is different from solving an equation for a numerical value. When you solve 3x + 5 = 20, you are finding the number represented by x. When you make x the subject of y = 3x + 5, you are writing x in terms of y and the constants. The result is x = (y − 5)/3. No numerical value is needed unless the question supplies y.

A useful habit is to identify the requested variable before doing any algebra. Circle that letter, then look at the operations connecting it to the other terms. Your goal is to undo those operations while keeping the equation balanced.

The balance principle

An equation states that its left side and right side have equal value. Any operation applied to one side must also be applied to the other side if equality is to remain true. This is why “moving a term across and changing its sign” is only a shortcut description. The actual operation is adding or subtracting the same quantity on both sides.

For example, start with v = u + at and make t the subject. Subtract u from both sides: v − u = at. Then divide both sides by a: t = (v − u)/a. The order matters. First remove the term added to at, then undo multiplication by a. If you divide before subtracting u, you will not isolate t correctly.

Inverse operations come in pairs: addition and subtraction, multiplication and division, squaring and taking a square root, and raising to a power and taking the corresponding root. Work from the outside of the target variable inward. When the expression is complicated, use a fresh line for every operation rather than trying to do several transformations mentally.

Rearranging when the variable appears once

Begin with simple linear formulas. From P = IV, making I the subject requires dividing both sides by V, giving I = P/V. Making V the subject gives V = P/I. From density ρ = m/V, multiply by V to get ρV = m, then divide by ρ to obtain V = m/ρ. The same method applies even when the symbols represent unfamiliar quantities.

If the target appears in a term with a coefficient, isolate the entire term before dividing. In y = 5x − 7, add 7 to both sides to get y + 7 = 5x, then divide by 5: x = (y + 7)/5. Do not divide only y and leave the 7 unchanged. Brackets make it clear that the whole numerator is divided by the coefficient.

When the target variable is in a denominator, multiply through carefully. From v = d/t, multiply both sides by t to get vt = d, then divide by v: t = d/v. A common source of errors is multiplying only one term when the other side contains a sum. If a denominator is shared by a whole bracket, multiply every term in that bracket.

When the target appears in a squared term

If the target is squared, isolate the squared expression first and then take a square root. For the circle formula A = πr², divide by π: A/π = r². Taking square roots gives r = ±√(A/π). The plus and minus values are both algebraic solutions because squaring either produces the same result. In a physical problem where r represents a radius, radius is a non-negative length, so the positive value is selected.

Another example is kinetic energy E = ½mv². Multiply by 2 to obtain 2E = mv², divide by m, and take the square root: v = ±√(2E/m). If v represents speed, use the positive root. If it represents a signed velocity in a one-dimensional problem, the sign may carry direction and must be interpreted using the question.

Be careful when the target occurs in more than one place. The formula x² + x = 6 is not solved by simply taking a square root after moving 6. It is a quadratic equation and needs a method such as factorisation or the quadratic formula. The calculator may return more than one solution when the algebra supports it.

Fractions, brackets and reciprocal formulas

Fractions are often easier to rearrange after multiplying both sides by a common denominator. Consider 1/f = 1/u + 1/v. To make v the subject, first subtract 1/u: 1/v = 1/f − 1/u. Combine the fractions: 1/v = (u − f)/(fu). Taking the reciprocal gives v = fu/(u − f). The same result may be written as −fu/(f − u). Both forms are equivalent.

When multiplying through by a denominator, remember that it may contain a variable. You must also note values that make a denominator zero, because the original formula is undefined at those values. In the lens relationship, f, u and v cannot be zero in the reciprocal form. A rearranged expression may look valid while a particular substitution still violates the original domain.

Brackets are essential when the numerator contains more than one term. For example, x = (p − q)/r means subtract q from p first, then divide the result by r. It is not p − q/r. When checking your answer, substitute it into the original equation and simplify both sides.

A reliable exam method

Write the original formula exactly as it appears in the question. Mark the requested subject. Then identify the last operation applied to that variable and undo it first. Continue until the variable stands alone. Keep equality signs aligned and avoid skipping steps when a negative sign or fraction is involved.

After rearranging, inspect the result for a sensible structure. If the target was multiplied by a quantity, the final expression will often contain division by that quantity. If it was in a denominator, it may move to the numerator after cross-multiplication. This is a quick reasonableness check, not a substitute for algebra.

Finally, test the formula with simple values. Choose values that avoid zero denominators, calculate the original subject, then use the rearranged formula to recover the target. If the two calculations agree, the rearrangement is likely correct. This numerical check can reveal a sign error that is difficult to notice in a long symbolic expression.

Choosing a sensible answer and checking restrictions

An algebraic answer is only part of the solution when the variable represents a real quantity. A length, mass or time may be restricted to positive values in a particular model, even though the equation has a negative mathematical solution. A direction-dependent quantity such as displacement can be negative, so do not discard a negative result automatically. Read the wording and identify whether the variable is a magnitude, a signed quantity or simply an unknown number.

Also check for values that make the original formula undefined. If the rearranged result contains division by a, then a cannot be zero in that form. If an equation was multiplied by an expression containing a variable, a special value may have been excluded during the rearrangement. Substituting the final expression back into the original equation is a strong way to reveal such restrictions.

For a practical calculation, rearrange first and substitute second. Keeping symbols until the final line makes it easier to spot a wrong sign and allows one rearranged formula to be reused for different data. It also keeps units visible: the algebra determines the structure of the expression, while dimensional checking helps determine whether the physical units are consistent.

Worked examples

Motion formula: make time the subject

Start with v = u + at. Subtract u from both sides: v − u = at. Divide by a: t = (v − u)/a. If v = 20 m/s, u = 4 m/s and a = 2 m/s², then t = (20 − 4)/2 = 8 s.

Make acceleration the subject

From v = u + at, subtract u to obtain v − u = at. Divide by t: a = (v − u)/t. With v = 18 m/s, u = 6 m/s and t = 3 s, a = 12/3 = 4 m/s².

Make mass the subject

Kinetic energy is E = ½mv². Multiply by 2: 2E = mv². Divide by v²: m = 2E/v². For E = 100 J and v = 5 m/s, m = 200/25 = 8 kg.

Make radius the subject

For A = πr², divide by π and take the square root: r = √(A/π) for a physical radius. If A = 78.54 cm², r ≈ √(25) = 5 cm.

Make current the subject

Ohm’s law is V = IR. Divide both sides by R: I = V/R. A 12 V supply across a 4 Ω resistor gives I = 12/4 = 3 A.

Rearrange a reciprocal relationship

For 1/f = 1/u + 1/v, subtract 1/u from both sides. This gives 1/v = (u − f)/(fu), so v = fu/(u − f). With f = 10 cm and u = 30 cm, v = 10 × 30/(30 − 10) = 15 cm.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Changing a sign without performing the same operation on both sides.
  • Dividing only one term in a bracket instead of the complete expression.
  • Forgetting brackets around a numerator such as (v − u)/a.
  • Taking a square root but ignoring the possible negative solution when the context does not restrict it.
  • Cancelling across addition, for example treating (a + b)/a as 1 + b.
  • Using a rearranged formula at a value that makes its denominator zero.
  • Confusing a symbolic rearrangement with finding a numerical answer.

Practice questions

  • Make x the subject of y = 4x + 9.
  • Make t the subject of s = ut + ½at² only if the equation can be treated as a quadratic in t; identify why two solutions may occur.
  • Make r the subject of V = πr²h, assuming r is a physical radius.
  • Make R the subject of V = IR.
  • Make v the subject of 1/f = 1/u + 1/v.

Frequently asked questions

What does make x the subject mean?

It means rearrange the equation until x is alone on one side. The other side may contain numbers, constants and other variables.

Why can an answer contain plus or minus?

When an equation contains a square, both a positive and negative number can have the same square. A physical context such as radius or speed may select the positive value.

Can I change the subject of any formula?

Many school-level formulas can be rearranged directly. Equations involving higher powers, multiple occurrences of the target or complicated functions may require more advanced solving methods.

What if the variable appears on both sides?

Collect all terms containing the target on one side and the remaining terms on the other. Then factor the target if possible. If the equation becomes quadratic or higher degree, use an appropriate equation-solving method.

Why does my result look different from the textbook?

Equivalent algebraic expressions can look different. For example, −fu/(f − u) equals fu/(u − f). Substitute values or simplify both expressions to compare them.

Should I use positive or negative roots?

Use the mathematical solutions first, then apply the meaning of the quantity. A radius cannot be negative, while a signed displacement or velocity may be negative depending on the chosen direction.

Find the exam score you need to reach your target grade

A final grade calculator answers a practical question: given the marks you have earned so far, what average do you need on the work that remains to finish the course with a particular overall result? It is useful before a final examination, a project submission, a practical assessment or any other weighted component. Instead of guessing whether a target is still possible, you can calculate the requirement and make a more informed study plan.

The result is a required percentage on all unfinished work considered together. It is not automatically the score required on one exam unless that exam is the only assessment left. If several assessments remain, the same result represents the weighted average you need across them.

What to enter in the calculator

Current grade: Enter your average percentage on the work that has already been assessed. This should be the average for completed work only, not a provisional overall grade that already includes empty or future assessments.

Current work weight: Enter the percentage of the whole course represented by the completed work. If completed coursework accounts for 60% of the final course grade, enter 60. The remaining weight is then 40%.

Target final grade: Enter the overall percentage you want when every assessment has been counted. It might be the minimum pass mark, a distinction threshold, or a personal goal. Use the percentage scale used by your course.

Check the course outline before entering the weight. A score of 80% on completed coursework does not mean that coursework is worth 80% of the course. One is your performance; the other is its contribution to the final result.

The final grade formula

Required score = (Target grade − Current grade × Completed weight) ÷ Remaining weight

Convert the two weights from percentages to decimals before using the formula. For example, 60% becomes 0.60 and 40% becomes 0.40. The completed contribution is the current grade multiplied by the fraction of the course already completed. Subtracting that contribution from the target tells you how many percentage points still need to be earned. Dividing by the remaining course weight converts that amount into the average required on unfinished work.

In symbols, if C is your current average, W is the completed weight written as a decimal, and T is your target, then the remaining weight is 1 − W and the required score R is (T − CW) ÷ (1 − W). The formula assumes that the course uses a weighted percentage total and that the current average correctly represents the completed portion.

Worked example: aiming for 75%

Suppose your current average is 70%, the completed work is worth 60% of the course, and your target is 75%. First calculate the contribution already earned: 70 × 0.60 = 42 percentage points. You need 75 − 42 = 33 more points from the remaining 40% of the course. Divide 33 by 0.40 to get 82.5%.

You therefore need an average of 82.5% on the remaining assessments. Notice that the required score is higher than your current average because you are trying to raise the overall result from 70% to 75%.

Worked example: checking whether a target is possible

Keep the same current average of 70% and completed weight of 60%, but set a target of 95%. The completed work has contributed 42 points. The remaining work must contribute 53 points, but it is worth only 40% of the course. The required score is 53 ÷ 0.40 = 132.5%.

A result above 100% means the target cannot be achieved through the remaining work under the stated weighting. It is not a calculator error. It tells you that you would need extra credit, a reassessment, a change in weighting, or a different target. Confirm whether your institution offers any such options rather than assuming they exist.

Worked example: a target already secured

Imagine that you have an 85% average on work worth 70% of the course and your target is 60%. Your completed contribution is 85 × 0.70 = 59.5. The remaining work is worth 30%, so the calculation is (60 − 59.5) ÷ 0.30, or about 1.67%.

This means that only a very small score is mathematically required to finish at 60%, assuming there are no separate minimum-exam rules. A negative answer can occur when the completed contribution already exceeds the target. In that situation, even zero on the remaining work would leave the weighted overall result at or above the target.

How to handle several assessments that remain

If a course has a final exam worth 25%, a presentation worth 10%, and a project worth 5%, the unfinished portion totals 40%. The calculator's required score is the average needed across that combined 40%, weighted by each assessment's contribution. It does not say that you must earn exactly the same percentage on each item.

For example, if the required average is 80%, you could score 90% on the presentation and 75% on the exam, provided the weighted combination of all remaining marks reaches the required average. To plan accurately, calculate each component's contribution using its own weight. A high mark on a small assessment cannot always compensate for a low mark on a much larger one.

When the current average is itself weighted

Some courses contain several completed components with different weights. Suppose quizzes are worth 10% of the course and you average 90%, while assignments are worth 30% and you average 70%. The completed work is worth 40% in total. Its weighted contribution is 9 + 21 = 30 points, so the current average across completed work is 30 ÷ 0.40 = 75%.

In this case, enter 75 as the current grade and 40 as the completed weight. Do not simply average 90 and 70 to get 80, because that would give the smaller quiz category the same influence as the larger assignment category. If your learning platform already reports a weighted average for completed work, check how it treats ungraded assessments before using it.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Confusing marks with weights. A score of 18 out of 20 is 90%, but it does not tell you how much that assessment contributes to the course.
  • Entering the remaining weight as completed weight. If 40% is left, the completed weight is 60%.
  • Using an unweighted average. If completed components have different weights, calculate their weighted average first.
  • Including future work in the current average. Use the average for work already assessed, then enter its share of the course.
  • Ignoring minimum assessment rules. A course may require a minimum exam score even when the overall grade is high enough.
  • Rounding too early. Keep full precision during the calculation and round the required score only at the end.

What this calculator assumes and what it cannot decide

The calculation assumes that grades are percentages, the completed and remaining weights add to 100%, and the current grade is a valid average for completed work. It does not know your institution's grading policy, moderation rules, attendance requirements, practical pass conditions, late penalties or supplementary examination rules. It also does not predict how difficult a future assessment will be.

The calculator uses one target and one combined remaining weight. It does not build a timetable or allocate a different target to each assessment. Treat the result as a planning figure, then compare it with your course handbook and ask your lecturer or academic adviser about rules that are not represented by a percentage average.

Turn the result into a study plan

Once you know the required score, break the remaining work into topics and tasks. Identify the areas where you lose marks, practise questions under realistic time limits, and review feedback from earlier assessments. If the required score is high, focus on the topics with the greatest assessment value and seek help early rather than waiting until the final week.

If the target is mathematically out of reach, the result can still help you make a sensible decision. Find out the highest possible overall grade, check whether reassessment or bonus work is permitted, and set a revised target. A calculation is most useful when it leads to a realistic next step.

How weighted grading differs from a simple average

Imagine a course with three assessments: a quiz worth 10%, an assignment worth 30%, and a final exam worth 60%. A student who earns 90% on the quiz and 70% on the assignment has completed 40% of the course. The weighted contribution is 9 + 21 = 30 points. The average across the completed portion is 30 ÷ 0.40 = 75%, not the simple average of 80%. Entering the correct completed average and weight is essential because a small assessment should not influence the result as much as a large one.

Some grade portals show a running total based only on graded work, while others show the points already earned toward the final course total. Those numbers can look similar but mean different things. If a portal says you have earned 30 out of the 40 percentage points available so far, your current average is 75%. Enter 75 for current grade and 40 for completed weight. If it instead displays a projected total that assumes future marks, do not use it as the current average without checking the calculation.

Plan for a range of targets

It can be useful to calculate more than one target. For example, find the required score for a pass, for a credit and for a distinction. This shows how much extra performance each higher target demands. It can also help you decide where to focus effort if several courses have assessments due at the same time.

Do not treat a target calculation as a guarantee. The required percentage is a mathematical threshold under the weights entered. Your actual result depends on assessment performance, marking rules and any course-specific conditions. Use the result as a planning guide, then build a study strategy around the topics and skills being assessed.

Check your course assessment structure

Before acting on a required score, confirm that all assessment components are included in the weighting and that no marks are capped. Some courses use hurdle assessments, mandatory practicals or separate pass conditions. A weighted average can show that your overall percentage is sufficient while a separate course rule still requires you to pass a particular component. If the handbook is unclear, ask the course coordinator to confirm how the final mark is calculated.

Frequently asked questions

Can the required score be more than 100%?

Yes. It means the target cannot be reached through the remaining weighted work alone, unless extra credit or another adjustment is available under your course rules.

Can the required score be negative?

Yes. It means your completed work has already contributed more than the target requires. Mathematically, a zero on the remaining work would still leave you at or above the target.

Can I use this for a final exam?

Yes, if the final exam is the only work remaining. Enter the current average, the weight of completed work, and your desired overall course grade.

What if I have two exams left?

Enter the combined weight of both exams as the remaining portion. The result is the weighted average required across them, not necessarily the same score on each exam.

Does it work with letter grades?

Not directly. Convert the letter grade to a percentage using your institution's official grading scale, then enter that percentage. Do not assume every institution uses the same letter-grade boundaries.

What if my institution requires a minimum mark in the final exam?

This calculator only checks the overall weighted grade. A separate minimum exam requirement must be checked in your course regulations.

Should I enter 60 or 0.60 for a 60% weight?

Enter 60. The calculator's field is labelled as a percentage and the calculation converts it to a decimal internally.

Why does my learning platform show a different result?

It may include ungraded work, apply different component weights, round marks, or use a grading rule that is not a simple weighted average. Compare the exact components and weights before deciding which figure is correct.

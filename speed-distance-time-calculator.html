The relationship between speed, distance and time

If you know two of speed, distance and time, the third follows from one simple relationship. Choose the unit set that matches your problem (kilometres and hours, metres and seconds, or miles and hours), choose what to find and enter the two known values.

The formulas

speed = distance ÷ time

distance = speed × time

time = distance ÷ speed

A triangle with distance on top and speed and time at the bottom is a popular memory aid: cover the quantity you want and the other two show the calculation.

Worked examples

Finding speed

A bus travels 120 km in 2 hours: 120 ÷ 2 = 60 km/h. A sprinter who runs 100 m in 12 s averages 100 ÷ 12 = 8.33333 m/s.

Finding distance

At 60 km/h for 2.5 hours you travel 60 × 2.5 = 150 km.

Finding time

A 150 km journey at 50 km/h takes 150 ÷ 50 = 3 hours. A 400 m lap run at 8 m/s takes 50 s.

The marathon

26.2 miles in 4 hours is 26.2 ÷ 4 = 6.55 mph.

Average speed on a round trip

Driving 120 km out at 60 km/h takes 2 hours and returning at 40 km/h takes 3 hours. The average speed is the total distance divided by total time: 240 ÷ 5 = 48 km/h, not 50. The slower leg takes longer, so it counts for more.

Converting between units

To convert metres per second to kilometres per hour, multiply by 3.6. The sprinter above runs at 8.33333 × 3.6 = 30 km/h. Going the other way, divide by 3.6. A mile per hour is about 1.609 km/h, and you can convert distances precisely with the Unit Converter.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing units, such as a distance in kilometres with a time in minutes.
  • Averaging two speeds directly. Use total distance divided by total time.
  • Entering a time as 2.30 for 2 hours 30 minutes. Write 2.5, because 30 minutes is half an hour.
  • Confusing speed with velocity. Velocity includes direction, so a round trip has an average velocity of zero.

Constant speed is a special case of constant acceleration with a = 0. When speed changes, use the Motion Calculator. For the time between two clock times, see the Time Duration Calculator.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert minutes to hours?

Divide by 60. 45 minutes is 0.75 hours and 90 minutes is 1.5 hours.

What is average speed?

Total distance divided by total time, regardless of how the speed varied during the journey.

Is speed the same as velocity?

Speed is the size of how fast something moves. Velocity also has a direction.

Which unit set should I use?

Use the one your problem is written in so you do not need to convert. The result is shown in the same units.

Understanding the speed, distance and time relationship

Speed describes how much distance is covered in a given amount of time. The relationship is straightforward when an object moves at a constant speed or when you are calculating an average over a whole journey. If you know the distance and time, divide distance by time to find speed. If you know speed and time, multiply them to find distance. If you know distance and speed, divide distance by speed to find the time. The calculator lets you select the quantity to find and enter the other two values.

The three formulas are different rearrangements of the same relationship: speed = distance ÷ time; distance = speed × time; time = distance ÷ speed. A useful memory aid places distance above speed and time in a formula triangle. Cover the unknown quantity and the remaining arrangement suggests whether to multiply or divide. The triangle is a memory aid, however, not a substitute for checking units and understanding the situation.

What the units mean

The calculator offers three consistent unit sets. In the kilometres option, distance is entered in kilometres, time in hours and speed is returned in kilometres per hour (km/h). In the metres option, distance is in metres, time in seconds and speed is in metres per second (m/s). In the miles option, distance is in miles, time in hours and speed is in miles per hour (mph). The arithmetic is the same in each case. Only the unit labels change.

Never mix kilometres with seconds or metres with hours unless you first convert one of the measurements. If a car travels 90 kilometres in 90 minutes, the time is 1.5 hours, not 90 hours. The speed is 90 ÷ 1.5 = 60 km/h. If you enter 90 as the number of hours, the numerical answer will be wrong even though the calculator performs the division correctly.

Further speed and journey examples

Finding the average speed of a bus

A bus covers 150 kilometres in 2.5 hours. Speed = distance ÷ time = 150 ÷ 2.5 = 60 km/h. This is the average speed for the journey. The bus may have travelled faster on an open road and slower in traffic, but the total distance divided by total elapsed time gives the overall average. If the journey included a 20-minute stop, include that stop in the elapsed time when calculating the average journey speed from departure to arrival.

Finding the distance travelled

A cyclist maintains an average speed of 18 km/h for 40 minutes. Convert 40 minutes to hours: 40 ÷ 60 = 2/3 hour. Distance = 18 × 2/3 = 12 kilometres. Multiplying 18 by 40 without converting the minutes would mix units and produce a meaningless result. The same method works for a runner: at 4 metres per second for 90 seconds, the distance is 4 × 90 = 360 metres.

Finding travel time

A delivery vehicle must cover 210 kilometres at an average speed of 70 km/h. Time = 210 ÷ 70 = 3 hours. This is a mathematical estimate, not a guaranteed arrival time. Traffic, weather, roadworks, stops and changes in speed can increase the real duration. If you are planning a trip, allow a margin rather than treating the calculated time as an exact schedule.

Converting between metres per second and kilometres per hour

One kilometre contains 1,000 metres and one hour contains 3,600 seconds. Therefore, to convert metres per second to kilometres per hour, multiply by 3,600 ÷ 1,000 = 3.6. A speed of 12 m/s is 12 × 3.6 = 43.2 km/h. To convert km/h to m/s, divide by 3.6. For example, 72 km/h ÷ 3.6 = 20 m/s. The Unit Converter can help with related distance conversions.

Round trip at different speeds

A car travels 120 kilometres to a destination at 60 km/h and returns over the same distance at 40 km/h. The outward journey takes 120 ÷ 60 = 2 hours. The return journey takes 120 ÷ 40 = 3 hours. Total distance is 240 kilometres and total time is 5 hours, so the average speed is 240 ÷ 5 = 48 km/h. It is not 50 km/h. Simply averaging 60 and 40 gives the wrong answer because the car spends more time travelling at the slower speed.

Average speed is not always the same as instantaneous speed

A speedometer shows the vehicle's speed at a particular moment. That is an instantaneous speed. Average speed considers the whole interval: total distance divided by total elapsed time. If a vehicle travels 30 kilometres in the first half-hour and 20 kilometres in the next half-hour, it covers 50 kilometres in one hour and its average speed is 50 km/h. The instantaneous speed may have varied throughout both halves of the journey.

When the object changes speed, the simple formula can still calculate average speed if you use total distance and total time. It does not describe every moment of the motion. If you need acceleration, initial velocity, final velocity or displacement under constant acceleration, use the Equations of Motion Calculator. For a journey involving direction, remember that speed is a scalar quantity while velocity includes direction.

Speed, velocity and displacement

Distance is the total length of the route travelled. Displacement is the straight-line change in position from the starting point to the finishing point, including direction. Speed is based on distance, while average velocity is based on displacement. A person who walks 400 metres around a track and returns to the starting point has travelled 400 metres, but their displacement is zero. Their average speed is not zero, while their average velocity over the complete lap is zero.

This distinction matters in physics questions. If a question asks for speed, use the distance covered. If it asks for average velocity, use displacement divided by time. Do not substitute the route length for displacement when direction is part of the problem. For everyday travel planning, average speed is often the more useful measure because it reflects the full route.

Using the calculator accurately

First select the unit system that matches the problem. Next decide which quantity is unknown: speed, distance or time. Enter the two known values using the units displayed beside the fields. Check the decimal point and confirm that the time is expressed in the selected unit. Then read the result with its unit. A value of 15 is incomplete without knowing whether it means 15 km/h, 15 m/s or 15 mph.

Use positive values for ordinary travel calculations. A zero time cannot be used to calculate speed by dividing a non-zero distance by zero. A zero speed cannot be used to calculate the time needed to cover a positive distance. If a result seems unrealistic, check the units and the time conversion before assuming the calculator is wrong. For example, 100 metres in 10 seconds is 10 m/s, which is 36 km/h. It is not 10 km/h.

Practical applications

Running and fitness

Runners often compare pace, which is time per kilometre or mile, rather than speed. If a runner completes 5 kilometres in 30 minutes, the average speed is 10 km/h. Their pace is 6 minutes per kilometre. These describe the same performance in different ways. To compare two runs, make sure the distance and time are measured consistently, and remember that hills, surface, weather and rest breaks can affect the result.

School laboratory work

In a simple experiment, a trolley travels 1.8 metres in 3 seconds. Its average speed is 1.8 ÷ 3 = 0.6 m/s. Repeating the measurement several times and comparing the results can reveal measurement uncertainty. Use the same start and finish points, measure time carefully and record units with every value. The calculator handles the arithmetic, but it cannot correct a stopwatch reaction-time error or an inaccurate distance measurement.

Delivery and trip planning

If a delivery route is 36 kilometres and the expected average speed is 24 km/h, the simple travel time is 36 ÷ 24 = 1.5 hours, or 1 hour 30 minutes. Add time for loading, traffic, stops and customer handover separately. The result is a planning baseline, not a live traffic prediction. For a more realistic schedule, use current route and traffic information alongside the calculation.

Common mistakes and limitations

  • Mixing units, such as entering distance in kilometres while selecting metres per second.
  • Using minutes as though they were hours. Convert minutes by dividing by 60.
  • Taking the arithmetic mean of two speeds for a round trip without considering the time spent at each speed.
  • Confusing speed with velocity, or distance with displacement.
  • Assuming the calculated time includes traffic, stops or delays. It does not.
  • Using an average speed to describe the exact speed at every point in a journey.
  • Rounding an intermediate conversion too early. Keep precision until the final answer.

The calculator uses the basic constant-speed relationship and the selected units. It does not model acceleration, changing road conditions, route geometry, fuel use or traffic. It also does not calculate a safe driving speed. Always follow road rules and posted speed limits rather than using a mathematical result to justify a particular driving speed.

More speed and time questions

How do I calculate speed?

Divide the distance travelled by the time taken. For example, 100 kilometres in 2 hours gives 50 km/h.

How do I calculate distance?

Multiply speed by time, after making sure the units match. At 15 m/s for 20 seconds, the distance is 300 metres.

How do I calculate time?

Divide distance by speed. A 90-kilometre journey at 45 km/h takes 2 hours at that constant average speed.

How do I convert km/h to m/s?

Divide the speed in kilometres per hour by 3.6. For example, 36 km/h is 10 m/s.

Can average speed be zero?

For a journey with positive distance and positive elapsed time, average speed is positive. Average velocity can be zero if the final position is the same as the starting position.

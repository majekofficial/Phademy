What BMI measures, and what it does not

Body mass index compares your weight with your height. It is a quick screening number used by health services and researchers because it is cheap and consistent, not because it measures health on its own. Enter your height and weight in metric or US units and the calculator shows your BMI, the usual category and the weight range that corresponds to a BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 at your height.

The formula

BMI = weight in kg ÷ (height in metres)²

In US units: BMI = 703 × weight in pounds ÷ (height in inches)². For a 175 cm, 70 kg adult, the height is 1.75 m and BMI = 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.9.

The standard adult categories

  • Below 18.5: underweight
  • 18.5 to 24.9: healthy range
  • 25 to 29.9: overweight
  • 30 and above: obesity

Worked examples

A BMI in the healthy range

175 cm and 70 kg gives 22.9, in the healthy range. The healthy weight range for that height is 56.7 to 76.3 kg.

Other categories

  • 170 cm and 50 kg: BMI 17.3, underweight.
  • 165 cm and 80 kg: BMI 29.4, overweight.
  • 180 cm and 100 kg: BMI 30.9, obesity.
  • 160 cm and 90 kg: BMI 35.2, obesity.

US units

Choose US units and enter feet, inches and pounds. A person who is 5 ft 9 in and weighs 160 lb has a BMI of 703 × 160 ÷ 69² = 23.6.

Under 18

If you enter an age under 18, the calculator adds a note: BMI is read differently for children and teenagers, whose healthy ranges depend on age and sex. Ask a doctor or use growth charts rather than the adult categories.

The limits of BMI

  • It does not separate muscle from fat. A muscular athlete can have a high BMI and low body fat.
  • It ignores where weight is carried. Waist measurement and other markers add useful information.
  • Thresholds can differ between populations. Some health bodies recommend lower cut-offs for certain ethnic groups.
  • It changes with age. Older adults may be advised to aim for a slightly higher range.
  • It is not a diagnosis. Use it as one data point, and talk to a doctor or dietitian about your health.

Common mistakes

  • Entering height in metres or weight in pounds in the metric form.
  • Reading a single result as a verdict. Weight varies by a kilogram or two across the day.
  • Comparing a child with the adult categories.

How to calculate BMI correctly

To calculate body mass index, use a weight measurement and a height measurement taken in compatible units. The metric formula uses kilograms and metres. If your height is recorded in centimetres, divide it by 100 before squaring it. For example, 168 cm becomes 1.68 m. Then multiply 1.68 by itself to get 2.8224, and divide the weight in kilograms by that result. The calculator performs this conversion when you choose the metric option.

Metric example: checking each step

Suppose an adult weighs 64 kg and is 1.60 m tall. First square the height: 1.60 × 1.60 = 2.56. Divide 64 by 2.56. The result is 25.0. Because the calculator displays one decimal place, the result appears as 25.0 and falls at the start of the overweight category under the standard adult cut-offs.

US example: feet, inches and pounds

A person who is 5 ft 4 in tall has a total height of 64 inches. If their weight is 140 lb, calculate 703 × 140 ÷ (64 × 64). The result is approximately 24.0. Enter 5 in the feet field, 4 in the inches field and 140 in the pounds field. Do not enter 5.4 as a decimal height: feet and inches are separate measurements.

Understanding the healthy-weight range

The calculator also estimates the body weights that correspond to BMI values of 18.5 and 24.9 at the entered height. This is a mathematical range, not a personal target or a promise that every weight inside it is healthy for every individual. For a height of 1.70 m, the lower calculation is 18.5 × 1.70², which is about 53.5 kg. The upper calculation is 24.9 × 1.70², which is about 72.0 kg. The calculator rounds the metric range to one decimal place.

Notice that the range changes with height. A person who is taller can have a higher body weight at the same BMI because the formula divides weight by height squared. That is why comparing two people by kilograms alone is not meaningful. BMI is designed to put weight in the context of height, although it still leaves out important information about body composition and health.

When BMI is useful, and when it is not

BMI is useful as a quick screening measure in population studies and as one part of an adult health assessment. It is easy to calculate consistently, which makes it useful for tracking broad patterns over time. It can prompt a more detailed conversation about nutrition, activity, sleep, blood pressure, waist measurement and medical history.

It is not a direct measurement of body fat. Two adults can have the same BMI but different amounts of muscle, fat and bone. A strength athlete may have a high BMI because of greater muscle mass. An older adult may have a BMI that looks ordinary while having low muscle mass. BMI also does not show whether fat is concentrated around the abdomen, which can matter for metabolic risk.

Children and teenagers

Do not interpret a child's BMI using the adult categories listed above. Children are growing, and their BMI is interpreted using age- and sex-specific growth charts or percentiles. A single adult cut-off cannot account for normal growth and development. If the result is for someone under 18, use an appropriate clinical growth assessment with a qualified health professional.

Pregnancy and other special circumstances

Pregnancy changes body weight and the clinical questions being asked, so a general adult BMI result is not a pregnancy assessment. People with fluid retention, certain disabilities, unusual body proportions or conditions affecting muscle mass may also need a different assessment. A calculator cannot account for these circumstances from height and weight alone.

Case study: interpreting a result without overreacting

Imagine an adult who is 1.75 m tall and weighs 82 kg. Their BMI is 82 ÷ (1.75²), or about 26.8. The calculator labels this within the overweight category. That is the end of what the number itself establishes. It does not reveal whether the person is muscular, how much visceral fat they carry, their blood pressure, their blood test results, or their overall fitness. A sensible next step is to treat the result as a prompt to review the broader picture, not as a diagnosis or a reason to begin a restrictive diet without advice.

Practice questions

  1. An adult is 1.80 m tall and weighs 72 kg. What is the BMI? Answer: 72 ÷ 3.24 = 22.2 to one decimal place.
  2. An adult is 1.50 m tall and weighs 60 kg. What is the BMI? Answer: 60 ÷ 2.25 = 26.7.
  3. Why should a 12-year-old not be classified with adult BMI cut-offs? Answer: Children require age- and sex-specific growth references because their bodies are still developing.

Common interpretation errors

  • Confusing centimetres with metres: 170 cm must be entered as 170 in a centimetre field, not treated as 170 metres in the formula.
  • Squaring the wrong value: square height, not weight.
  • Assuming the category is a diagnosis: BMI is a screening measure and should be interpreted with other information.
  • Comparing unlike populations: age, pregnancy, ethnicity and clinical context can affect how BMI should be interpreted.

Adding context to a BMI result

A more complete discussion of weight-related health looks beyond BMI. A healthcare professional may consider waist circumference, blood pressure, blood glucose, cholesterol, family history, medication, activity level and recent changes in weight. These measures answer different questions. Waist circumference, for example, can provide information about abdominal fat distribution that BMI cannot show. Neither measurement should be interpreted in isolation.

Why trends can be more informative than one reading

Body weight naturally changes from day to day due to hydration, food in the digestive system, salt intake, exercise and other factors. A single measurement can therefore be a noisy snapshot. If a clinician or an individual is tracking weight, consistent measurement conditions can make the trend easier to interpret: use the same scale, similar clothing, and a consistent time of day. A trend still does not explain the cause of a change, so unexpected or concerning changes deserve professional advice.

Understanding the boundaries of a category

Categories are ranges created for screening and communication. They are not sudden biological switches. A BMI of 24.9 and a BMI of 25.0 are extremely close numerically, even though they fall on opposite sides of a conventional category boundary. Rounding also matters: the calculator displays one decimal place, while the underlying calculation uses the entered measurements. Do not treat a difference of a tenth as a complete description of health.

How to use the result responsibly

Use the number to frame a question rather than to label yourself. If the result surprises you, first check that height and weight were entered in the correct fields and units. If the inputs are correct, consider whether the adult screening categories are appropriate for the person. Then discuss the result in context if it raises a concern. Avoid using BMI alone to set a diet, exercise target or medical treatment. A qualified clinician or registered dietitian can help interpret it alongside individual circumstances.

Questions to take to a health professional

If you are discussing BMI at an appointment, useful questions include whether the adult categories are appropriate for your age and circumstances, whether waist measurement or other screening would add information, and whether a recent weight change needs investigation. You can also ask how medications, pregnancy, muscle mass or a long-term condition affect interpretation. Bringing a record of measurements taken consistently over time may be more useful than relying on a single reading. The purpose of a screening number is to support a better conversation, not replace one.

Using measurements consistently

For a meaningful comparison over time, measure height and weight carefully and record the units. Adults generally do not gain height in ordinary day-to-day circumstances, but posture and measurement technique can still affect a height reading. Weight scales can differ, and clothing or carried items can add weight. If measurements are being used in a clinical setting, follow the health professional's instructions rather than trying to make readings comparable by guesswork.

Frequently asked questions about BMI

Does BMI use age?

The standard adult BMI formula uses height and weight. Age does not appear in the arithmetic, although age can matter when a clinician interprets the result.

Does BMI change if I use pounds instead of kilograms?

No, not when the correct US formula and units are used. The two formulas are designed to give the same BMI from equivalent measurements, apart from rounding.

Why does the calculator show a healthy weight range?

It calculates the weights that would produce BMI values from 18.5 to 24.9 at the entered height. It is a reference range, not an individual prescription.

Can BMI tell me how much body fat I have?

No. It uses weight and height and cannot separate fat from muscle or identify where fat is stored.

Should I try to change my weight based on one result?

Do not make a major health decision from one number alone. Consider your medical history and discuss concerns with a qualified healthcare professional.

Frequently asked questions

What is a healthy BMI?

For most adults, 18.5 to 24.9 is considered the healthy range, though individual circumstances vary.

Is BMI accurate for athletes?

Not always. Muscle weighs more than fat, so a very muscular person can have a high BMI without excess body fat.

Can I use it for children?

No. Children and teenagers are assessed with age- and sex-specific growth charts. A doctor can interpret them.

How was the healthy weight range calculated?

It is the weight that gives a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9 at your height.

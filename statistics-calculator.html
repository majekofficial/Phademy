Analyse a data set with descriptive statistics

A statistics calculator helps summarise a collection of numbers so that patterns are easier to understand. Instead of reading every observation individually, you can describe the centre of the data, its spread, and how frequently values occur. This calculator accepts up to 500 numbers separated by commas, spaces or new lines. It reports the count, sum, mean, median, mode, smallest and largest values, range, quartiles, interquartile range, variance and both sample and population standard deviation.

These measures are called descriptive statistics because they describe the data you entered. They do not automatically explain why a pattern occurred, prove a cause, or predict what will happen next. The meaning of a result depends on how the observations were collected and what they represent.

Enter and prepare your data

Type or paste numerical observations into the data field. Separate each value with a comma, a space or a new line. For example, enter 4, 8, 15, 16, 23, 42. You can paste a column of numbers from a spreadsheet, but remove headings, labels, currency symbols and other text first. The calculator expects numbers only.

Before calculating, check that each value uses the same unit and refers to the same kind of measurement. A list mixing centimetres and metres is not meaningful until the units are converted. Likewise, do not combine exam scores out of 20 with scores out of 100 without first converting them to a common scale.

Count and sum

The count, written n, is the number of observations in the data set. The sum is the total after all observations are added together. If the data are 4, 8, 15, 16, 23 and 42, the count is 6 and the sum is 108.

Count and sum are basic but important checks. If you expected 30 survey responses and the calculator reports 29, a value may be missing. If the sum is unexpectedly large, check for a duplicated observation or a misplaced decimal point. A summary is only as reliable as the data entered.

Mean: the arithmetic average

Mean = Sum of all observations ÷ Number of observations

The mean uses every value in the data set. For 4, 8, 15, 16, 23 and 42, the sum is 108 and the count is 6, so the mean is 108 ÷ 6 = 18. The mean is useful when the values are reasonably balanced and every observation should contribute to the average.

However, the mean can be strongly affected by an unusually high or low value. Consider monthly incomes of 100, 110, 120, 130 and 1,000 units. The mean is 292, which is much higher than the income of most people in the group. The mean is mathematically correct, but it may not describe a typical person well. In a skewed distribution, the median is often more informative.

Median: the middle observation

The median is found by sorting the values from smallest to largest. If there is an odd number of observations, it is the middle value. If there is an even number, it is the average of the two middle values. For 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, the median is 8. For 3, 5, 8, 10, the median is (5 + 8) ÷ 2 = 6.5.

The median is less affected by extreme values than the mean. In the income example 100, 110, 120, 130 and 1,000, the median is 120. It better reflects the centre of most observations. Median is often useful for income, property prices, waiting times and other data sets where a small number of very large values may distort the average.

Mode: the most frequent value

The mode is the value or values that occur most often. In 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, the mode is 3 because it appears twice while the other values appear once. A data set can have more than one mode if several values tie for the highest frequency. If every value appears once, there is no mode.

The mode can be useful for identifying the most common shoe size, product choice, response category or repeated measurement. It is the only one of the three common measures of centre that can be used directly with categorical data, provided the categories can be counted. This calculator works with numerical entries.

Range: the overall spread

Range = Largest value − Smallest value

The range measures the distance between the two extreme observations. For 4, 8, 15, 16, 23 and 42, the range is 42 − 4 = 38. It is simple to calculate and gives a quick sense of how widely values vary.

The weakness of range is that it uses only the smallest and largest values. It ignores how the observations are distributed between them. Two data sets can have the same range but very different patterns. For a fuller description of spread, consider the interquartile range and standard deviation.

Quartiles and the interquartile range

Quartiles divide sorted data into sections. The lower quartile Q1 is the median of the lower half, and the upper quartile Q3 is the median of the upper half. The interquartile range is Q3 − Q1 and describes the spread of the middle half of the observations.

For the sorted data 4, 8, 15, 16, 23, 42, the median is (15 + 16) ÷ 2 = 15.5. The lower half is 4, 8, 15, whose median is Q1 = 8. The upper half is 16, 23, 42, whose median is Q3 = 23. The interquartile range is 23 − 8 = 15.

Quartile conventions vary across textbooks and statistical software, especially for small samples and even-sized data sets. This calculator uses the median-of-halves approach: when the number of observations is odd, the overall middle value is excluded before finding the medians of the lower and upper halves. Another program may use interpolation and return slightly different quartiles. State the method when precise comparison matters.

Variance and standard deviation

Variance describes the average squared distance of observations from the mean. First subtract the mean from each value, square each difference, and add the squared differences. Squaring prevents positive and negative deviations from cancelling and gives more weight to larger deviations.

Population variance divides the sum of squared deviations by n. Population standard deviation is the square root of that variance. Standard deviation is expressed in the same unit as the original observations, which often makes it easier to interpret than variance, whose unit is squared.

For a sample drawn from a larger population, sample variance divides the sum of squared deviations by n − 1. Sample standard deviation is the square root of sample variance. The n − 1 denominator is known as Bessel's correction and helps correct the tendency of a sample to underestimate population variance when the sample mean is used.

Sample or population: which should you choose?

Use population standard deviation when your data include every member of the group you want to describe. For example, if you have the scores of every student in a small class and your question concerns only that class, the population measure may be appropriate.

Use sample standard deviation when your observations are a sample intended to represent a larger group. If you measure 40 products from a factory's full production, the sample measure is usually more appropriate when estimating variation across all products. The calculator displays both versions so that you can select the one that matches your study design.

With only one observation, sample standard deviation cannot be calculated because n − 1 is zero. The calculator therefore indicates that at least two observations are needed for the sample result. Population standard deviation for a single value is zero because there is no variation within that one-value set.

Worked example: compare the main measures

Use the values 2, 4, 4, 6 and 9. The count is 5 and the sum is 25. The mean is 25 ÷ 5 = 5. The sorted middle value is 4, so the median is 4. The mode is also 4 because it occurs twice. The range is 9 − 2 = 7.

The lower half is 2, 4, so Q1 = 3. The upper half is 6, 9, so Q3 = 7.5. The interquartile range is 7.5 − 3 = 4.5. The mean is 5, so the deviations are −3, −1, −1, 1 and 4. Their squares are 9, 1, 1, 1 and 16, giving a sum of squared deviations of 28. Population variance is 28 ÷ 5 = 5.6 and population standard deviation is approximately 2.3664. Sample variance is 28 ÷ 4 = 7 and sample standard deviation is approximately 2.6458.

Choosing a useful summary

Use mean and standard deviation when the distribution is reasonably symmetric and the arithmetic average is meaningful. Use median and interquartile range when data are skewed or contain outliers. Use mode when the most frequent value or category is important. Range is a quick measure of the full spread, but it should rarely be the only measure reported.

For example, a report about household income may present the median and interquartile range because a small number of very high incomes can pull the mean upward. A laboratory report about repeated measurements may include mean and standard deviation when the measurements are approximately symmetric. The correct choice depends on the question and the shape of the data, not on which statistic looks most impressive.

Common data and interpretation mistakes

  • Leaving a heading in the data: remove labels such as “Score” or “Age” before pasting a column.
  • Mixing units: convert measurements to one consistent unit first.
  • Ignoring outliers: compare mean and median and inspect unusual observations.
  • Using the wrong standard deviation: decide whether the data are a full population or a sample.
  • Assuming quartile methods are identical: different software may use different conventions.
  • Confusing variance and standard deviation: standard deviation is the square root of variance and uses the original unit.
  • Assuming correlation or causation: descriptive statistics alone do not establish why values differ.

Limits of a calculator summary

This tool calculates descriptive statistics for the numbers entered, up to 500 observations. It does not clean missing data, detect measurement errors, identify a sampling bias, test a hypothesis or calculate confidence intervals. It also does not know whether a value is an outlier for a meaningful reason. Inspect the source data and describe how they were collected before drawing conclusions.

If you only need the arithmetic mean of a short list, the Average Calculator is a simpler option. For marks and percentages, use the Grade Calculator. Statistics are most useful when paired with a clear explanation of the population, sample, units and method.

Outliers and why they matter

An outlier is an observation that is unusually far from the rest of the data. It may be a genuine rare value, a measurement error, a data-entry mistake or a member of a different group. Do not delete an outlier simply because it changes the mean. First check the original record and decide whether there is a defensible reason to exclude it.

The mean and standard deviation are sensitive to extreme values, while the median and interquartile range are more resistant. Reporting both types of summary can make the effect visible. A box plot is often used to inspect quartiles and potential outliers, although this calculator reports the numerical summaries rather than drawing a box plot.

Report statistics with context

A result should include more than a number. State what was measured, the unit, the number of observations and whether the data represent a sample or a full population. For example, “The mean mass of 25 sampled items was 48.2 g, with a sample standard deviation of 2.1 g” is more informative than “mean = 48.2”. Explain any exclusions or unusual observations as well.

When comparing two groups, use the same measurement scale and describe how each group was selected. A difference between two sample means does not automatically prove that the populations differ. Sampling variation, sample size and study design all matter. Descriptive statistics are the starting point for analysis, not the entire conclusion.

Frequently asked questions

How should I separate the numbers?

Use commas, spaces or new lines. You can paste a column of numerical values from a spreadsheet.

What is the difference between mean and median?

The mean is the sum divided by the count. The median is the middle value after sorting. The median is less sensitive to extreme observations.

Can a data set have two modes?

Yes. If two or more values share the highest frequency, the data set is multimodal. If every value occurs once, there is no mode.

Why are sample and population standard deviations different?

Population standard deviation divides the squared-deviation total by n. Sample standard deviation divides by n − 1 to account for estimating variation from a sample.

Why might my quartiles differ from another calculator?

There are several accepted quartile conventions. This calculator uses the median-of-halves method, while other tools may use interpolation.

Can I enter negative numbers or decimals?

Yes. The data field accepts numerical values, including negative and decimal values.

How many values can I enter?

The calculator accepts up to 500 numbers in one data set.

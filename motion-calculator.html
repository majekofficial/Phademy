Solving constant-acceleration problems with the SUVAT equations

When an object moves in a straight line with constant acceleration, five quantities describe the motion: displacement s, initial velocity u, final velocity v, acceleration a and time t. Any three determine the other two. Enter the three you know, leave the other two boxes empty and the calculator returns the missing values.

The four equations

v = u + at

s = ut + ½at²

v² = u² + 2as

s = ½(u + v)t

Each equation leaves out one of the five quantities, which helps when choosing one by hand: v² = u² + 2as has no t, and s = ½(u + v)t has no a. The calculator selects the right combination for you.

Worked examples

Starting from rest

A car starts from rest (u = 0) and accelerates at 2 m/s² for 10 s. Then v = 0 + 2 × 10 = 20 m/s and s = ½ × 2 × 10² = 100 m. Enter u = 0, a = 2 and t = 10: the result is s = 100 m and v = 20 m/s.

Finding the acceleration

A train speeds up from 10 m/s to 30 m/s in 5 s. Then a = (30 − 10) ÷ 5 = 4 m/s² and s = ½ × (10 + 30) × 5 = 100 m.

Starting from distance and speeds

A runner covers 100 m while speeding up from 10 m/s to 30 m/s. From v² = u² + 2as, 900 = 100 + 200a, so a = 4 m/s², and t = (30 − 10) ÷ 4 = 5 s.

Braking to a stop

A vehicle at 20 m/s brakes to rest (v = 0) with a = −4 m/s². The stopping distance is s = (0 − 400) ÷ (2 × −4) = 50 m and the time is 5 s. The negative acceleration means it opposes the motion.

When two answers are possible

With s = 40 m, u = 5 m/s and a = 3 m/s², v² = 25 + 240 = 265, so v = ±16.2788 m/s. The calculator shows both values and a note that two answers are possible, for instance when an object passes the same point going up and then coming down. The time shown, 3.75961 s, goes with the positive velocity.

Signs and direction

Choose one direction as positive and keep to it. For an object thrown upwards, taking up as positive makes the acceleration due to gravity −9.8 m/s² (or −10 in many exams). A negative displacement means the object is below its starting point, and a negative velocity means it is moving in the negative direction.

Common mistakes

  • Entering only two values. Three are needed, and the result shows a dash otherwise.
  • Using the equations when acceleration is not constant, such as in a lift that speeds up and slows down smoothly.
  • Mixing units, for example kilometres per hour with seconds. Convert to m/s first; multiply km/h by 1 ÷ 3.6.
  • Forgetting the sign of a deceleration.
  • Treating an upward throw and its return as one equation without checking which sign applies.

Frequently asked questions

What does SUVAT stand for?

The five quantities s (displacement), u (initial velocity), v (final velocity), a (acceleration) and t (time).

Which three values should I enter?

Any three, in any combination. The calculator solves for the other two.

Why are there two possible answers sometimes?

Equations such as v² = u² + 2as involve a square, so there can be a positive and a negative velocity that satisfy them.

Can I use this for free fall?

Yes. Use a = 9.8 m/s² downward with a consistent sign convention, and ignore air resistance.

What each SUVAT symbol means

The letters are a compact way to describe one-dimensional motion. Displacement, s, is the change in position from the starting point and includes direction. Initial velocity, u, is the velocity at the beginning of the time interval. Final velocity, v, is the velocity at the end. Acceleration, a, measures how quickly velocity changes, while t is the elapsed time. The units are normally metres, metres per second, metres per second squared and seconds. Keeping the meanings separate prevents a common mistake: speed is not exactly the same as velocity because velocity includes direction.

Displacement is also different from distance travelled. If a runner moves 10 m forward and then 4 m backward, the total distance is 14 m but the displacement from the starting point is 6 m forward. The SUVAT equations use displacement. If a question asks for total distance along a route that includes a turn-around, divide the journey into suitable intervals instead of treating the whole trip as one straight-line displacement.

Why constant acceleration is essential

The standard SUVAT equations are derived for motion in a straight line with constant acceleration. Constant means that the acceleration does not change during the interval. An object can speed up, slow down or reverse direction, but its acceleration must remain uniform for the equations to apply directly. A car moving through stop-start traffic does not usually have constant acceleration for the entire journey. A car accelerating steadily along a straight test track is a better approximation.

If acceleration changes with time, a single value of a cannot describe the full motion accurately. The problem may need a velocity-time graph, calculus or separate intervals. Do not force a constant-acceleration formula onto a situation where the acceleration is clearly changing. The calculator is a learning aid for the standard school-level model, not a simulator for every possible journey.

How to choose the three known values

Enter exactly three quantities that are given in the question and leave the other two blank. Do not enter a value of zero merely because a box looks as if it should contain something. Zero is a real value and changes the calculation. For example, an object starting from rest has u = 0, so enter zero for initial velocity. If the initial velocity is not stated, leave the box empty rather than assuming the object starts from rest.

Before pressing calculate, write down the three known values with their units. Check that all distances are in metres, all times are in seconds and velocities are in metres per second. Convert kilometres per hour to metres per second by dividing by 3.6. Convert minutes to seconds by multiplying by 60. Mixing units can produce an answer that is numerically neat but physically wrong.

Worked example: a vehicle slows down

Suppose a vehicle travels at 24 m/s and slows uniformly at 3 m/s² until it stops. Take the original direction of travel as positive. The known values are u = 24 m/s, v = 0 m/s and a = −3 m/s². From v = u + at, rearrange to t = (v − u) / a. Substitution gives t = (0 − 24) / −3 = 8 seconds. The negative acceleration indicates that acceleration acts opposite to the chosen positive direction.

To find the displacement, use v² = u² + 2as. Rearranging gives s = (v² − u²) / (2a). Therefore s = (0 − 576) / (−6) = 96 m. The vehicle travels 96 m before stopping under this idealised constant-deceleration model. Notice that the displacement is positive even though acceleration is negative. This is sensible: the vehicle continues moving forward while its forward velocity decreases.

Interpreting negative answers

A negative value is not automatically an error. It usually indicates a direction opposite to the positive direction selected for the problem. If the final velocity is −5 m/s, the object is moving in the negative direction at 5 m/s. If acceleration is −2 m/s², the acceleration points in the negative direction. Whether that means speeding up or slowing down depends on the direction of velocity. When velocity and acceleration have the same sign, speed increases; when their signs are opposite, speed decreases.

For a vertical-motion question, many textbooks choose upward as positive, while others choose downward as positive. Either convention can work, but every velocity and acceleration must follow the same choice. Near Earth's surface, gravitational acceleration is approximately 9.8 m/s² downward. It is therefore −9.8 m/s² if upward is positive and +9.8 m/s² if downward is positive.

Using SUVAT for vertical motion

Free fall is a special case of constant-acceleration motion when air resistance is ignored and the object is close enough to Earth's surface for g to be treated as constant. Replace a with the signed value of gravitational acceleration. A ball thrown vertically upward has a positive initial velocity if upward is chosen as positive, but its acceleration is negative throughout the flight. At the highest point, its instantaneous vertical velocity is zero. Its acceleration is not zero there: gravity is still pulling it downward.

This distinction is a frequent examination test. Students sometimes write a = 0 at the top of a throw because the ball pauses momentarily. The ball's velocity is zero for an instant, but its acceleration remains approximately 9.8 m/s² downward. If the question involves a projectile moving horizontally as well as vertically, use the projectile motion calculator, which separates the two components.

Velocity-time graphs and the SUVAT equations

A velocity-time graph provides a visual way to understand the same relationships. The gradient of a velocity-time graph is acceleration. A straight line with a constant gradient represents constant acceleration. The signed area between the graph and the time axis represents displacement. A line above the axis indicates positive velocity, while a line below indicates negative velocity under the chosen sign convention.

For uniform acceleration, the graph is a straight line, so the average velocity is (u + v) / 2. Multiplying average velocity by time gives s = ½(u + v)t, one of the SUVAT equations. This is why the formula works: the displacement is the area of a trapezium under the straight-line velocity-time graph. If the velocity-time graph curves, acceleration is changing and this simple average-velocity formula may not apply to the whole interval.

Exam strategy for motion questions

Start by listing the quantities named in the question and writing the correct symbol beside each one. Identify the unknown, convert units, then select an equation containing the known values and the unknown. If the problem asks for two unknowns, calculate one and use it in a second equation. Include the direction or sign in your final statement. A result such as −12 m/s should be explained as 12 m/s in the negative direction, not simply written as a negative speed.

When a question describes an object coming to rest, set final velocity to zero. When it says starts from rest, set initial velocity to zero. When it says uniform acceleration, the SUVAT model is appropriate. When it says constant speed, acceleration is zero. These phrases are clues, but always read the complete question because the starting and ending conditions determine which symbol receives the value.

Practice questions with answers

  1. A cyclist starts from rest and accelerates at 1.5 m/s² for 6 s. Find the final velocity. Answer: v = 0 + 1.5 × 6 = 9 m/s.
  2. A train moving at 20 m/s slows at 0.5 m/s² for 10 s. Find its final velocity. Answer: v = 20 − 0.5 × 10 = 15 m/s.
  3. A ball is thrown upward at 19.6 m/s. Taking upward as positive and g = 9.8 m/s², how long does it take to reach its highest point? Answer: set v = 0, so 0 = 19.6 − 9.8t and t = 2 s.

Limitations and when to use another tool

This calculator assumes straight-line motion with constant acceleration and uses the five standard SUVAT quantities. It does not model changing acceleration, air resistance, turning paths or a complete multi-stage journey with different accelerations. Split a journey into intervals when the acceleration changes, then calculate each interval separately. For a launch at an angle, use the Projectile Motion Calculator. For a direct distance, speed or time calculation, use the Speed, Distance and Time Calculator. For the force causing an acceleration, use the Force Calculator.

Adding a percentage tax to an amount

This is a simple, general-purpose tax calculator. You enter an amount and a tax rate; it returns the tax and the total including tax. That covers sales tax, value added tax and any other flat percentage charge. It is not a personal income tax calculator, and it does not know the rules of any country.

The formula

tax = amount × tax rate ÷ 100

total = amount + tax

Use the rate that applies to your transaction. The result is exactly the arithmetic above, rounded to two decimals for display.

Worked examples

A flat rate

240 with 7.5% tax.

240 × 0.075 = 18 tax, total 258

A price with cents

89.99 at 20% is 17.998, shown as 18, so the total is 107.99.

Taking the tax out again

The calculator adds tax. If you know a tax-inclusive price and want the pre-tax amount, divide by 1 plus the rate by hand. For 258 at 7.5%, 258 ÷ 1.075 = 240. Do not simply subtract 7.5% of 258, which would give 238.65.

Tax in bands, built from several calculations

Some taxes apply different rates to slices of an amount. Take a hypothetical system with 0% on the first 1,000, 10% on the next 2,000 and 20% above 3,000. For 4,000:

  • 0% of the first 1,000 = 0
  • 10% of 2,000 = 200 (enter 2,000 and 10)
  • 20% of the remaining 1,000 = 200 (enter 1,000 and 20)

The total tax is 400, an effective rate of 10%. The bands here are only an illustration, not any real tax schedule.

Where this calculator stops

  • Real income taxes use allowances, reliefs, deductions and thresholds that vary by country and by year.
  • It cannot decide whether an item is exempt or zero-rated.
  • It does not account for withholding, social contributions or pension deductions.
  • For formal filing or large decisions, check the current rules with your tax authority or a qualified adviser.

Common mistakes

  • Applying the rate to a price that already includes tax.
  • Using an old rate. Rates change, so confirm the current one for your location.
  • Treating the effective rate (total tax divided by income) as the rate on the next unit earned. Those are different numbers in a banded system.

To find a percentage on its own, use the Percentage Calculator. To convert pay between periods before tax, see the Salary Calculator.

Frequently asked questions

Is this suitable for VAT or sales tax?

Yes, as long as one flat rate applies. Enter the net price and the rate to get the tax and gross price.

Can it calculate income tax?

No. Income tax depends on bands, allowances and your circumstances. This tool applies one percentage to one amount.

How do I find the price before tax?

Divide the tax-inclusive price by 1 plus the rate as a decimal, as shown in the example above.

Why is my invoice slightly different?

Businesses can round per item or on the total. The calculator rounds only the displayed result.

What this tax calculator can and cannot calculate

This tool calculates a simple percentage tax on one amount. You enter the amount and a tax rate, and it returns the tax amount and the total after adding that tax. It is useful for checking a flat sales tax, value added tax (VAT), service levy or another percentage-based charge when one rate applies to the whole taxable amount. It is not a country-specific tax estimator, payroll calculator or personal income tax filing tool.

The distinction is important because the word tax covers many different systems. A simple transaction tax may be a flat percentage of a purchase price. Personal income tax may use progressive bands, allowances, deductions, reliefs and credits. A business tax return may involve expenses, input credits, exemptions and reporting rules. This calculator does not know those rules. It performs the arithmetic for the single rate you provide, so you must confirm that the rate and taxable base are correct for your situation.

The formula explained

The formula is tax = amount × tax rate ÷ 100. The tax-inclusive total is total = amount + tax. Dividing the percentage by 100 changes it into a decimal multiplier. For example, 7.5% becomes 0.075. A tax rate of 20% becomes 0.20. This is why multiplying an amount by 0.20 is the same as finding 20% of it.

Suppose the pre-tax amount is 240 and the tax rate is 7.5%. The tax is 240 × 7.5 ÷ 100 = 18. The total is 240 + 18 = 258. The amount entered is the taxable base, not necessarily the amount shown on a receipt. If the price already includes tax, use the reverse calculation described below instead of adding tax a second time.

Further tax calculations

A flat sales tax

A product costs 500 before tax and the applicable flat rate is 5%. The tax is 500 × 0.05 = 25. The customer pays 525 before any delivery charge or other fee. If the rate were 7.5%, the tax would be 37.50 and the total would be 537.50. The tool can calculate either case, provided you enter the rate that genuinely applies to the item and transaction.

A price with decimal places

A service costs 89.99 and the tax rate is 20%. The unrounded tax is 89.99 × 0.20 = 17.998. If the displayed tax is rounded to two decimal places, it becomes 18.00. The displayed total is 107.99. Real invoices may calculate tax per line item or on the combined invoice, and rounding rules can vary. A one-cent difference between a simple estimate and an invoice does not necessarily mean the arithmetic is wrong; check the tax authority's rules and the seller's calculation method.

Finding the original price before tax

If you know a tax-inclusive total and want to recover the pre-tax amount, divide by one plus the tax rate expressed as a decimal. For a total of 258 at 7.5%, calculate 258 ÷ 1.075 = 240. The tax portion is then 258 − 240 = 18. Do not subtract 7.5% of 258. That would use the gross amount as the base and produce a different result. The original percentage was applied to 240, not to 258.

Comparing two possible rates

Imagine a taxable amount of 1,200. At 5%, tax is 60 and the total is 1,260. At 7.5%, tax is 90 and the total is 1,290. The difference in tax is 30. This comparison is useful for understanding how a rate affects a purchase, but it does not establish which rate is legally applicable. A product category, customer status, location or transaction type may affect tax treatment.

Understanding tax-inclusive and tax-exclusive prices

A tax-exclusive price is stated before tax is added. A tax-inclusive price already contains the tax. The distinction should be clear on a quotation or invoice. If a shop displays a tax-exclusive price of 100 and the tax is 10%, the tax is 10 and the total is 110. If a receipt shows a tax-inclusive total of 110, the pre-tax amount is 110 ÷ 1.10 = 100, and the tax portion is 10.

When comparing prices from two sellers, make sure you are comparing the same basis. One seller may advertise a price before tax while another shows the final amount. Delivery, handling and other charges may also be treated differently. The calculator can add a single percentage to the amount you enter, but it cannot identify which invoice lines are taxable or determine whether a fee belongs in the taxable base.

Why real tax systems can be more complicated

Some tax systems apply a rate only to part of an amount or use different rates for different goods and services. Some items may be exempt, zero-rated or subject to a reduced rate. A zero-rated item and an exempt item may have different consequences for a registered business, even though both can produce no tax charged on the sale. This simple tool does not classify goods or services. It assumes the amount and rate have already been determined.

Progressive income tax is another important example. In a progressive system, portions of income may be taxed at different marginal rates. The higher rate generally applies only to income within the relevant band, not necessarily to every unit of total income. A single percentage applied to the entire income would not reproduce that calculation. Allowances, deductions, pension contributions, social insurance and tax credits can also affect the final liability. Use the relevant tax authority's current guidance or a qualified tax professional for an actual filing or personal tax estimate.

Illustration of a banded calculation

Consider a purely hypothetical system with 0% on the first 1,000, 10% on the next 2,000 and 20% on any amount above 3,000. For taxable income of 4,000, the first 1,000 produces no tax. The next 2,000 produces 200 tax. The remaining 1,000 produces another 200. Total tax is 400. Dividing 400 by 4,000 gives an effective rate of 10%. The marginal rate on the final portion is 20%, but the effective rate across the whole amount is 10%. These bands are an arithmetic illustration only, not a real country's tax schedule.

Practical steps before using the calculator

  1. Identify what the amount represents: a price, fee, taxable income or another base.
  2. Check whether the amount already includes tax.
  3. Confirm the applicable rate for the transaction, location and date.
  4. Check whether the whole amount is taxable at one flat rate.
  5. Enter the amount and percentage, then review the tax and total.
  6. Compare the result with the invoice or official calculation, taking rounding rules into account.

Rates and tax rules can change. A rate remembered from a previous year may no longer apply, and different goods or services may have different treatment. If the transaction crosses borders or involves a business registration, additional rules may apply. The calculator should be treated as an arithmetic aid rather than an authority on current law.

Tax calculation errors to watch for

  • Adding tax to a price that already includes tax, thereby charging it twice in the calculation.
  • Using a percentage as a whole number in one formula and as a decimal in another without converting consistently.
  • Assuming one rate applies to every product, service or income type.
  • Confusing an effective tax rate with a marginal tax rate in a banded system.
  • Ignoring exemptions, deductions, credits or other adjustments that apply to a real tax return.
  • Using an outdated rate without checking current official guidance.
  • Expecting every invoice to round tax in exactly the same way.

How this relates to other money calculations

If you are working from a gross salary or a regular pay amount, the Salary Calculator can convert pay between yearly, monthly and weekly periods, but it does not calculate take-home pay after tax. For a percentage increase or decrease, use the Percentage Calculator. If a tax-exclusive purchase price is discounted before tax, calculate the discount first with the Discount Calculator, then apply the relevant tax rate to the correct taxable amount.

More tax questions

Can I use this for VAT or sales tax?

Yes, for a simple transaction where one flat rate applies to the full amount. Enter the pre-tax amount and the correct rate to calculate the tax and total.

Can it calculate personal income tax?

No. Personal income tax may involve progressive bands, allowances, reliefs, deductions, credits and other rules. This calculator applies one percentage to one amount.

How do I remove tax from a total?

Divide the tax-inclusive total by 1 plus the tax rate as a decimal. For a 10% rate, divide by 1.10. Subtract the resulting pre-tax amount from the gross total to find the tax portion.

Why is an invoice a little different?

The seller may round tax on each line or on the full invoice, and local rules may specify a rounding method. Compare the calculation method and the applicable rules before assuming there is an error.

Does the calculator know the current tax rate in my country?

No. You must supply the rate. Confirm it using current official information for the relevant country, product, service and transaction date.

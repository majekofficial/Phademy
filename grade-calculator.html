Turning marks into a percentage

A mark of 42 means little until you know what it was out of. This calculator converts the points you earned into a percentage of the points available, so you can compare a quiz marked out of 20 with an exam marked out of 60 on the same footing. Enter your points and the total, and the percentage appears immediately.

The formula

Percentage = (points earned ÷ total points) × 100

The total must be greater than zero. If it is empty or zero the calculator shows a dash, because a percentage of nothing is undefined. The result is not capped at 100, so a mark with bonus points, such as 105 out of 100, displays 105%.

Worked examples

A class test

You scored 42 out of 60.

(42 ÷ 60) × 100 = 70%

A short quiz

You scored 17 out of 20.

(17 ÷ 20) × 100 = 85%

A smaller quiz can feel less important, but 85% here is worth exactly the same as 85% on a long paper.

A half mark

13.5 out of 18 gives (13.5 ÷ 18) × 100 = 75%. Decimal marks are fine.

Working out a course grade from several marks

One percentage is rarely your whole grade, because coursework and exams usually carry different weights. To combine them by hand, multiply each percentage by its weight and add the results. If coursework is worth 40% and you scored 68%, and the exam is worth 60% and you scored 75%:

0.40 × 68 + 0.60 × 75 = 27.2 + 45 = 72.2%

Percentages, marks and why they need context

A raw mark carries almost no information by itself. Scoring 42 in one test and 17 in another tells you nothing about which performance was better until you know the maximum for each. Converting to a percentage puts every assessment on the same 0 to 100 scale, which is the common currency of grading. This calculator does that one conversion, and the rest of this page explains what to do with the result.

Percentages also let you compare across courses, because teachers set different totals for different assessments. A 25-mark quiz and a 120-mark exam are easy to compare once both are percentages. They are also the building block for weighted grades, where each assessment contributes a share of the final result.

From points to percentage, step by step

  1. Identify the points you earned. Subtract any penalties first if the marking scheme applies them.
  2. Identify the maximum points available for that assessment, including any bonus section if it counts towards the total.
  3. Divide earned by maximum.
  4. Multiply by 100.
  5. Round only at the end, to the precision your course uses (often one or two decimals, or the nearest whole number).

More worked examples

Whole-number percentages

36 out of 40 is (36 ÷ 40) × 100 = 90%. 27 out of 45 is 60%. 7.5 out of 10 is 75%. 66 out of 88 is 75%.

A paper with bonus marks

A paper marked out of 100 has a 10-mark bonus question and you score 105. The calculator shows 105%, because the maximum entered is 100. If your teacher caps the total at 100%, the official figure will be 100%.

Combining coursework and exam

Coursework is 30% of the final mark and you earned 72%. The exam is 70% and you earned 64%. The weighted final is 0.30 × 72 + 0.70 × 64 = 21.6 + 44.8 = 66.4%. Notice that the larger weight on the exam pulls the result closer to 64.

Averaging several tests of equal weight

Test scores of 18/20, 33/40 and 45/50 are 90%, 82.5% and 90%. If the tests have equal weight, the average is (90 + 82.5 + 90) ÷ 3 = 87.5%. If you simply added the points, 96 out of 110 is 87.27%, a little different, because longer tests would count more.

Working back to marks

You want 80% on a test that is out of 60. The marks needed are 0.80 × 60 = 48. If you scored 45, you were 3 marks short, which is 5 percentage points.

Practice questions with answers

  1. What percentage is 33 out of 55? (33 ÷ 55) × 100 = 60%.
  2. You scored 19 out of 25. What is the percentage? 19 ÷ 25 = 0.76, so 76%.
  3. A test is out of 80 and you want 75%. How many marks do you need? 0.75 × 80 = 60 marks.
  4. Two tests: 14/20 and 36/60. Which is better? 70% and 60%, so the first.
  5. Assignments are worth 25%, a project 25% and an exam 50%. You scored 80%, 70% and 60%. What is the final? 0.25 × 80 + 0.25 × 70 + 0.5 × 60 = 20 + 17.5 + 30 = 67.5%.
  6. You scored 45 out of 50, but 4 marks are deducted for lateness. What percentage? 41 out of 50 = 82%.

Where this conversion is used

  • Checking progress. After each assessment, convert the mark and record it. Patterns appear quickly: a run of 62%, 65% and 61% tells you more than three raw marks.
  • Comparing sections. Percentages show whether you are stronger in coursework or exams, helping you target revision.
  • Setting targets. Work backwards from the grade you want to the marks you need on each remaining assessment, using the Final Grade Calculator.
  • Applications. Many scholarship and admission forms ask for percentages rather than raw marks.
  • Feedback conversations. A percentage gives you and your teacher a shared number to discuss.

Limits and cautions

  • A percentage is not a letter grade. The boundaries between grades differ by institution, subject and sometimes by the year, so convert using your own scale.
  • Marks are not always linear. In some exams the grade boundaries move depending on how the whole cohort performs, so the same percentage can earn different grades in different years.
  • Rounding: 69.5% may be treated as 70% by one teacher and 69% by another. Ask what rule applies.
  • The calculator does not know about weights. For a final course grade, combine percentages with the weights as in the example above.

A case study: turning a term of marks into a course grade

Tunde's Physics course has four components: a lab report (15%), a mid-term test (25%), homework (20%) and a final exam (40%). So far he has the lab report 27 out of 30, the mid-term 38 out of 50 and homework 82 out of 100. He wants to know where he stands and what the final needs to be for an overall 70%.

  1. Convert each mark. 27 ÷ 30 = 90%. 38 ÷ 50 = 76%. 82 ÷ 100 = 82%.
  2. Weight what is done. 0.15 × 90 = 13.5, 0.25 × 76 = 19, 0.20 × 82 = 16.4. Together, 48.9 of the possible 60 weighting points.
  3. What the exam must add. To reach 70, he needs 70 − 48.9 = 21.1 points from a 40% component, so the exam percentage must be 21.1 ÷ 0.40 = 52.75%.
  4. Interpretation. A little over 53% on the final secures an overall 70%, which gives Tunde a comfortable margin. For an 80% overall he would need (80 − 48.9) ÷ 0.40 = 77.75%.

Each of the first three conversions is what this calculator does. The weighting step is multiplication, and the final step is what the Final Grade Calculator automates. Doing it once by hand shows what the tools are really computing.

Common misconceptions about marks and percentages

  • “A mark out of 20 is easier than a mark out of 100.” The percentage matters, not the denominator. 16 out of 20 and 80 out of 100 are the same 80%.
  • “If I get 50% on every assessment, my final is 50%.” Correct, but only if all the assessments use the same scale. A weighted course is still 50% overall if every component is 50%.
  • “I can average percentages from different totals.” A plain average of percentages ignores the size of each test. Use weights or add points before dividing, depending on how your course is marked.
  • “A percentage above 100% is a mistake.” It may be real if there is bonus credit.
  • “Rounding up always helps.” Many courses apply a fixed rounding rule. Do not assume one.

Different ways grades are combined

  • Total points. All marks are added and divided by the total available. Larger assessments count more naturally.
  • Weighted categories. Each category has a percentage weight, and the average inside each category is multiplied by it.
  • Dropped scores. Some courses drop the lowest quiz or homework mark. Recalculate after dropping.
  • Minimum components. Some courses require a minimum exam mark regardless of the overall average.
  • Curving. A few courses adjust scores relative to the class. The calculator cannot predict that.

Key terms

  • Raw score: the points earned.
  • Percentage: earned divided by possible, times 100.
  • Weight: the share of the final grade an assessment carries.
  • Weighted average: the sum of each percentage times its weight.
  • Grade boundary: the percentage needed for a given letter grade.

More practice questions

  1. You score 38 out of 50. 76%.
  2. You score 27 out of 30. 90%.
  3. Coursework is 40% and you earned 78%; the exam is 60% and you earned 66%. Final? 31.2 + 39.6 = 70.8%.
  4. A quiz of 12 marks, a test of 40 marks and an exam of 100 marks, with scores 9, 30 and 71. What is the percentage on total points? 110 ÷ 152 = 72.37%.
  5. What score on a 25-mark test gives 84%? 0.84 × 25 = 21 marks.
  6. You want an average of 75% across three tests of equal weight and scored 70 and 72. What is needed on the third? 3 × 75 − 142 = 83%.

How to use percentages to improve

  • Record every assessment as a percentage in a simple table. Patterns show which topics and which assessment types you are weaker at.
  • Compare your percentage with the course average if the teacher shares it. A score that looks low may be above average on a hard paper.
  • Work backwards from your target to what you need on the remaining assessments, and set small goals for each.
  • Use feedback comments with the percentage. A mark tells you what, comments tell you why.

A short guide to grading systems around the world

Grades look different from country to country, which is why converting to a percentage is a useful first step. Many African and Commonwealth systems use percentage bands with letters A to F, or classes such as distinction, merit and pass. The United States uses letters with a 4.0 grade point scale, and many European systems use scales such as 1 to 5, 1 to 6 or 0 to 20, where the best grade may be the lowest or highest number. In these systems a pass mark can range from around 40% to 60% of the maximum. When you apply abroad, institutions often publish equivalence tables, so always use the official conversion rather than guessing.

How teachers and exam boards set grade boundaries

  • Fixed boundaries. A teacher decides in advance that 70% is an A, 60% a B and so on. The same percentage always earns the same grade.
  • Grade descriptors. Exam boards describe what a typical A or C performance looks like and set the boundary marks after seeing how the paper performed.
  • Norm-referenced grading. Grades are assigned according to rank in a cohort, such as the top 10% receiving an A.
  • Criterion-referenced grading. Grades reflect whether set standards are met regardless of how others perform.

This is why the same raw mark can earn different letter grades in different years or subjects. The percentage tells you the proportion of marks earned. The boundary tells you what that proportion means.

Further worked examples

Percentages from decimal marks

A lab report scored 17.5 out of 25 is 70%. A quiz scored 6.5 out of 8 is 81.25%. A presentation scored 43 out of 60 is 71.67%. The calculator shows these rounded to two decimals.

Comparing two classes

Class A has 24 passes out of 30 students, 80%. Class B has 33 passes out of 44 students, 75%. Class A has the higher pass rate even though Class B has more passes.

Setting a target

To finish a module with 65% overall from a current 58% on 60% of the course, the remaining 40% needs (65 − 0.6 × 58) ÷ 0.4 = 75.5% on average.

Extra practice questions

  1. 47 out of 60. 78.33%.
  2. 13 out of 16. 81.25%.
  3. A test is out of 45 and the pass mark is 40%. How many marks to pass? 18.
  4. The class average is 64% and you scored 72%. How many points above? 8 percentage points.
  5. A student scored 56 out of 80 in Paper 1 and 41 out of 70 in Paper 2. Overall on total points? 97 ÷ 150 = 64.67%.

Common mistakes

  • Entering the percentage as the points. If your mark is already 70%, enter 70 and 100, not 70 and 60.
  • Ignoring negative marking. Subtract penalties first, then enter the net points earned.
  • Comparing percentages with different weights. Scoring 90% on a task worth 5% of the grade helps far less than 70% on one worth 50%.
  • Rounding too early. Keep decimals until the final grade, then round once.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert a percentage back into marks?

Divide the percentage by 100 and multiply by the total. For 70% of 60 marks, 0.70 × 60 = 42.

What percentage is a pass?

Pass marks differ by school, country and subject, commonly between 40% and 50%. Check your own grading policy.

Why does the calculator show a dash?

The total points field is empty or zero, so no percentage can be calculated. Enter a total greater than zero.

Is this the same as a letter grade?

No. Letter grades are assigned from percentage ranges set by each institution. Convert the percentage using your own scale.

How do I calculate a percentage grade without a calculator?

Divide the marks by the total to get a decimal and multiply by 100. For quick checks, 10% of a total is the total divided by 10, and you can build other percentages from it.

What is the difference between a percentage and a percentile?

A percentage is a proportion of the total marks. A percentile ranks you against others: the 80th percentile means you scored higher than 80% of the group.

Can I get more than 100%?

Yes, if bonus marks go beyond the maximum. Many teachers cap the final figure at 100%, but the raw percentage can exceed it.

How do I calculate a weighted average of grades?

Multiply each percentage by its weight as a decimal, then add the results. Weights must total 100% (1.0).

Why do two tests with the same percentage count differently?

Because assessments carry different weights in the final grade. A 90% on a 5% quiz moves the final mark much less than 90% on a 50% exam.

What if my teacher marks out of a total that changes?

Always use the total for the assessment you are converting. If the total was changed after marking, ask which total applies.

How many decimal places should I keep?

Keep at least one extra decimal while calculating and round only the final answer. Many courses report whole percentages.

How do I turn the percentage into a letter grade?

Look at the grade boundaries in your syllabus or handbook. There is no universal conversion.

What is a good percentage?

It depends on the course and the scale. Many institutions treat 70% and above as a strong result, but check your own grade descriptors.

How do I calculate the total if each part has a different weight?

Convert every part to a percentage, multiply each by its weight, and add the results.

What if a test is cancelled?

Ask your teacher how the weight will be redistributed. The usual approach is to scale the remaining weights proportionally.

Can I convert a percentage to a letter grade here?

No. Letter boundaries are set by each institution, so use your own table.

Why does the tool allow numbers above 100?

Bonus marks and extra credit can legitimately push a percentage above 100.

How do I calculate a percentage from marks with decimals?

Divide the decimal mark by the total and multiply by 100. The calculator accepts decimals directly.

Is 50% always a pass?

No. Pass marks vary by country, institution and subject, with 40% and 50% both common.

What does a class average tell me?

It shows how your percentage compares with others. A score above the average on a hard paper can be better than a score below the average on an easy one.

How do I compare grades from different systems?

Use official conversion tables from the institution you are applying to.

Should I round my percentage up?

Only if your institution says so. Report the figure as it appears on your results.

Planning revision time across several subjects

Most students do not run out of effort before an exam; they run out of time, and the time that is left gets spent on whichever subject feels most comfortable. Dividing the available hours across your subjects before you start is the simplest way to stop that happening. This calculator takes the total number of minutes you have and the number of subjects you need to cover, and returns an equal share for each one.

It is deliberately a starting point rather than a finished timetable. An equal split tells you what “fair” looks like, so you can then decide, on purpose, where to take time from and where to add it.

How the calculation works

Time per subject = total available minutes ÷ number of subjects

The result is rounded to the nearest whole minute, which is why 110 minutes shared between 3 subjects shows 37 rather than 36.67. If you are planning several sessions, work in minutes for the total and convert at the end: 90 minutes is 1 hour 30 minutes, and 150 minutes is 2 hours 30 minutes.

Enter the time you will genuinely have, not the time on the clock. A three-hour evening with a meal, a phone call and two breaks is closer to 130 minutes of real study, and a plan built on 180 will run over.

Worked examples

An even split

You have 180 minutes after school and need to revise 4 subjects.

180 ÷ 4 = 45

Each subject gets 45 minutes. The calculator shows exactly this.

A result that does not divide evenly

You have 100 minutes and 3 subjects.

100 ÷ 3 = 33.33…

The calculator shows 33 minutes per subject. The rounding means three sessions of 33 minutes add up to 99, so you can give the spare minute to your hardest subject.

Giving harder subjects a larger share by hand

The calculator splits time equally, but you can weight the split yourself. Suppose you have 240 minutes and want Chemistry to get three parts, Maths two parts and English one part. Choose “Sharing an amount in a ratio” in the Word Problem Solver, enter 240 and the ratio 3:2:1.

The shares are 120, 80 and 40 minutes. The same method works for any set of priorities.

Making the number useful

  • Add breaks to the plan, not on top of it. A common pattern is 25 to 50 minutes of work followed by a 5 to 10 minute break. A 45-minute share fits one focused block with a short break after it.
  • Weight by exam date and confidence. A subject you will sit next week and find difficult deserves more than one you sit in a month and already understand.
  • Split large shares into two sessions. Two sessions of 50 minutes on different days usually work better than one of 100, because you revisit the material rather than meeting it once.
  • Keep a review slot. Reserve the last 10 to 15 minutes of a session to write down what you can remember without looking.

Why time allocation matters more than total hours

Research on learning keeps returning to the same practical point: how you spread study across subjects and across days matters at least as much as how many hours you put in. Two students can each study twenty hours in a week and finish with very different results if one spends fifteen of those hours on the subject they already enjoy and the other covers every course in proportion to its difficulty and its exam date. Allocating time is a planning skill, and like any skill it improves when you make the reasoning explicit.

The simplest allocation is an equal split, and that is what this calculator produces. An equal split is not meant to be the final answer. It is a neutral baseline that you can compare your instincts against. If your gut feeling says that Chemistry deserves twice as long as Geography, the equal share tells you how far you are about to deviate from fairness, and it forces you to justify the deviation.

Three ideas that make the number useful

  • Spaced practice. Material revisited several times over days or weeks is remembered better than the same total time spent in a single sitting. Splitting a subject's share into two or three shorter sessions on different days usually beats one long session.
  • Retrieval practice. Time spent testing yourself (past questions, flash cards, explaining a topic out loud) tends to produce more durable memory than the same time spent re-reading notes. When you look at a 45-minute share, plan how much of it is retrieval.
  • Interleaving. Mixing different kinds of problem within a session can feel harder but supports better discrimination between methods. A 90-minute block covering two subjects can be more productive than three separate 45-minute blocks on one subject at the end of a long day.

None of these ideas needs special equipment, and none contradicts the calculator. They simply shape how you spend each share once you know its size.

Step by step: turning the result into a weekly plan

  1. Decide the real total. Start with the hours you are free, subtract meals, travel, chores and the buffer that unplanned events always consume. Many students find that 60% to 70% of nominal free time is what they actually use.
  2. List the subjects. Count each subject that needs revision once. A subject with two papers is still one subject for the equal split; weight it later if one paper is harder.
  3. Enter minutes and subjects. The calculator returns the equal share per subject, rounded to the nearest minute.
  4. Weight by exam date. Multiply or divide shares by a priority factor. A subject examined next week might get 1.5 times the baseline, one that is a month away 0.75 times.
  5. Weight by confidence. Be honest. Subjects you rate 2 out of 5 for confidence deserve more time than those you rate 4 or 5.
  6. Check the total. After adjusting, the shares must still add up to your real total. If they exceed it, something has to give.
  7. Place sessions in the calendar. Put the largest shares at the time of day you concentrate best, and the lightest at the end of the day.
  8. Review weekly. Compare plan and reality, adjust the total, and recalculate.

More worked examples

A weekend of revision for five subjects

You have 240 minutes on Saturday morning and five subjects. The equal split is 240 ÷ 5 = 48 minutes per subject. Forty-eight minutes is awkward in practice, so you might run four subjects at 50 minutes and one at 40, which still totals 240.

An evening with two priorities

Ninety minutes, two subjects: 90 ÷ 2 = 45 minutes each. If one of them is an exam tomorrow, the equal split is clearly wrong. Use a ratio instead: with a 2:1 split, 90 minutes becomes 60 and 30. The 2:1 sharing is done with the Sharing in a ratio mode of the Word Problem Solver.

A longer plan across a week

You expect 420 real minutes of study across the week and have six subjects: 420 ÷ 6 = 70 minutes each. Seventy minutes is about one 45-minute focused session plus a 25-minute review session, so each subject appears twice in the week.

When the division is not exact

125 minutes between 4 subjects is 31.25, which the calculator rounds to 31 minutes. Four sessions of 31 minutes total 124, leaving one minute spare. For 50 minutes between 3 subjects the result is 17 (from 16.67), and three sessions of 17 minutes total 51, one minute over. In both cases the discrepancy is a rounding effect and does not matter in practice.

Planning with breaks included

You have a block from 14:00 to 17:00, which is 180 minutes. You want a 10-minute break after every 50 minutes of work, so three sessions need two breaks: 180 − 20 = 160 minutes of work. With 4 subjects that is 40 minutes each, a different answer from 45 and a more honest one.

Practice questions with answers

  1. You have 300 minutes and 4 subjects. How long per subject? 300 ÷ 4 = 75 minutes each.
  2. You have 2 hours 30 minutes and 5 subjects. Convert first: 2 hours 30 minutes is 150 minutes. Then 150 ÷ 5 = 30 minutes per subject.
  3. You have 200 minutes and 3 subjects but want Maths to get twice as long as each of the others. That is a 2:1:1 split of four parts. One part is 200 ÷ 4 = 50 minutes, so Maths gets 100 and the other two get 50 each.
  4. Your plan gives 42 minutes per subject for 5 subjects. How many minutes did you start with? Multiply back: 42 × 5 = 210 minutes (allowing for rounding of a minute or so).
  5. You study 6 days a week for 90 minutes a day across 3 subjects. How many minutes does each subject receive in a week? Total 6 × 90 = 540 minutes. Equal split: 540 ÷ 3 = 180 minutes each, which is 3 hours a week per subject.
  6. An exam is in 14 days. You can study 75 minutes a day, every day, on 5 subjects. Total 14 × 75 = 1,050 minutes, equal share 1,050 ÷ 5 = 210 minutes per subject over two weeks.

Practical applications beyond school exams

  • University coursework. A student with four modules and 16 hours of independent study a week can use the calculator to see a starting share of 4 hours per module, then weight by assessment deadlines.
  • Professional exams. Candidates for accountancy, medical, engineering or language exams often juggle five to eight topics; the equal share highlights how little time each topic really gets.
  • Skill practice. Musicians dividing a 60-minute practice session between scales, repertoire and sight-reading use the same arithmetic.
  • Team meetings. A 45-minute meeting with five agenda items is 9 minutes per item, which makes it obvious when the agenda is unrealistic.
  • Household projects. Dividing a Saturday between jobs works exactly like dividing revision time.

Limits of an equal split

An equal split ignores everything that makes subjects different: how much content there is, how difficult it is for you, how it is assessed and how soon. It also ignores energy: forty minutes at the end of a tiring day is worth less than forty minutes in the morning. Use the calculator for the arithmetic and your own judgement for the weighting. If your results are poor in a subject, consider whether the cause is time at all. Sometimes the method (re-reading instead of practising problems) matters more than the amount.

The tool also does not read your calendar, track what you have studied or remember your plan. It divides one number by another and rounds the answer to a whole minute, which is exactly why it is quick and predictable.

A full case study: planning the two weeks before exams

Amara has 14 days until her first exam and five subjects: Mathematics, Physics, Chemistry, English and Economics. She is free for about 3 hours on weekdays and 5 hours on weekend days, but knows from experience that she only really studies about 70% of any free block. Here is how she turns that into a plan.

  1. Count the real time. Ten weekdays at 3 hours is 30 hours, and four weekend days at 5 hours is 20 hours, so 50 hours of free time. At 70% that is 35 hours, which is 2,100 minutes.
  2. Get the baseline. 2,100 ÷ 5 = 420 minutes per subject over the fortnight, or 7 hours each.
  3. Weight the subjects. The exams are in the order Mathematics, Physics, Economics, Chemistry and English, and she is least confident in Physics and Chemistry. She gives Physics and Chemistry a factor of 1.3, Mathematics 1.1, Economics 0.9 and English 0.7.
  4. Convert factors to minutes. 420 × 1.3 = 546 for each of Physics and Chemistry, 420 × 1.1 = 462 for Mathematics, 420 × 0.9 = 378 for Economics and 420 × 0.7 = 294 for English. These total 546 + 546 + 462 + 378 + 294 = 2,226, which exceeds the 2,100 available.
  5. Scale down. She multiplies every figure by 2,100 ÷ 2,226 = 0.943, giving roughly 515, 515, 436, 357 and 277 minutes, which she rounds to 515, 515, 435, 355 and 280.
  6. Schedule. She splits each subject into sessions of 35 to 50 minutes spread across the fortnight, and places the two hardest subjects in her best concentration window (after breakfast).

The calculator supplied the first and most important number, the baseline of 420, in a second. Everything after that was judgement, and the baseline kept the judgement honest: Amara could see that English was getting 30% less than the equal share and Physics 30% more, and she decided she was comfortable with both.

Common misconceptions about study time

  • “More hours always means better results.” Beyond a point, tired study is slow and error-prone. Shorter, focused sessions with breaks usually give better returns than marathon sessions.
  • “I should study my best subject most because it earns the most marks.” Marks are usually easiest to gain where you are weakest, because a small improvement there is bigger than the same effort on a subject you already know.
  • “A timetable must be followed exactly.” A plan is a guide. If you miss a session, move it rather than abandoning the plan, and recalculate with the minutes you have left.
  • “Equal time means equal effort.” Forty-five minutes of active problem-solving is not the same as 45 minutes of reading. Decide what the share will be used for.
  • “I can catch up on everything the weekend before.” Memory consolidates better when you revisit material several times, so leaving everything to the end wastes the effect of repetition.

Checking that your plan is realistic

  • Add up all the planned minutes. They must not exceed the real time you entered.
  • Look at the smallest share. If it is under about 25 minutes, the subject will not get a proper session, so merge it with another or schedule it less often.
  • Look at the longest single session. If it is more than about 90 minutes without a break, split it.
  • Check that every subject has at least two sessions before the exam, spaced a few days apart.
  • After the first week, compare the minutes you actually studied with the plan, and run the calculator again with the corrected total.

Key terms

  • Allocation: the minutes assigned to each subject.
  • Baseline: the equal share, used as a reference.
  • Weighting: multiplying a share by a factor to reflect priority.
  • Spaced practice: spreading study of a topic over several sessions.
  • Retrieval practice: testing yourself instead of re-reading.
  • Interleaving: mixing topics within a session.

More practice questions

  2. You study 45 minutes a day for 20 days across 6 subjects. How many minutes per subject in total? 900 ÷ 6 = 150 minutes each.
  3. A plan gives Physics 1.5 times the equal share of 60 minutes. How many minutes? 90 minutes.
  4. If the equal share is 35 minutes and you weight a subject by 0.6, what is its share? 35 × 0.6 = 21 minutes.
  5. Five subjects have shares of 60, 60, 45, 45 and 30 minutes. What was the total? 240 minutes.

Common mistakes

  • Entering hours in the minutes field. 3 hours is 180 minutes, so type 180.
  • Counting the number of topics instead of the number of subjects, then wondering why each share is tiny.
  • Treating the result as fixed. If one subject needs more time, change the split; the total does not change.
  • Planning for every day without allowing a rest day. Include rest in the total you enter or the plan will not survive the week.

Frequently asked questions

How many hours should I study per subject?

There is no single correct figure. It depends on how many subjects you have, how close the exam is and how confident you feel. Use the equal share as a baseline, then move time towards subjects that are weaker or sooner.

Why is my result a whole number?

The calculator rounds to the nearest whole minute, because planning to the second is not realistic. If the shares do not add up exactly to your total, adjust one session by a minute or two.

Does this build a full timetable?

No. It gives the time available to each subject. Placing those sessions across days and times is up to you, which keeps the plan flexible.

Can I use it for work or other tasks?

Yes. Anything where one block of time must be shared equally, such as practising several instruments or dividing a meeting agenda, uses the same division.

Is it better to study one subject for longer or many subjects for shorter sessions?

For most learners, shorter, regular sessions spread across several days work better than a single long session, because repeated exposure supports memory. The best length depends on concentration: many people work well in blocks of 25 to 50 minutes.

How do I decide how much to weight a hard subject?

A practical rule is to give difficult or soon-to-be-examined subjects between 1.25 and 2 times the equal share, and easy or distant ones between 0.5 and 0.75 times. Then check that the total still fits.

Should I include breaks in the minutes I enter?

Enter only the time you plan to spend working. If you will take a 10-minute break every hour, reduce the total by those breaks first, as in the example with a 14:00 to 17:00 block.

Can I use the calculator for more than one day at a time?

Yes. Add up the minutes for the days you plan to cover and divide by the number of subjects, as in the weekly examples above. Then split each subject's share across the days.

What if one subject has two papers?

Count it as one subject for the baseline, then give it a larger weight afterwards. Alternatively, count the two papers as separate subjects if they are examined separately and are equally demanding.

Does the calculator tell me what to study?

No. It tells you how long to spend. Choosing topics is easier with a past-paper analysis or a syllabus checklist.

How many subjects is too many to revise at once?

There is no fixed limit, but when the equal share drops below about 20 to 30 minutes, sessions become too short for deep work. Consider rotating subjects across days instead.

Why does the calculator show a whole number of minutes?

Planning to the second is not realistic, so the tool rounds to the nearest minute. Small rounding differences can be absorbed by adjusting one session.

How early should I start planning revision?

As soon as you know the exam dates. Starting four to six weeks ahead allows each subject to appear several times, which is far more effective than cramming.

Should I study the hardest subject first in a session?

Many people find that starting with a demanding task while energy is high works well, but the best order depends on you. Try it for a week and see.

What if I fall behind my plan?

Recalculate with the time you have left instead of trying to cram everything into the original shares.

Do breaks count as study time?

No. Enter working time only, as in the case study above, and treat breaks as part of the session length.

Is it better to study in the morning or evening?

There is no universal answer. Use the time of day when you concentrate best for your hardest work.
